Session 5 • Fundamentals of Mass Transport

1. Concept of Diffusion

Microscopic definition of atomic transport in crystalline solids, thermodynamic origin in thermal vibrations, kinetic comparison across states of matter, and driving force.

🔬 What is Diffusion in Solids?

In materials science and engineering, diffusion in solids is formally defined as the movement and net transport of atoms or ions within a solid medium. Contrary to the intuitive perception that atoms in rigid engineering components are permanently fixed in space, crystal lattice constituents undergo continuous oscillatory vibrations around their equilibrium lattice coordinates.

  • Thermal Vibrations: The thermal vibrations in solids permit movement of some atoms. At any temperature above absolute zero ($T > 0\text{ K}$), the atoms possess vibrational kinetic energy that occasionally concentrates into a specific bond, allowing an atom to overcome surrounding interatomic potential barriers and jump into an adjacent site.
  • Thermally Activated Phenomena: Diffusion belongs strictly to the class of phenomena that need thermal activation. The probability of an atom acquiring sufficient energy to surmount the local energy barrier increases exponentially with absolute temperature.
  • Transport Phenomena: Diffusion (transport phenomena): mechanisms by which material is transported through the material. It is the primary physical process governing solid-state phase transformations, precipitation hardening, sintering, oxidation, and surface thermochemical treatments.
📖 Verbatim Slide Definition (Carlos III de Madrid • Session 5)

Diffusion in solids: movement of atoms within a solid.
• The thermal vibrations in solids permit movement of some atoms.
• Phenomena that need thermal activation.
• Diffusion (transport phenomena): mechanisms by which material is transported through the material.

⚖ Atomic Movement Speed Across States of Matter

The rate at which atoms or molecules migrate varies drastically depending on the physical state of aggregation, reflecting fundamental differences in atomic packing density and bond rigidity:

$$\underbrace{\text{Solid (Sólido)}}_{\text{Slow / Lento}} \ll \underbrace{\text{Liquid (Líquido)}}_{\text{Intermediate}} < \underbrace{\text{Gas (Gas)}}_{\text{Fast / Rápido}}$$
Hierarchy of atomic movement rate across states of matter (Slide 2).

Underlying Physical Origin:

  • In Gases: Interatomic distances are vast relative to atomic diameters, intermolecular forces are negligible, and mean free paths are long ($\sim 10^{-7}\text{ m}$). Molecules move at hundreds of meters per second ($D \sim 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • In Liquids: Atoms maintain short-range coordination but lack long-range periodic order. Shear resistance is zero, permitting rapid atomic sliding and convective mixing ($D \sim 10^{-9}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • In Solids: Atoms are trapped in deep potential energy wells within rigid, densely packed 3D crystal lattices. For an atom to migrate, it must locally break multiple coordinated bonds and elastically distort adjacent lattice neighbors. Consequently, solid-state diffusion is orders of magnitude slower ($D \sim 10^{-11}\text{ to } 10^{-20}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$).
Figure 1.1: Schematic potential energy landscape of solid-state diffusion. For an atom to execute a jump into an adjacent vacant site, it must acquire thermal energy equal to or exceeding the activation energy barrier $E_a$, squeezing past neighboring matrix atoms.
🔄 Driving Force for Diffusion (DRIVING FORCE)

Individual atomic jumps in an isotropic crystal lattice occur in random directions (random-walk Brownian motion). A net, macroscopic transport of matter only arises when an asymmetry exists in the spatial distribution of chemical species:

$$\text{DRIVING FORCE} = \text{Composition Gradient} = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = \frac{C_{\text{ext}} - C_{\text{int}}}{x_2 - x_1}$$
Spatial concentration gradient acting as the thermodynamic driving force toward equal composition (Slide 2).

Thermodynamic Significance:

  • Equilibrium Tendency: The composition gradient produces a gradient in chemical potential ($\nabla \mu$). Atoms migrate spontaneously from regions of high chemical potential to low chemical potential, driving the system toward maximum configurational entropy and chemical equilibrium (uniform composition).
  • Zero Net Transport in Homogeneous Systems: In a perfectly uniform solid solution at constant temperature, atomic exchanges occur continuously (self-diffusion), but the net flux through any arbitrary internal plane is zero ($J_{\text{net}} = 0$).
🔑 Prerequisites for Atomic Diffusion (What is Necessary for Diffusion?)

According to Slide 3, for an atom to execute an effective jump into a new equilibrium position, two mandatory physical conditions must be satisfied simultaneously:

Prerequisite Physical Mechanism Manifestation in Crystal Lattices
1. Empty Site
EMPTY SITE		 An adjacent site must be unoccupied. Due to Pauli exclusion and electrostatic nuclear repulsion, two atoms cannot occupy the same lattice coordinate. • Vacancies: missing host atoms on lattice sites.
• Interstitials: unoccupied crystallographic interstitial voids.
2. Sufficient Energy
ACTIVATION ENERGY (Ea)		 The migrating atom must possess vibrational kinetic energy sufficient to overcome the bond-stretching and lattice-distortion barrier of the constriction. • Requires $E_{\text{thermal}} \ge E_a$.
• Governed by the Boltzmann probability factor: $\propto \exp(-E_a / RT)$.
💡 Essential Takeaway from Slide 3

Temperature Increase ⇒ Diffusion Acceleration ($\uparrow T \implies \uparrow \text{Diffusion}$):
Elevating the temperature simultaneously multiplies the equilibrium vacancy concentration and increases the fraction of atoms possessing thermal energies exceeding $E_a$, exponentially increasing the atomic jump frequency.

Session 5 • Microscopic Transport

2. Diffusion Mechanisms

Arrhenius thermodynamics of atomic mobility, activation energy components, substitutional vacancy diffusion (self-diffusion and Hume-Rothery interdiffusion), and ultra-fast interstitial diffusion.

🔬 Temperature Dependence of Diffusivity: The Arrhenius Equation

Solid-state reactions and phase transformations fundamentally depend on the rate at which atoms are transferred through the host lattice. The rate of atomic jumping is quantified by the diffusion coefficient or diffusivity ($D$), which follows an Arrhenius-type temperature dependence:

$$D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{E_a}{R \cdot T}\right) = D_0 \, e^{-\frac{E_a}{R \cdot T}}$$
Arrhenius equation for the diffusion coefficient in solids (Diffusivity equation).

Strict definition of physical variables and standard units (verbatim from slides):

  • $D$ (Diffusivity or diffusion coefficient): Quantitative measure of atomic mobility. SI units: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (technical units: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$ or $\text{mm}^2/\text{s}$).
  • $D_0$ (Pre-exponential factor or frequency factor): Constant for a given diffusion system ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$), independent of temperature. It accounts for the atomic jump attempt frequency ($\approx 10^{13}\text{ s}^{-1}$), coordination number, nearest-neighbor jump distance, and vibrational entropy.
  • $E_a$ (or $E_D$ — Activation Energy): Thermal energy barrier required to move one mole of diffusing atoms past neighboring atoms into an adjacent site. Expressed in $\text{J/mol}$ or $\text{cal/mol}$ (or on an atomic scale in $\text{eV/atom}$, with $1\text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}$).
  • $R$ (Molar gas constant):
    $$R = 8.314\text{ J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K}) = 1.987\text{ cal}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$$
  • $T$ (Absolute temperature): Thermodynamic temperature, which must always be evaluated in Kelvin ($\text{K}$): $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
⚠ Fundamental Thermal Principle from Official Slides

"Energy is required to move atoms past one another during diffusion. This energy is the activation energy, $E_a$ ($\text{J/mol}$, $\text{cal/mol}$)."
"Thermal energy should be larger than $E_a$ to produce diffusion:" $$\uparrow T \implies \uparrow \text{Diffusion}$$ "Activation energy depends on both the type of atom that diffuses and the material in which the diffusion occurs."

🔄 Canonical Classification of Diffusion Types

According to the atomic nature of the diffusing solute and the crystallographic pathway traversed through the lattice, solid-state diffusion is formally categorized into two major mechanisms:

Category Subtype Key Characteristics & Transport Mechanism
1. Vacancy Diffusion
(or Substitutional Atom Diffusion)		 1.a Self-diffusion Atomic exchange between chemically identical host atoms in a pure elemental metal. Requires both vacancy formation and vacancy migration.
1.b Interdiffusion
(Impurity Diffusion)		 Net transport of foreign solute atoms through a host solvent lattice, forming substitutional solid solutions. Regulated by Hume-Rothery solubility rules.
2. Interstitial Diffusion Migration of small solute atoms (C, H, O, N) between interstitial lattice interstices without displacing host matrix atoms. Very low activation barrier.
🔴 1. Vacancy (or Substitutional) Diffusion

Vacancy diffusion involves the interchange of an atom from a normal lattice site to an adjacent vacant lattice site or vacancy. Crucially, the motion of atoms in one direction corresponds physically to the motion of vacancies in the opposite direction:

  • Thermal defect concentration: Vacancies are intrinsic thermodynamic equilibrium defects. As temperature rises, thermal agitation exponentially multiplies the equilibrium vacancy concentration: $$\uparrow T \implies \uparrow [\text{defects / vacancies}]$$ $$\uparrow \text{Thermal energy} > E_a \implies \uparrow \text{Diffusion rate}$$
  • Atomic jump event: If a host or substitutional solute atom acquires thermal vibrational energy exceeding the local activation energy $E_a$, it overcomes the steric barrier and hops into the adjacent vacancy.

⚒ 1.a Self-Diffusion: Energetic Components and Melting Point Correlation

In pure elemental crystals, self-diffusion occurs via lattice vacancies. The total activation energy per mole ($E_a$) is the direct sum of two independent physical energy contributions:

$$\underbrace{E_a(\text{Self-diffusion})}_{\text{Total Activation Energy}} = \underbrace{E_{\text{formation}}}_{\text{Energy required to form a vacancy}} + \underbrace{E_{\text{movement}}}_{\text{Energy required to move the vacancy}}$$
Thermodynamic decomposition of activation energy in vacancy self-diffusion.

Fundamental correlation with Melting Temperature ($T_m$):

Both creating a vacancy and squeezing an atom through the saddle-point constriction of surrounding neighbors require stretching and severing interatomic metallic bonds. Since a higher melting point ($T_{\text{melting}}$) directly reflects stronger interatomic bonding energy ($E_{\text{bond}}$), the slides formulate the following universal rule:

$$\uparrow T_{\text{melting}} \implies \uparrow E_{\text{bond}} \implies \uparrow E_a$$
Governing trend: The higher the melting temperature of the metal, the greater the activation energy for self-diffusion.

🔄 1.b Interdiffusion (Impurity Diffusion) and Hume-Rothery Rules

Interdiffusion refers to the transport process whereby atoms of one metal diffuse into another distinct host metal matrix. This is the atomic mechanism responsible for forming substitutional solid solutions and intermetallic phases.

  • Solubility criteria (Hume-Rothery Rules): Extensive substitutional interdiffusion is governed by Hume-Rothery criteria (atomic size difference $\Delta r < 15\%$, identical crystal structure, matching valency, and small electronegativity difference).
  • Engineering application:
    • Drives homogenization and heat treatment of multi-component aerospace superalloys.
    • Forms the physical foundation of diffusion welding / diffusion bonding for critical turbine components.
Figure 2.1: Copper-Nickel Diffusion Couple (from slide illustrations). At high temperature, Cu and Ni atoms interdiffuse in opposite directions across the junction plane, converting the initially sharp step concentration into a smooth, continuous S-shaped concentration gradient.
⚡ 2. Interstitial Diffusion

Interstitial diffusion involves solute atoms migrating from an interstitial position into an adjacent unoccupied interstitial site, without permanently displacing any host lattice atoms.

  • Atomic size requirement: The diffusing solutes must have atomic radii significantly smaller than host matrix atoms. The canonical small interstitial elements in engineering metallurgy are:
    $$\mathbf{Carbon\ (C)},\quad \mathbf{Hydrogen\ (H)},\quad \mathbf{Oxygen\ (O)},\quad \mathbf{Nitrogen\ (N)}$$
Comparative Characteristic Interstitial Diffusion Vacancy Diffusion
Activation Energy ($E_a$) Much lower ($E_{\text{interstitial}} \ll E_{\text{vacancy}}$): No energy is spent creating lattice defects; the solute only overcomes the migration barrier between interstices ($E_a = E_{\text{movement}}$). High ($E_{\text{vacancy}} = E_{\text{formation}} + E_{\text{movement}}$): Requires creating a vacancy and distorting host bonds to jump.
Diffusion Rate / Diffusivity ($D$) Extremely rapid: Often $10^6$ to $10^{10}$ times faster than vacancy diffusion at the same temperature. Relatively slow: Strongly throttled by the small equilibrium concentration of lattice vacancies.
Availability of Destination Sites Abundant: In dilute solid solutions, virtually all adjacent interstitial cavities are empty and ready to receive a hopping solute. Severely limited: Vacancy fraction even near the melting point is only $\sim 10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$.

Session 5 • Conservation of Mass Laws

3. Steady State Diffusion & Fick’s 1st Law

Physical definition of diffusion flux J, steady-state condition, formulation of Fick’s first law, and fully worked slide example on hydrogen purification through a palladium membrane.

📊 Quantifying the Diffusion Rate: Matter Flux & Diffusion Flux

To quantify the rate and amount of atomic mass transferred through a cross-section of solid material, we define the diffusion flux ($J$) (or flux density):

$$J = \text{Flux} = \frac{\text{Moles or Mass diffusing}}{\text{Area} \times \text{Time}} = \frac{M}{A \cdot t} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{1}{A} \frac{dm}{dt}$$
Canonical definition of diffusion flux per unit transversal area and per unit time.

Standard Units in Engineering and Physical Science:

  • Molar flux: $\text{mol}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ or $\text{mol}/(\text{cm}^2\cdot\text{s})$.
  • Mass flux: $\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ or $\text{g}/(\text{cm}^2\cdot\text{s})$.
  • Atomic flux: $\text{atoms}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ (especially practical when evaluating unit-cell volumes and planar densities).
⚖ Fundamental Distinction of Regimes from Official Slides

• Steady-state conditions: The diffusion flux $J$ does not change with time ($\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} = 0$). Governed by Fick’s 1st Law.
• Non steady-state conditions: Both diffusion flux $J$ and local concentration change with time ($\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} \neq 0$, $\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} \neq 0$). Governed by Fick’s 2nd Law.

⚙ Fick’s First Law of Diffusion

Under steady-state conditions, the concentration of diffusing species at any spatial position within the solid remains constant with time ($\partial C / \partial t = 0$). Consequently, equal amounts of solute mass enter and exit any differential slice of the material every second.

Postulated by Adolf Fick in 1855, Fick’s 1st Law states that diffusion flux is directly proportional to the concentration gradient:

$$J = -D \cdot \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = -D \cdot \frac{dC}{dx}$$
Fundamental equation of Fick's 1st Law for one-dimensional steady-state diffusion.

For a flat membrane or plate of finite thickness $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$, with fixed solute concentrations maintained at the outer boundary ($C_{\text{ext}}$) and inner boundary ($C_{\text{int}}$):

$$J = -D \cdot \frac{C_{\text{int}} - C_{\text{ext}}}{x_2 - x_1} = D \cdot \frac{C_{\text{ext}} - C_{\text{int}}}{\Delta x}$$
Operational formula for flat plates with linear concentration profiles.

Physical Breakdown of Terms and Symbols:

  • $J$: Flux of atoms or mass per unit area and time in the direction of diffusion $x$ ($\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ or $\text{at}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$).
  • $D$: Diffusivity or diffusion coefficient at the operational temperature ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • $\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = \frac{C_2 - C_1}{x_2 - x_1}$: Concentration gradient along coordinate $x$ ($\text{kg/m}^4$ or $\text{at/m}^4$). Represents the chemical DRIVING FORCE.
  • The Negative Sign: Dictated by the Second Law of Thermodynamics: net mass transport spontaneously flows "downhill" from high-concentration to low-concentration regions ($\Delta C < 0 \implies J > 0$).
💡 Necessary Condition for Constant Flux J

At constant temperature ($T = \text{const}$), the flux $J$ remains strictly constant if and only if the boundary compositions on both surfaces of the plate are held constant over time, ensuring a stationary concentration gradient.

Figure 3.1: Linear concentration profile characteristic of steady-state Fick’s 1st Law ($\partial J / \partial t = 0$). As long as boundary concentrations $C_{\text{ext}}$ and $C_{\text{int}}$ remain fixed, both the concentration gradient and flux $J$ remain perfectly constant.
🔮 Fully Worked Slide Example: Hydrogen Purification through a Palladium (Pd) Plate
📝 Verbatim Problem Statement from Slides

"Determine the thickness of a plate of Pd with transversal area of $0.2\text{ m}^2$ so that it can purify $1.733 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg/h}$ of hydrogen, if the hydrogen concentration at the side of high pressure of the plate is $1.5\text{ kg/m}^3$ and at the side of low pressure is $0.3\text{ kg/m}^3$. The diffusion coefficient of hydrogen in Pd is $1 \times 10^{-8}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$."

Step 1: Data Extraction and SI Unit Conversions

  • Transversal plate area: $A = 0.2\text{ m}^2$.
  • Purification mass flow rate: $\dot{m} = 1.733 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg/h}$.
  • Time conversion: $1\text{ h} = 3600\text{ s} \implies \dot{m} = \frac{1.733 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}}{3600\text{ s}} = 4.814 \times 10^{-7}\text{ kg/s}$.
  • Concentration at high-pressure side ($P_{\text{high}}$): $C_{\text{ext}} = C_1 = 1.5\text{ kg/m}^3$.
  • Concentration at low-pressure side ($P_{\text{low}}$): $C_{\text{int}} = C_2 = 0.3\text{ kg/m}^3$.
  • Diffusivity of $H$ in $Pd$: $D = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$.

Step 2: Calculation of Surface Diffusion Flux $J$

Total mass purified per unit area and time:

$$J = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Area} \cdot \text{Time}} = \frac{1.733 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}}{0.2\text{ m}^2 \times 3600\text{ s}} = 2.407 \times 10^{-6}\text{ kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$$

Step 3: Applying Fick’s 1st Law and Solving for Thickness $\Delta x$

Since $J = -D \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = D \frac{C_1 - C_2}{\Delta x}$, solving for required plate thickness $\Delta x$ yields:

$$\Delta x = D \cdot \frac{C_{\text{ext}} - C_{\text{int}}}{J} = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ m}^2/\text{s} \cdot \frac{(1.5 - 0.3)\text{ kg/m}^3}{2.407 \times 10^{-6}\text{ kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})}$$ $$\Delta x = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-8}}{2.407 \times 10^{-6}} = 4.985 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m} \approx \mathbf{5\text{ mm}}$$
🚨 Classic Exam Pitfall

Hour-to-second unit omission: Forgetting to divide $\text{kg/h}$ by $3600\text{ s}$ is the most common error in this problem. Diffusivity $D$ is given in $\text{m}^2/\mathbf{s}$, so omitting the $3600$ factor distorts the result by $3600\times$, erroneously yielding $18\text{ meters}$ instead of $5\text{ millimeters}$.

4. Non-Steady State Diffusion & Fick’s 2nd Law

Transient diffusion kinetics, partial differential governing equations, concentration-independent diffusivity hypothesis, semi-infinite solid boundary conditions, and Gaussian error function analytical solutions.

⏱ Why Most Practical Engineering Processes are Non-Steady State

In industrial materials processing and engineering heat treatments, diffusion takes place under non-steady state (transient) conditions for the overwhelming majority of practical applications:

  • Time-dependent concentration: The concentration of solute atoms at any given spatial coordinate inside the solid changes continuously over elapsed time ($\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} \neq 0$).
  • Spatially variable diffusion flux: Because the local concentration evolves dynamically, the concentration gradient $\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$ and the diffusion flux $J(x, t)$ differ from one plane to the next across the component ($\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} \neq 0$).
  • Thermodynamic outcome (verbatim from lecture slides): "Non stationary state ⇒ The substances come to an end through extinction" (diffusing solute atoms from the surface progressively penetrate the host matrix until chemical saturation or equilibrium is attained).

📜 General Partial Differential Equation of Fick’s 2nd Law

Applying the principle of conservation of mass over a one-dimensional differential volume element of thickness $dx$:

$$\frac{\partial C_x}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left( D \frac{\partial C_x}{\partial x} \right)$$
General differential formulation of Fick’s Second Law in one dimension.

🔬 Fundamental Hypothesis: Diffusivity Independent of Composition

The slides formulate the foundational hypothesis universally applied in engineering diffusion calculations:

📋 HYPOTHESIS (Verbatim from Lecture Slides)

"If $D \neq f(C)$ (i.e. diffusion coefficient is independent of composition; there is no interaction between diffusing atoms):"

$$\frac{\partial C_x}{\partial t} = D \cdot \frac{\partial^2 C_x}{\partial x^2}$$

Physical meaning: Rate of composition change equals diffusivity multiplied by the rate of change of the composition gradient. In dilute solid solutions, solute atoms are sufficiently separated that mutual interactions are negligible, rendering $D$ solely a function of temperature.

📐 Analytical Solution: Semi-Infinite Solid with Constant Surface Concentration

To solve this second-order partial differential equation, boundary conditions matching the experimental setup must be imposed. The classic metallurgical case models a semi-infinite solid exposed to a constant active gas atmosphere:

Temporal / Spatial Coordinate Mathematical Boundary Condition Physical Engineering Meaning
Initial instant ($t = 0$) $$C = C_0 \quad \text{for all } 0 \le x \le \infty$$ Before initiating the thermal cycle, the alloy has a uniform, homogeneous solute concentration $C_0$ throughout its bulk.
Surface boundary ($x = 0, t > 0$) $$C = C_s \quad \text{at } x = 0$$ The furnace atmosphere immediately establishes and permanently maintains a constant surface solute concentration $C_s$ (e.g. carburizing gas mixture).
Semi-infinite depth ($x \to \infty, t > 0$) $$C \to C_0 \quad \text{as } x \to \infty$$ The workpiece thickness is sufficiently large that the diffusion front does not penetrate to the opposite face during the processing window.

🧮 Analytical Solution via the Gaussian Error Function ($\text{erf}$)

By applying the Boltzmann similarity variable transformation ($\eta = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$), the exact mathematical solution to Fick’s second law is obtained:

$$\frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = 1 - \text{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{D \cdot t}} \right)$$
Canonical Gaussian Error Function solution for a semi-infinite solid.

Or expressed in the complementary ratio frequently adopted in case-hardening problems:

$$\frac{C_s - C_x}{C_s - C_0} = \text{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{D \cdot t}} \right)$$

Exhaustive Nomenclature of Physical Quantities:

  • $C_x$: Solute concentration at depth $x$ below the surface after diffusion time $t$ ($\text{wt}\%$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$).
  • $C_s$: Constant solute concentration imposed at the exterior surface ($x = 0$).
  • $C_0$: Initial homogeneous solute concentration inside the base alloy ($t = 0$).
  • $x$: Depth or perpendicular distance measured inwards from the surface ($\text{m}$ or $\text{mm}$).
  • $t$: Elapsed duration of thermal treatment ($\text{s}$).
  • $D$: Diffusivity of the solute species at the operating temperature ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • $\text{erf}(z)$: Gaussian error function evaluated at the dimensionless argument $z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$.
📚 Benchmark Industrial Examples Cited in Lecture Slides

Diffusion of a gas (with constant surface concentration) into the interior of a semi-infinite solid:
• Carbon diffusion in steel: Gas carburizing / cementation of gears and drive shafts.
• Saturation of a metal with atmospheric gases: Surface pickup of oxygen and nitrogen in reactive metals (e.g. titanium, zirconium).

Figure 4.1: Transient concentration profiles predicted by Fick’s 2nd Law for increasing heat treatment durations ($t_1 < t_2 < t_3$). While the surface concentration remains anchored at $C_s$, solute penetration deepens into the semi-infinite medium over time.
🛠 Parabolic Scaling Law: The $\frac{x^2}{Dt} = \text{constant}$ Principle

When an engineering design specifies a target concentration $C_x$ (such as achieving $0.3\text{ wt}\%\text{ C}$ for tooth hardening in a transmission gear):

$$\text{If } \frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = \text{const} \implies \text{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}} \right) = \text{const} \implies \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}} = \text{constant}$$ $$\frac{x^2}{D \cdot t} = \text{constant} \implies x \propto \sqrt{D \cdot t}$$
Parabolic penetration law in non-steady state diffusion.
🚨 Critical Examination Rule

Because penetration depth $x$ scales with the square root of time ($\sqrt{t}$):
• To double the case-hardened depth ($2x$) at constant temperature, furnace processing time must be increased by fourfold ($2^2 = 4t$).
• To triple penetration depth ($3x$), furnace processing time must increase by ninefold ($3^2 = 9t$).

5. Factors Affecting Diffusion

Fundamental physical drivers of atomic mobility: mechanism and atomic radius, host lattice crystal structure (BCC vs FCC), crystal defects and short-circuit diffusion, concentration effects, temperature, Arrhenius linearization, and the master diffusivity dataset.

🔍 Nature of the Solute-Solvent System & Diffusion Rate Definition

The diffusion coefficient or diffusivity ($D$) is a characteristic transport parameter dictated by the identity of both atomic species involved and the thermodynamic state of the host matrix:

  • Dual dependency: "Diffusivity depends on both the atoms that diffuse (solute) and the matrix (solvent)."
  • Equilibrium constancy: "It is constant for a particular system (solute + solvent) and at a specific temperature."
  • Definition of Diffusion Rate (verbatim from slides): "The 'speed' of diffusion of observed species through the material. It depends both on diffusing species and characteristics of the material in which diffusion occurs."

Below is an in-depth analysis of the five canonical factors detailed in the lecture syllabus:

🔴 a) Diffusion Mechanism & Atomic Size (Atom Size)

Small solute atoms migrating along interstitial pathways experience vastly lower steric hindrance and activation barriers than bulky solute atoms relying on vacancy exchanges.

📋 Quantitative Evidence from Slides (at 1000 ºC in Fe-γ FCC)

• Interstitial Carbon in $\text{Fe}-\gamma$: $D_{1000^\circ\text{C}} = 3 \times 10^{-11}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
• Substitutional Iron (vacancy self-diffusion) in $\text{Fe}-\gamma$: $D_{1000^\circ\text{C}} = 2 \times 10^{-16}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
Physical Conclusion: Interstitial carbon diffuses over 150,000 times faster than host iron atoms through the exact same austenitic lattice at identical temperature.

🔵 b) Crystal Structure of the Host Matrix (Solvent Lattice Structure)

The atomic packing efficiency of the solvent crystal strongly dictates the resistance encountered by hopping atoms. A more open crystalline structure (lower atomic packing factor) provides wider interstitial bottlenecks:

📋 Quantitative Evidence from Slides (Carbon in Iron at 500 ºC)

• In Ferrite $\text{Fe}-\alpha$ (BCC): $D(\text{C in Fe-}\alpha, \text{BCC}) = 10^{-12}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
• In Austenite $\text{Fe}-\gamma$ (FCC): $D(\text{C in Fe-}\gamma, \text{FCC}) = 5 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
Crystallographic rationale: $$\text{Atomic Packing Factor (BCC: 0.68)} < \text{Atomic Packing Factor (FCC: 0.74)}$$ $$d_{\text{interatomic}}(\text{BCC}) > d_{\text{interatomic}}(\text{FCC})$$ Although individual octahedral voids in FCC are volumetrically larger, the BCC lattice is overall significantly more open (packing efficiency of $68\%$ vs $74\%$). Consequently, carbon diffuses 200 times faster in BCC ferrite than in FCC austenite at $500^\circ\text{C}$.

🔀 c) Crystal Imperfections & Short-Circuit Diffusion Paths

Two-dimensional boundaries and external surfaces exhibit disrupted metallic bonds, high atomic disorder, and substantially greater free volume than the pristine crystal interior. They serve as rapid transport superhighways (short-circuit diffusion paths):

$$D_{\text{surface}} > D_{\text{grain boundary}} > D_{\text{volume (lattice)}}$$
Diffusivity hierarchy governed by defect density and free volume (Open structures ⇒ ↑ D).
📊 Official Experimental Data for Silver (Ag) at 500 ºC

• Self-diffusion through single crystal lattice (Volume): $D = 10^{-17}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
• Self-diffusion along grain boundaries (Grain Boundary): $D = 10^{-11}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
Grain boundary diffusion in polycrystalline silver is one million times ($10^6\times$) faster than volume lattice diffusion at $500^\circ\text{C}$!

⚖ d) Concentration of Diffusing Species

"When we ↑ the concentration of solute atoms we modify $D$." At higher concentrations, solute atoms experience mutual elastic and chemical interactions, modifying the activity coefficient of the solid solution and causing diffusivity to vary with local composition ($D = D(C)$).

🔥 e) Temperature & Arrhenius Linearization

Temperature exerts the most pronounced exponential effect on solid diffusion. Increasing temperature elevates the thermal vibrational kinetic energy of lattice atoms, exponentially boosting the fraction of atoms possessing thermal energy in excess of $E_D$:

$$\uparrow T \implies \uparrow \text{Vibrational amplitude} \implies \uparrow \text{Jump probability} \implies \uparrow D$$

📈 Mathematical Linearization of the Arrhenius Equation

Starting from the exponential rate law $D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{E_D}{R \cdot T}\right)$ and taking natural logarithms on both sides:

$$\ln(D) = \ln(D_0) - \frac{E_D}{R} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{T}\right)$$ $$\underbrace{\ln(D)}_{y} = \underbrace{\ln(D_0)}_{y_0} - \underbrace{\left(\frac{E_D}{R}\right)}_{m} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)}_{x}$$
Linear equation for the Arrhenius plot ($\ln D$ versus $1/T$).
  • Dependent variable ($y$): $\ln(D)$ (dimensionless).
  • Independent variable ($x$): $1/T$ (reciprocal absolute temperature, $\text{K}^{-1}$).
  • Slope of the line ($m$): $$\text{Slope} = -\frac{E_D}{R} \implies E_D = -R \cdot (\text{Slope})$$
  • Y-intercept ($y_0$): Extrapolated value at $1/T \to 0$ equals $\ln(D_0) \implies D_0 = \exp(y_0)$.
Figure 5.1: Linearized Arrhenius plot ($\ln D$ versus $1/T$). Systems with higher activation energy ($E_D$) exhibit steeper negative slopes, reflecting greater temperature sensitivity.

⚖ Comprehensive Comparative Synthesis (Slides 20 & 21)

▲ DIFFUSION is FASTER in...

  • Open crystalline structures (e.g. BCC vs FCC).
  • Lower density materials.
  • Structures with a large number of defects (dislocations, grain boundaries).
  • Neutral diffusing species.
  • Materials with low melting point ($T_m$).
  • Higher temperatures ($T \uparrow$).
  • Small atomic species (interstitial diffusion: C, H, O, N).

▼ DIFFUSION is SLOWER in...

  • Compact crystalline structures (close-packed FCC, HCP).
  • Higher density materials.
  • Structures with small number of defects (single crystals).
  • Charged diffusing species (ionic coulombic traps).
  • Materials with high melting point ($T_m$) (refractory metals).
  • Low temperatures.
  • Large atomic species (substitutional vacancy diffusion).
Session 5 • Engineering Technologies

6. Applications of Diffusion

Critical industrial and aerospace manufacturing processes governed by atomic mass transport: case hardening of transmission gears, ceramic sintering, polymer food barrier membranes, silicon powder nitriding, Rolls-Royce Trent 500 diffusion bonding, semiconductor wafer doping, and diffusion soldering.

⚙ 1. Cementation & Case Hardening of Steels

Gas carburizing (cementation) is a thermochemical surface engineering process based on the interstitial diffusion of carbon atoms into the surface of low-carbon steel components (such as AISI 1010 or 1018 steels, with initial $C_0 \approx 0.10-0.18\text{ wt}\%\text{ C}$). Carburizing operates at elevated temperatures within the austenitic $\gamma$-Fe range ($900^\circ\text{C}-950^\circ\text{C}$) in a controlled hydrocarbon-carrier gas atmosphere ($\mathbf{CH_4 - H_2}$):

🛡 Hardening Mechanism & Residual Stress State

  • Dislocation blocking: Small carbon atoms occupy interstitial positions in the iron lattice, locally distorting the crystal matrix and creating elastic stress fields that "block" or pin dislocation glide. Impeding dislocation motion elevates the yield strength and makes plastic deformation far more difficult $\implies$ Hardening.
  • Compressive residual stresses: The high carbon content on the outer case produces a greater specific volume expansion during martensitic quenching than the low-carbon core. This localized volume expansion leaves the surface in a permanent state of compressive residual stresses, which directly counteract applied tensile cyclic loads and markedly prolong fatigue life.

⚖ Engineering Design Duality: Tough Core vs Hard Case

Workpiece Region Critical Mechanical Property In-Service Engineering Function
Inner core Maximum toughness: Low carbon base steel ($0.10-0.18\text{ wt}\%$). Provides high fracture toughness and impact energy absorption, preventing catastrophic brittle failure under shock loading.
Outer case High hardness & wear resistance: High surface carbon ($\sim 0.8-1.2\text{ wt}\%$). Resists rolling-sliding frictional wear, abrasive scuffing, and contact surface fatigue on gear tooth flanks and bearings.
🛠 Industrial Manufacturing Sequence

Optimal manufacturing sequence: Complex machining (gear hobbing, spline broaching, threading) is performed while the steel is in its ductile, "soft" state to minimize cutting tool wear and machining costs. Once the final geometric tolerances are established, the parts undergo gas carburizing and quenching to achieve the hard exterior case.

Figure 6.1: Cross-section schematic of a case-hardened transmission gear tooth. The carbon-enriched exterior case imparts wear resistance and residual compressive stresses against fatigue, while the low-carbon interior core maintains impact toughness.
🧱 2. Sintering in Ceramics & Powder Metallurgy
📖 Verbatim Definition from Lecture Slides

Sintering (e.g. ceramics): "Mass transport process, thermally activated, conducing to the chemical bonding among particles, and to the reduction of porosity."

Physical Driving Force & Microstructural Evolution:

  • Thermodynamic driving force: Reduction in total excess surface free energy ($\Delta G < 0$) achieved by substituting free powder particle surfaces with grain boundaries and solid interparticle interfaces.
  • Neck formation: Carried out at temperatures typically between $70\%$ and $85\%$ of the absolute melting point ($T_{\text{homologous}} = 0.7-0.85\,T_m$), atomic diffusion through lattice, grain boundaries, and surfaces forms solid neck bridges between adjacent powder particles.
  • Pore elimination: A continuous flux of vacancies flows from pores into grain boundaries (which act as defect sinks), shrinking and closing internal pores to achieve dense, high-strength bulk components.
🥤 3. Gas Transport Through Polymer Films (Food Packaging Barriers)

In the packaging and preservation of fresh and processed food products, shelf life is fundamentally limited by molecular diffusion and permeation of gases through polymeric barrier films:

💧 Water Vapour Barrier Performance

  • Degradation mechanisms under poor water vapor barrier: Fresh agricultural and meat products dry out; dried culinary powders agglomerate and form solid lumps (clumping); fried snacks and potato chips absorb ambient moisture and lose crispness (lose crispness).
  • Quantitative quality benchmark (verbatim from slides):
    $$\mathbf{\text{Water Vapour Transmission Rate} < 10\text{ g}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{day}) \quad \text{measured at } 23^\circ\text{C} \text{ and } 90\%\text{ RH (Relative Humidity)}}$$

🐟 Oxygen & Gas Barrier Performance

  • In the presence of diffusing oxygen ($O_2$), par-baked bakery items and cheeses rapidly develop mold.
  • Fresh meats experience surface discoloration and accelerated aerobic bacterial growth. Specifically noted in slides: "Sliced meat turns grey under shop lighting."
  • High-fat food products undergo oxidative rancidity, particularly when accelerated by ambient artificial or solar light.
  • "Best atmospheres depend on food:" Modified Atmosphere Packaging (MAP) frequently replaces oxygen with nitrogen ($N_2$) or carbon dioxide ($CO_2$).

Formula Cheatsheet & Exam Reference

High-density summary of governing equations, physical constants, SI unit conventions, and classic examination traps for Topic 3 (Diffusion in Solids).

📜 1. Fundamental Governing Equations
Equation / Law Mathematical Expression Variables & SI Units Validity & Physical Conditions
Diffusion Flux ($J$) $$J = \frac{\Delta m}{A \cdot \Delta t}$$ • $\Delta m$: $\text{kg}$ or $\text{mol}$
• $A$: $\text{m}^2$
• $\Delta t$: $\text{s}$
• $J$: $\text{kg}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$ or $\text{mol}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s})$		 General definition applicable to any regime (steady-state or transient).
Fick’s 1st Law
(Steady State)		 $$J = -D \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = D \frac{C_{\text{ext}} - C_{\text{int}}}{\Delta x}$$ • $D$: diffusivity ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
• $\Delta C$: $\text{kg/m}^3$ or $\text{at/m}^3$
• $\Delta x$: thickness ($\text{m}$)		 $\partial C / \partial t = 0$ and $\partial J / \partial t = 0$. Boundary concentrations are strictly constant.
Arrhenius Law
(Diffusivity)		 $$D = D_0 \exp\left(-\frac{E_D}{R \cdot T}\right)$$ • $D_0$: pre-exponential factor ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
• $E_D$: activation energy ($\text{J/mol}$ or $\text{cal/mol}$)
• $R$: $8.314\text{ J/mol}\cdot\text{K}$ or $1.987\text{ cal/mol}\cdot\text{K}$
• $T$: Kelvin ($\text{K}$)		 Thermally activated atomic transport in solid media.
Linearized Arrhenius
(Plot Form)		 $$\ln D = \ln D_0 - \frac{E_D}{R} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{T}\right)$$ • Slope: $m = -E_D / R$
• Y-intercept: $y_0 = \ln(D_0)$
• X-variable: $1/T$ ($\text{K}^{-1}$)		 Allows determining $E_D$ and $D_0$ from two experimental points $(D_1, T_1)$ and $(D_2, T_2)$.
Fick’s 2nd Law
(Differential Form)		 $$\frac{\partial C_x}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 C_x}{\partial x^2}$$ • $C_x$: concentration ($C(x,t)$)
• $t$: elapsed time ($\text{s}$)
• $x$: distance ($\text{m}$)		 Non-steady state regime ($\partial C / \partial t \neq 0$) under the hypothesis $D \neq f(C)$.
Semi-Infinite Solid Solution
(Error Function)		 $$\frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = 1 - \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)$$ $$\frac{C_s - C_x}{C_s - C_0} = \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)$$ • $C_x$: conc. at depth $x$ after time $t$
• $C_s$: surface conc. ($x=0$)
• $C_0$: initial bulk conc. ($t=0$)
• Argument $z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$		 Flat surface, semi-infinite solid, constant $C_s$, uniform initial $C_0$.
Parabolic Scaling Law $$\frac{x_1^2}{D_1 \cdot t_1} = \frac{x_2^2}{D_2 \cdot t_2}$$ • At constant $T \implies D = \text{const} \implies \frac{x_1^2}{t_1} = \frac{x_2^2}{t_2}$ Applies whenever target concentration ratio is fixed: $\frac{C_x - C_0}{C_s - C_0} = \text{constant}$.
Self-Diffusion Components $$E_a = E_{\text{formation}} + E_{\text{movement}}$$ • $E_a$: total self-diffusion energy
• $E_f$: vacancy formation energy
• $E_m$: vacancy migration energy		 Pure elemental crystals. Correlates directly with melting point: $\uparrow T_m \implies \uparrow E_a$.
🚨 Checklist of Classic Examination Pitfalls (Exam Traps)
1. Temperature Scale Trap (Celsius vs Kelvin)

Fatal error: Inserting $T$ in $^\circ\text{C}$ into the Arrhenius exponent or gas law. In the term $e^{-E_a/RT}$, temperature must always and strictly be evaluated in Kelvin: $$T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$$ Examples: $927^\circ\text{C} = 1200\text{ K}$; $1293^\circ\text{C} = 1566\text{ K}$.

2. Energy Units and Gas Constant R Consistency

Always double check unit consistency between activation energy $E_a$ and the molar gas constant $R$:
• If $E_a$ is in $\text{J/mol} \implies$ use $R = 8.314\text{ J}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$.
• If $E_a$ is in $\text{cal/mol} \implies$ use $R = 1.987\text{ cal}/(\text{mol}\cdot\text{K})$.
• If $E_a$ is given in $\mathbf{kcal/mol} \implies$ remember to multiply by $1000$! ($E_a = 73\text{ kcal/mol} = 73,000\text{ cal/mol}$).

3. Time Unit Conversions in Fick’s 1st and 2nd Laws

Diffusivity $D$ is almost universally given in $\text{m}^2/\mathbf{s}$ or $\text{cm}^2/\mathbf{s}$.
• If mass flow is given in $\text{kg}/\mathbf{h}$, convert to $\text{kg}/\mathbf{s}$ by dividing by $3600\text{ s}$.
• If a heat treatment runs for $8\text{ hours}$, convert to seconds: $t = 8 \times 3600 = 28,800\text{ s}$.

4. Error Function Argument Factor 2

The argument of $\text{erf}(z)$ is $z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}$. Do not drop the factor of $2$ in the denominator! $$z = \frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}} \implies 2\sqrt{Dt} = \frac{x}{z} \implies \sqrt{Dt} = \frac{x}{2z} \implies Dt = \left(\frac{x}{2z}\right)^2$$
