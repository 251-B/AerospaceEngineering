Crystalline Systems & Bravais Lattices

The geometric foundations of solids: spatial lattices, the 7 crystal systems, and the 14 Bravais lattices.

1. Why Study the Structure of Materials?

In aerospace, mechanical, and materials engineering, understanding the microscopic structure is not an abstract academic exercise. Every macroscopic engineering property and system performance threshold is a direct consequence of the internal arrangement of its constituents:

$$\mathbf{\text{Properties of a Material}} = f(\mathbf{\text{Structure}})$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Performance of an Engineering Component}} = f(\mathbf{\text{Properties of the Material}})$$
The fundamental law of materials selection and structural design (Session 3, Slides 2–4).
✈️ Classic Engineering Thought Experiment: Choosing an Aircraft Wing Material

Consider what happens if we attempt to manufacture an aircraft wing from various material families:

  • Polymer (e.g. Vulcanized Rubber): Too flexible. Under aerodynamic lift, elastic deformation is excessive; the airfoil geometry cannot be maintained, causing immediate loss of control.
  • Pure Elemental Copper (FCC Metal): Too weak / ductile. Although it possesses high electrical and thermal conductivity, pure copper undergoes severe plastic yield at low stresses and has high density ($\rho \approx 8.96\text{ g/cm}^3$).
  • Ceramic (e.g. Diamond or Alumina): Too brittle. While boasting extreme hardness and high elastic modulus, ceramics exhibit near-zero fracture toughness ($K_{Ic}$); any micro-flaw propagates catastrophically under tensile or vibrational flight loads.

Definition of Structure: The spatial arrangement of the internal components of a material, spanning across four distinct observational scales:

  1. Subatomic scale: Electronic configurations, bonding nature, orbital hybridization.
  2. Atomic scale: Arrangement of atoms, ions, or molecules in space (crystal lattices, long-range vs. short-range order).
  3. Microscopic (microstructural) scale: Grain boundaries, phases, precipitate dispersions, dislocation networks.
  4. Macroscopic scale: Visible structural shapes, porosity, surface finishes, and bulk component geometry.

2. Crystalline vs. Amorphous (Non-Crystalline) Solids

Depending on how atoms, ions, or molecules position themselves when condensing from the liquid or gas phase into a solid, matter divides into two fundamental states:

💎 Crystalline Solids

  • Periodic long-range order: Atoms arrange in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern spanning billions of atomic distances.
  • Thermodynamic driver: Atoms naturally tend to assume positions of minimum potential energy ($E_{\text{min}}$).
  • Dense, ordered packing: Maximizes interatomic bonding forces and minimizes system free energy.
  • Sharp melting point: All bonds break cooperatively at a precise thermodynamic transition temperature ($T_m$).
  • Prevalence: Almost all metals and metallic alloys, semiconductor crystals (Si, Ge), and most ceramics ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{MgO}$, $\text{ZrO}_2$, $\text{NaCl}$).

🏺 Amorphous (Non-Crystalline) Solids

  • Absence of long-range order: Lacks long-range spatial periodicity or crystal symmetry; exhibits only short-range order (nearest-neighbor coordination).
  • Kinetic constraint: Solids remain amorphous only if they are prevented from crystallizing (e.g. extremely rapid cooling rates "freezing" the disordered liquid structure).
  • Non-dense, random packing: Larger internal free volume, lower density than their crystalline counterparts.
  • Glass transition range: Softens progressively over a temperature range ($T_g$) rather than exhibiting a sharp melting point.
  • Prevalence: Inorganic oxide glasses ($\text{SiO}_2$ silica glass), amorphous polymers (polystyrene, PMMA), and metallic glasses (melt-spun alloys).

3. The Discovery of Space Filling: René Just Haüy (1781)

The modern scientific theory of crystal structures originated with the French mineralogist Abbé René Just Haüy in 1781 through his accidental dropping and fracturing of a calcite ($\text{CaCO}_3$) crystal:

💎 Haüy's Two Foundational Discoveries
  1. Constancy of Cleavage Angles: Regardless of how violently the calcite specimen was shattered or how infinitesimal the resulting shards became, every fragment consistently cleaved into perfect rhombohedra with identical interfacial angles.
  2. Void-Free Space Filling: In the intact crystal, no microscopic voids existed between these building blocks; when packed together, these constituent polyhedra fill 3-dimensional Euclidean space continuously and completely without gaps.

This led to the fundamental mathematical question of crystallography: How many distinct geometric shapes can fill three-dimensional space completely via simple translational repetition without creating voids?

4. Space Lattice, Basis (Motif), and Unit Cell

To analyze crystal structures rigorously, crystallographers separate the abstract geometry from the physical atoms:

$$\mathbf{\text{Crystal Structure}} = \mathbf{\text{Lattice}} + \mathbf{\text{Basis (Motif)}}$$
The fundamental equation of structural crystallography (Session 3, Slide 8).
  • Crystalline Space Lattice: An infinite three-dimensional periodic array of mathematical points in space, wherein every lattice point possesses an identical atomic environment. Any lattice point can be reached from the origin by the translation vector: $$\vec{T} = u\,\vec{a} + v\,\vec{b} + w\,\vec{c} \quad (u, v, w \in \mathbb{Z})$$
  • Basis (Motif): A group of one or more physical atoms, ions, or molecules attached identically to every single lattice point. In pure metals (e.g. Cu, Al, Fe), the basis consists of a single atom located at each lattice point. In compounds (e.g. NaCl, GaAs), the basis consists of multiple distinct ions or atoms.
  • Unit Cell: The smallest repeating spatial volume (parallelepiped) that embodies all the symmetry elements of the crystal. Parallelepiped capable of reproducing the crystal — the "master model" (Session 3, Slide 10). By translating this master model along its edge vectors $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$, the entire macroscopic crystal is generated without voids or overlaps.
⚔️ Discussion Hook: The Metallurgy of a "Hattori Hanzo (Hanzō)" Sword

"How are atoms arranged in a 'Hattori Hanzo (Hanzō)' sword? (Kill Bill, 2003) — Or in any metallic material?" (Session 3, Slide 10).

Whether examining legendary Japanese katana steel (e.g. folded tamahagane consisting of BCC ferrite $\alpha$-Fe and BCT martensite) or modern aerospace turbine alloys, metals are not amorphous blobs. Every individual atom sits at precise, repetitive coordinates within a crystalline space lattice, assuming positions of minimum potential energy.

The 6 Lattice Parameters

A unit cell is completely defined in space by 3 basis vectors originating from a common vertex ($\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$), yielding 6 independent lattice parameters:

  • Axial lengths (edge dimensions): $a, b, c$ (measured in Ångströms, $\text{\AA} = 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.1\text{ nm}$).
  • Interaxial angles:
    • $\alpha$: angle between vectors $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$ (opposite to axis $x$).
    • $\beta$: angle between vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{c}$ (opposite to axis $y$).
    • $\gamma$: angle between vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ (opposite to axis $z$).
Figure 2.2: Unit Cell Geometry and the 6 Lattice Parameters. Vectors $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ define edge lengths along crystallographic axes $x, y, z$, while interaxial angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ describe interfacial skewness (Session 3, Slide 10).

5. The 7 Crystalline Systems

By considering all geometric permutations of axial equalities ($a, b, c$) and angle constraints ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$), three-dimensional space can be partitioned into exactly 7 crystal systems:

1. Cubic a = b = c α = β = γ = 90° Four 3-fold rotation axes ($\langle 111 \rangle$) P, I, F (3)
2. Tetragonal a = b ≠ c α = β = γ = 90° One 4-fold rotation axis ($c$-axis) P, I (2)
3. Orthorhombic a ≠ b ≠ c α = β = γ = 90° Three mutually perpendicular 2-fold axes P, C, I, F (4)
4. Rhombohedral (Trigonal) a = b = c α = β = γ ≠ 90° (< 120°) One 3-fold rotation axis along body diagonal P (1)
5. Hexagonal a = b ≠ c α = β = 90°, γ = 120° One 6-fold rotation axis ($c$-axis) P (1)
6. Monoclinic a ≠ b ≠ c α = γ = 90° ≠ β One 2-fold rotation axis or mirror plane P, C (2)
7. Triclinic a ≠ b ≠ c α ≠ β ≠ γ ≠ 90° No rotational symmetry (inversion center only) P (1)

6. The 14 Bravais Lattices (Auguste Bravais, 1848)

The 7 crystal systems define only the exterior geometry (shape) of the unit cell. To describe where atoms, ions, or molecules are located, French physicist Auguste Bravais demonstrated mathematically in 1848 that there are only 4 distinct ways to place lattice points within a unit cell such that every point retains an identical environment:

1. Simple / Primitive ($P$)

Contains lattice points only at the 8 corners of the unit cell. Each corner point is shared among 8 adjacent cells, contributing exactly $8 \times (1/8) = 1$ lattice point per unit cell.

2. Body-Centered ($I$, Innenzentriert)

Points at the 8 corners plus 1 point at the exact center $(1/2, 1/2, 1/2)$ of the unit cell volume. Net points per cell $= 8 \times (1/8) + 1 = 2$.

3. Face-Centered ($F$)

Points at the 8 corners plus points on all 6 face centers. Each face point is shared by 2 neighboring cells. Net points per cell $= 8 \times (1/8) + 6 \times (1/2) = 1 + 3 = 4$.

4. Base-Centered ($C$, or $A, B$)

Points at the 8 corners plus points on one pair of opposite parallel faces (usually the top and bottom $xy$ bases). Net points per cell $= 8 \times (1/8) + 2 \times (1/2) = 1 + 1 = 2$.

📐 Why 14 and Not $7 \times 4 = 28$ Lattices?

If every centring type were mathematically distinct in every system, there would be $7 \times 4 = 28$ lattices. However, only 14 combinations preserve distinct crystallographic symmetry and cannot be reduced to a simpler, smaller unit cell:

  • Face-Centered Tetragonal ($FCT$): An $FCT$ cell can always be chosen as a Body-Centered Tetragonal ($BCT$) cell with a volume $1/2$ as large, rotated by $45^\circ$ around the $c$-axis. Thus, $FCT \equiv BCT$.
  • Base-Centered Tetragonal ($C$): Choosing new axes at $45^\circ$ in the basal plane yields a smaller Primitive Tetragonal ($P$) cell. Thus, Base-Centered Tetragonal does not exist.
  • Base-Centered Cubic ($C$): Placing points on only two opposite faces of a cube violates the mandatory 3-fold cubic symmetry along $\langle 111 \rangle$ diagonals, lowering the symmetry from cubic to tetragonal.

Atomic Packing & Stacking Sequences

From 2D hard-sphere arrangements to 3D compact crystal structures: $ABAB\dots$ (HCP) vs. $ABCABC\dots$ (FCC).

1. Planar Packing of Spheres (2D Geometry)

When identical rigid spheres (representing metallic atoms with non-directional bonds) are laid out on a flat two-dimensional plane, they can pack in two fundamentally different configurations:

A. Non-Compact Plane (Square Grid)

  • Arrangement: Atoms align in orthogonal rows and columns (square lattice).
  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Each atom contacts directly 4 coplanar neighbors ($CN = 4$).
  • Interstitial Voids: Large square voids are created between four contacting spheres.
  • Planar Packing Density: $$\eta_{\text{2D, square}} = \frac{\pi R^2}{(2R)^2} = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 78.54\%$$
  • Significance: Represents the $\{100\}$ cubic face of Simple Cubic and Body-Centered Cubic structures.

B. Close-Packed Plane (Hexagonal Grid)

  • Arrangement: Atoms of each row sit in the triangular hollows formed by the adjacent row.
  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Each atom contacts directly 6 coplanar neighbors ($CN = 6$).
  • Interstitial Voids: Small triangular hollows pointing alternatingly up ($\triangle$) and down ($\triangledown$).
  • Planar Packing Density: $$\eta_{\text{2D, hex}} = \frac{\pi R^2}{2\sqrt{3}R^2} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \approx 90.69\%$$
  • Significance: The mathematically maximum possible packing efficiency of identical circles in 2D Euclidean space. Forms the $\{111\}$ planes of FCC and the $(0001)$ basal planes of HCP.
Figure 2.3: 2D Packing of Spheres. Left: Non-compact square plane with coordination number $CN=4$ and $78.5\%$ area density. Right: Close-packed hexagonal plane with coordination number $CN=6$ and maximum planar packing density of $90.7\%$ (Session 3, Slides 27–29).

2. Stacking Sequences of 3D Layers

When building three-dimensional crystals from two-dimensional planes, the structural outcome depends directly on whether non-compact or close-packed planes are stacked:

A. Stacking Non-Compact (Square) Planes

  • Non-compact stacking ($AAAA\dots$): Spheres of Layer 2 sit directly on top of spheres of Layer 1. Generates the Simple Cubic (SC) crystal structure. Each atom touches 4 in-plane neighbors + 1 above + 1 below ($CN = 6$). Packing is exceptionally loose ($\text{APF} = 52.4\%$). Polonium ($\alpha$-Po) is the sole known elemental crystal.
  • Interleaved stacking ($ABAB\dots$): Spheres of Layer B are shifted so that each sphere sits nested in the central square depression between four spheres of Layer A. Generates the Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) crystal structure ($CN = 8, \text{APF} = 68.0\%$).

B. Stacking Close-Packed (Hexagonal) Planes: HCP vs. FCC

When close-packed hexagonal layers are stacked, maximum volumetric efficiency ($74.0\%$) is achieved. Let the first close-packed plane be designated as Layer A.

In Layer A, there are two distinct sets of triangular hollows: upward-pointing triangles ($\triangle$, say set $B$) and downward-pointing triangles ($\triangledown$, say set $C$). To place a second close-packed layer (Layer B), spheres nest into all triangular hollows of set $B$. The hollows of set $C$ remain vacant.

Now, when adding the third close-packed layer, two geometrically distinct choices exist:

1. Stacking Sequence $ABABAB\dots$ → HCP Structure

The spheres of the 3rd layer are placed into the triangular depressions of Layer B that lie directly vertically above the atoms of Layer A.

  • Layer 1 = A, Layer 2 = B, Layer 3 = A, Layer 4 = B $\dots$
  • Crystallographic Symmetry: Hexagonal symmetry.
  • Resulting Structure: Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP).
  • Coordination Number: $CN = 12$ (6 coplanar in layer B, 3 in layer A below, 3 in layer A above).
  • Volumetric Packing: $\text{APF} = 74\%$.
  • Examples: Ti, Mg, Cd, Co, Zn, Zr, Be.

2. Stacking Sequence $ABCABC\dots$ → FCC Structure

The spheres of the 3rd layer are placed into the triangular depressions of Layer B that lie over the vacant set $C$ hollows of Layer A.

  • Layer 3 aligns with neither Layer A nor Layer B! It forms a new position: Layer C.
  • Layer 4 then aligns with Layer A, establishing the repeating sequence $ABCABC\dots$
  • Crystallographic Symmetry: Cubic symmetry! The close-packed planes form the family of $\{111\}$ octahedral planes viewed along the cube's body diagonal $\langle 111 \rangle$.
  • Resulting Structure: Face-Centered Cubic (FCC).
  • Coordination Number: $CN = 12$ (6 in layer B, 3 in layer A below, 3 in layer C above).
  • Volumetric Packing: $\text{APF} = 74\%$.
  • Examples: Al, Cu, Ni, Pb, Pt, Ag, Au, $\gamma$-Fe.
Figure 2.4: 3D Close-Packed Stacking Sequences. Left: $ABABAB\dots$ sequence in Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP). Right: $ABCABC\dots$ sequence in Face-Centered Cubic (FCC), revealing that FCC is close-packed along $\{111\}$ plane normals (Session 3, Slides 34–38).

Main Metallic Structures (BCC, FCC, HCP)

Exhaustive geometric derivations: hard-sphere contact, coordination numbers, effective atoms per cell, and complete APF proofs.

1. Physical Origins of Metallic Crystal Structures

More than 90% of all metallic elements in the periodic table crystallize into just three densely packed structures: BCC, FCC, or HCP. This remarkable physical convergence stems from three core principles of metallic bonding:

  1. Single Elemental Identity (Equal Atomic Radii): In a pure metal, all lattice sites are occupied by atoms of the same chemical element possessing an identical atomic radius ($R$). Spheres of equal size pack with high symmetry.
  2. Non-Directional Nature of the Metallic Bond: Unlike covalent bonds (which require strict orbital overlap angles such as $109.5^\circ$ $sp^3$ tetrahedra) or ionic bonds (which demand local cation-anion charge neutrality), the metallic bond consists of positive ion cores submerged in a delocalized, freely floating "sea" of valence electrons. Bonds can form with equal strength in any spatial direction.
  3. Minimization of Potential Energy ($E_{\text{minimum}}$): Because metallic bonds are non-directional, atoms pack as closely as physically possible to maximize the number of nearest neighbors (coordination number). Shorter interatomic distances produce deeper potential energy wells and significantly higher cohesive lattice energies: $$\text{Smaller interatomic distances } r_0 \implies \text{Deeper potential well } E_0 \implies \text{Higher lattice energy & mechanical stability}$$

2. Body-Centered Cubic Structure (BCC)

In the Body-Centered Cubic structure, a unit cell cube contains one atom at each of the 8 corners plus one full atom positioned at the precise geometric center of the cell.

🔩 Representative BCC Metals
Alpha-iron ($\alpha$-Fe, room temperature ferrite), Chromium (Cr), Molybdenum (Mo), Vanadium (V), Tungsten (W), Tantalum (Ta), Sodium (Na), Potassium (K), Barium (Ba).
Derivation 3.1: Relationship Between Lattice Parameter $a$ and Atomic Radius $R$ in BCC
Step 1
Identify the hard-sphere contact direction: In a BCC lattice, corner atoms do NOT touch each other along the cubic edges ($a > 2R$). Instead, the central body atom touches all 8 corner atoms along the body diagonals ($\langle 111 \rangle$).
Step 2
Calculate the face diagonal $d$: Applying the Pythagorean theorem to any face of the cube: $$d^2 = a^2 + a^2 = 2a^2 \implies d = a\sqrt{2}$$
Step 3
Calculate the body diagonal $D$: Applying the Pythagorean theorem to the triangle formed by face diagonal $d$, vertical edge $a$, and body diagonal $D$: $$D^2 = d^2 + a^2 = 2a^2 + a^2 = 3a^2 \implies D = a\sqrt{3}$$
Step 4
Equate body diagonal to hard-sphere radii: The body diagonal spans one corner radius ($R$), the full central atom diameter ($2R$), and the opposite corner radius ($R$): $$D = R + 2R + R = 4R$$ $$4R = a\sqrt{3} \implies \mathbf{a = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 2.3094\,R}$$

Structural Characteristics of BCC

  • Effective Number of Atoms per Unit Cell ($N$): $$N = 1 \text{ (central atom)} + 8 \times \left(\frac{1}{8}\right) \text{ (corner atoms)} = 1 + 1 = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms/cell}}$$
  • Coordination Number ($CN$): The central atom contacts exactly the 8 corner atoms surrounding it: $$CN = \mathbf{8}$$
Derivation 3.2: Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of BCC
Step 1
Calculate total atomic volume inside the unit cell ($V_{\text{atoms}}$): $$V_{\text{atoms}} = N \cdot V_{\text{sphere}} = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) = \frac{8}{3}\pi R^3$$
Step 2
Calculate total unit cell volume ($V_{\text{cell}}$): $$V_{\text{cell}} = a^3 = \left(\frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^3 = \frac{64\,R^3}{3\sqrt{3}}$$
Step 3
Compute the ratio $\text{APF} = \frac{V_{\text{atoms}}}{V_{\text{cell}}}$: $$\text{APF} = \frac{\frac{8}{3}\pi R^3}{\frac{64\,R^3}{3\sqrt{3}}} = \frac{8\pi \cdot 3\sqrt{3}}{3 \cdot 64} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{8} \approx \mathbf{0.68017 \implies 68.0\%}$$

Physical takeaway: In a BCC metal, $68.0\%$ of the unit cell volume is occupied by solid hard spheres, while the remaining $32.0\%$ consists of unoccupied interstitial voids.

3. Face-Centered Cubic Structure (FCC)

In the Face-Centered Cubic structure, atoms sit at all 8 corners of the cube, plus one atom centered on each of the 6 cube faces.

✈️ Representative FCC Metals (High Ductility)
Aluminum (Al), Copper (Cu), Nickel (Ni), Lead (Pb), Platinum (Pt), Silver (Ag), Gold (Au), Gamma-iron ($\gamma$-Fe, high-temperature austenite), Palladium (Pd).
Derivation 3.3: Relationship Between Lattice Parameter $a$ and Atomic Radius $R$ in FCC
Step 1
Identify the hard-sphere contact direction: In an FCC unit cell, atoms touch directly across the face diagonals ($\langle 110 \rangle$). Corner atoms do NOT touch along the cubic edge.
Step 2
Calculate the face diagonal $d$: Applying the Pythagorean theorem to any face: $$d^2 = a^2 + a^2 = 2a^2 \implies d = a\sqrt{2}$$
Step 3
Equate face diagonal to hard-sphere radii: Along the face diagonal, the segment covers one corner radius ($R$), the entire central face-atom diameter ($2R$), and the opposite corner radius ($R$): $$d = R + 2R + R = 4R$$ $$4R = a\sqrt{2} \implies \mathbf{a = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}\,R \approx 2.8284\,R}$$

Structural Characteristics of FCC

  • Effective Number of Atoms per Unit Cell ($N$): $$N = 8 \times \left(\frac{1}{8}\right) \text{ (corners)} + 6 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \text{ (face centers)} = 1 + 3 = \mathbf{4\text{ atoms/cell}}$$
  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Each atom (e.g. at a face center) touches 4 coplanar corner atoms, plus 4 face-centered atoms in the plane ahead, and 4 face-centered atoms in the plane behind: $$CN = 4 + 4 + 4 = \mathbf{12}$$
Derivation 3.4: Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of FCC
Step 1
Calculate total atomic volume inside the unit cell ($V_{\text{atoms}}$): $$V_{\text{atoms}} = N \cdot V_{\text{sphere}} = 4 \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) = \frac{16}{3}\pi R^3$$
Step 2
Calculate total unit cell volume ($V_{\text{cell}}$): $$V_{\text{cell}} = a^3 = (2\sqrt{2}\,R)^3 = 8 \cdot 2\sqrt{2}\,R^3 = 16\sqrt{2}\,R^3$$
Step 3
Compute the ratio $\text{APF} = \frac{V_{\text{atoms}}}{V_{\text{cell}}}$: $$\text{APF} = \frac{\frac{16}{3}\pi R^3}{16\sqrt{2}\,R^3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{6} \approx \mathbf{0.74048 \implies 74.0\%}$$

Physical takeaway: FCC represents a close-packed 3D crystal structure. Its Atomic Packing Factor of $74.0\%$ is the theoretical maximum for identical rigid spheres in 3D space (Kepler conjecture, proved by Thomas Hales). Unoccupied void space is only $26.0\%$.

Figure 2.6: Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) Unit Cell. Left: 3D perspective showing 8 corner atoms and 6 face-centered atoms. Right: Front face cross-section proving hard-sphere contact along the face diagonal: $4R = a\sqrt{2}$ (Session 3, Slides 51–53).

4. Hexagonal Close-Packed Structure (HCP)

The Hexagonal Close-Packed structure is built from a hexagonal prism containing two parallel hexagonal basal planes (top and bottom) separated by a distance $c$, with an intermediate mid-plane containing 3 interior atoms nested in the hollows at height $c/2$.

🛡️ Representative HCP Metals
Titanium ($\alpha$-Ti), Magnesium (Mg), Zinc (Zn), Cadmium (Cd), Cobalt (Co), Zirconium (Zr), Beryllium (Be).
Derivation 3.5: Rigorous Proof of the Ideal $c/a$ Axial Ratio in HCP
Step 1
Contact in the basal plane: In each hexagonal base, atoms touch along the outer edges of the regular hexagon: $$\mathbf{a = 2R}$$
Step 2
Analyze the interior tetrahedron: An interior atom in the mid-plane (at height $z = c/2$) sits directly in the triangular hollow between three mutually contacting basal atoms of Layer A. These four atoms form a regular tetrahedron of edge length $a$.
Step 3
Find the distance from a basal vertex to the triangular centroid: The altitude of an equilateral triangle of side $a$ is $h_{\text{tri}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a$. The distance from a vertex to the centroid is two-thirds of the altitude: $$r_{\text{centroid}} = \frac{2}{3} h_{\text{tri}} = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\right) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}$$
Step 4
Apply Pythagorean theorem to the vertical right triangle: The hypotenuse is the interatomic distance between the basal atom and the interior atom ($a$). The horizontal leg is $r_{\text{centroid}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}$, and the vertical leg is $c/2$: $$\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 = a^2$$ $$\frac{c^2}{4} + \frac{a^2}{3} = a^2 \implies \frac{c^2}{4} = a^2 - \frac{a^2}{3} = \frac{2}{3}a^2$$ $$c^2 = \frac{8}{3}a^2 \implies \mathbf{\frac{c}{a} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 1.63299 \approx 1.633}$$

Structural Characteristics of HCP

  • Effective Number of Atoms per Hexagonal Unit Cell ($N$):
    • 12 corner atoms (6 top + 6 bottom), each shared by 6 neighboring hexagonal prisms $\implies 12 \times \frac{1}{6} = 2$ atoms.
    • 2 basal face-center atoms (1 top + 1 bottom), each shared by 2 prisms $\implies 2 \times \frac{1}{2} = 1$ atom.
    • 3 interior atoms fully contained within the unit cell $\implies 3 \times 1 = 3$ atoms.
    $$N = 2 + 1 + 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ atoms/cell}}$$ (Note: In the primitive hexagonal rhombohedral cell, $N = 2$ atoms).
  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Each atom contacts 6 neighbors in its own basal plane + 3 in the plane above + 3 in the plane below: $$CN = 6 + 3 + 3 = \mathbf{12}$$
Derivation 3.6: Atomic Packing Factor (APF) of HCP
Step 1
Calculate the base area $B$ of the regular hexagon: The hexagonal base consists of 6 equilateral triangles of side $a$: $$B = 6 \cdot \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}a^2$$
Step 2
Calculate total unit cell volume ($V_{\text{cell}}$): $$V_{\text{cell}} = B \cdot c = \left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}a^2\right) \cdot \left(a\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}\right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot a^3 = 3\sqrt{2}\,a^3$$ Substituting $a = 2R$: $$V_{\text{cell}} = 3\sqrt{2}\,(2R)^3 = 3\sqrt{2} \cdot 8R^3 = \mathbf{24\sqrt{2}\,R^3}$$
Step 3
Calculate total atomic volume inside the unit cell ($V_{\text{atoms}}$): $$V_{\text{atoms}} = N \cdot V_{\text{sphere}} = 6 \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) = \mathbf{8\pi R^3}$$
Step 4
Compute the ratio $\text{APF} = \frac{V_{\text{atoms}}}{V_{\text{cell}}}$: $$\text{APF} = \frac{8\pi R^3}{24\sqrt{2}\,R^3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{6} \approx \mathbf{0.74048 \implies 74.0\%}$$

Remarkable Crystallographic Identity: Both **FCC** and **HCP** possess identical coordination numbers ($CN = 12$) and identical theoretical Atomic Packing Factors ($\text{APF} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{2}} \approx 74.048\%$). They differ exclusively in their third-layer stacking topology: $ABCABC\dots$ generates cubic symmetry, whereas $ABABAB\dots$ generates hexagonal symmetry.

Interstitial Sites (Octahedral & Tetrahedral)

Geometry, site counts, coordinate locations, and radius ratio derivations in FCC, BCC, and HCP lattices.

1. Fundamentals of Interstitial Voids

In any crystalline solid assembled from contacting spherical atoms, complete spatial filling is impossible. The unoccupied spaces remaining between the host lattice atoms are called interstitial sites (or interstitial voids):

🔘 Definition of Interstitial Site
An interstitial site is a crystallographic position situated between the regular lattice points of a crystal that can be occupied by smaller foreign atoms (solute elements such as Carbon, Nitrogen, Hydrogen, Boron) or smaller ions in ceramic lattices (Session 3, Slide 61).

Depending on the number of host lattice atoms immediately surrounding and coordinating the void space, two fundamental types of interstitial sites exist across crystal structures:

A. Tetrahedral Interstitial Site

  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Coordinated by exactly 4 host lattice atoms forming the vertices of a regular tetrahedron.
  • Void Geometry: Formed when one sphere rests in the triangular hollow between three mutually contacting coplanar spheres.
  • Relative Size: Characterized by a smaller interstitial radius ratio ($r/R$).

B. Octahedral Interstitial Site

  • Coordination Number ($CN$): Coordinated by exactly 6 host lattice atoms forming the vertices of an octahedron.
  • Void Geometry: Formed when three spheres in one close-packed plane meet three spheres in an adjacent plane with opposing triangular orientations.
  • Relative Size: Characterized by a significantly larger interstitial radius ratio ($r/R$) in close-packed structures.
$$\text{For any Close-Packed Structure with } n \text{ host atoms per unit cell:}$$ $$\mathbf{N_{\text{octahedral sites}} = n} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathbf{N_{\text{tetrahedral sites}} = 2n}$$ $$\text{Stoichiometric Void Ratio: } \mathbf{1\text{ host atom} : 1\text{ octahedral site} : 2\text{ tetrahedral sites}}$$
The universal interstitial counting theorem for compact crystals (FCC and HCP) (Session 3, Slide 62).

2. Interstitial Sites in the FCC Structure ($n = 4$)

Because the Face-Centered Cubic lattice contains $n = 4$ host atoms per unit cell, it possesses exactly 4 octahedral sites and 8 tetrahedral sites per unit cell:

A. Octahedral Sites in FCC (Total = 4 sites/cell)

  • Unit Cell Center: Exactly 1 site at the geometric body center: $$\text{Position: } \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) \implies 1 \times 1 = \mathbf{1\text{ site}}$$ It is coordinated by the 6 face-centered atoms of the cube.
  • Unit Cell Edges: Exactly 12 sites located at the midpoint of each of the 12 cubic edges: $$\text{Positions: } \left(\frac{1}{2}, 0, 0\right), \left(0, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right), \left(0, 0, \frac{1}{2}\right), \dots \implies 12 \times \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \mathbf{3\text{ sites}}$$ Each edge site is shared equally among 4 adjacent unit cells sharing that edge.
  • Net Octahedral Count: $1 + 3 = \mathbf{4\text{ octahedral sites per FCC unit cell}}$.

Derivation 4.1: Maximum Radius of an Octahedral Void in FCC ($r_{\text{oct}}/R$)
Step 1
Examine hard-sphere contact along the cubic edge: Along any edge of length $a$, the edge-center octahedral void of radius $r_{\text{oct}}$ is wedged directly between two corner host atoms of radius $R$: $$2R + 2r_{\text{oct}} = a \implies R + r_{\text{oct}} = \frac{a}{2}$$
Step 2
Substitute the FCC lattice parameter relation: Recall that in FCC, face-diagonal contact gives $a = 2\sqrt{2}\,R$: $$R + r_{\text{oct}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\,R}{2} = \sqrt{2}\,R$$
Step 3
Solve for the radius ratio: $$r_{\text{oct}} = \sqrt{2}\,R - R = (\sqrt{2} - 1)R \approx \mathbf{0.4142\,R \implies \frac{r_{\text{oct}}}{R} \approx 0.414}$$

B. Tetrahedral Sites in FCC (Total = 8 sites/cell)

  • Interior Sub-Cubes: If the FCC unit cell (edge length $a$) is partitioned into 8 smaller sub-cubes of edge length $a/2$, exactly one tetrahedral void sits at the center of each sub-cube.
  • Positions: Located at fractional coordinates: $$\left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}\right), \left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}\right), \left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{4}\right), \left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{3}{4}\right), \dots \text{ (8 distinct positions)}$$
  • Cell Ownership: Because all 8 sub-cube centers reside completely within the interior of the unit cell, none are shared: $$8 \times 1 = \mathbf{8\text{ tetrahedral sites per FCC unit cell}}$$
  • Coordination: Coordinated by 1 corner atom and 3 adjacent face-centered atoms forming a regular tetrahedron of side length $a/\sqrt{2} = 2R$.
Derivation 4.2: Maximum Radius of a Tetrahedral Void in FCC ($r_{\text{tet}}/R$)
Step 1
Distance from corner to tetrahedral site: The site lies at $(a/4, a/4, a/4)$. The distance from corner $(0,0,0)$ to the site is: $$d = \sqrt{\left(\frac{a}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{4}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{3a^2}{16}} = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{4}$$
Step 2
Hard-sphere contact equation: At maximum packing without lattice distortion: $$R + r_{\text{tet}} = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{4}$$
Step 3
Substitute $a = 2\sqrt{2}\,R$: $$R + r_{\text{tet}} = \frac{(2\sqrt{2}\,R)\sqrt{3}}{4} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{4}R = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}R = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,R$$ $$r_{\text{tet}} = \left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} - 1\right)R \approx (1.2247 - 1)R \approx \mathbf{0.2247\,R \implies \frac{r_{\text{tet}}}{R} \approx 0.225}$$

  • Edge Midpoints: Located at the center of each of the 12 cubic edges: $$\text{Positions: } \left(\frac{1}{2}, 0, 0\right), \left(0, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right), \left(0, 0, \frac{1}{2}\right), \dots \implies 12 \times \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \mathbf{3\text{ sites}}$$
  • Net Octahedral Count: $3 + 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ octahedral sites per BCC unit cell}}$.

⚠️ The Anisotropic Distorted Octahedral Void in BCC

Unlike the regular, spherical octahedral sites of FCC, the octahedral site in BCC is severely compressed and distorted. Consider the site at the face center $(1/2, 1/2, 0)$:

  • It is bounded by 2 axial host atoms (the body-center atoms of this cell and the neighboring cell along the $z$-axis) at a very short separation distance of $a/2 = \mathbf{0.500\,a}$.
  • It is bounded by 4 coplanar corner atoms in the $xy$-plane at a much larger distance of $\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \mathbf{0.707\,a}$.
  • Because the 2 axial atoms are intensely crowded, the maximum allowable radius without straining the lattice along the $z$-axis is: $$2R + 2r_{\text{oct}} = a \implies R + r_{\text{oct}} = \frac{a}{2}$$ Substituting $a = \frac{4R}{\sqrt{3}}$: $$R + r_{\text{oct}} = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}} \implies r_{\text{oct}} = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - 1\right)R \approx \mathbf{0.1547\,R \implies \frac{r_{\text{oct}}}{R} \approx 0.155}$$

B. Tetrahedral Sites in BCC (Total = 12 sites/cell)

  • Face Positions: Each of the 6 faces contains 4 tetrahedral sites situated between two face edges and the center: $$\text{Positions on } z=0 \text{ face: } \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, 0\right), \left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, 0\right), \left(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right), \left(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$$
  • Total Count: With 4 sites on each of the 6 faces, and each face shared by 2 unit cells: $$6 \times 4 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \mathbf{12\text{ tetrahedral sites per BCC unit cell}}$$
  • Radius Ratio: The tetrahedral void is more symmetrical, yielding: $$\frac{r_{\text{tet}}}{R} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{3}} - 1 \approx \mathbf{0.291}$$
💡 The Carbon-in-Iron Paradox (Austenite vs. Ferrite & Martensite Formation)

In BCC, the tetrahedral site ($r = 0.291\,R$) is significantly larger than the octahedral site ($r = 0.155\,R$). Why, then, does interstitial carbon ($r_C \approx 0.071\text{ nm}$) in $\alpha$-Fe ($R_{\text{Fe}} \approx 0.124\text{ nm}$, so $r_C/R_{\text{Fe}} \approx 0.57$) preferentially occupy the smaller OCTAHEDRAL site?

The Physical Mechanism: The octahedral site has only 2 close neighbors along one cubic axis, while its 4 other neighbors have ample room. To accommodate the oversized carbon atom, the lattice only needs to displace two iron atoms along a single axis ($\vec{c}$). In contrast, a tetrahedral site is tightly constricted by 4 symmetrically placed atoms; inserting carbon requires forcing 4 atoms apart simultaneously, demanding drastically higher elastic strain energy! When steel is quenched from FCC austenite ($\gamma$, maximum $2.14\text{ wt}\%\text{ C}$) to BCC ferrite ($\alpha$, max $0.022\text{ wt}\%\text{ C}$), carbon atoms trapped in these octahedral sites force an expansion along one axis, transforming the cubic lattice into Body-Centered Tetragonal (BCT) Martensite—the source of hardened steel's extreme strength!

Directions in Crystalline Cells

Vector indexing $[uvw]$, origin shifts for negative indices, directional families $\langle uvw \rangle$, angle formulas, and hexagonal Miller-Bravais notation.

1. Crystallographic Direction Indices $[u\,v\,w]$

A crystallographic direction is defined as a line between two lattice points, represented by a vector passing through the origin.

↗️ Official Definition: Crystallographic Direction Indices
"Crystallographic direction indices are the vector components of the direction resolved along each of the coordinate axes and reduced to the smallest integers" (Session 3, Slide 72).
Standard 4-Step Procedure to Determine Direction Indices $[u\,v\,w]$
Step 1
Vector Positioning: Establish a coordinate origin. If the direction vector does not originate at $(0,0,0)$, translate it parallel to itself until its tail rests at the origin $O$, or compute coordinate differences: $$\Delta x = x_{\text{head}} - x_{\text{tail}}, \quad \Delta y = y_{\text{head}} - y_{\text{tail}}, \quad \Delta z = z_{\text{head}} - z_{\text{tail}}$$
Step 2
Determine Axis Projections: Measure the projections along the three coordinate axes in terms of unit cell dimensions $a, b, c$: $$\text{Projections: } \left(\frac{\Delta x}{a}, \frac{\Delta y}{b}, \frac{\Delta z}{c}\right)$$
Step 3
Clear Fractions to Smallest Integers: Multiply or divide all three components by a common factor to reduce them to the smallest integers $u, v, w$.
Step 4
Enclose in Square Brackets: Write the indices enclosed in square brackets without commas: $$\mathbf{[u\,v\,w]}$$ If any component is negative, represent it by placing a bar over the index: $[\bar{u}\,v\,w]$ (read: "bar-u, v, w").

2. Drawing Directions & The Origin Translation Rule

When drawing a direction $[u\,v\,w]$ inside a unit cell, or when handling negative indices, follow these exact class rules:

A. Drawing Directions with Positive Indices (Class Slide Examples)

To keep the direction vector within the bounds of a single unit cell, apply the standard 4-step procedure (Session 3, Slides 73–76):

Example 1: Draw Direction $[0\,1\,2]$ (Slide 73)

  1. Step 1 (Origin): Select origin $O$ at $(0,0,0)$.
  2. Step 2 (Scaling): Reduce indices to a maximum value of 1 to keep directions inside the cell: $$\frac{1}{2}[0\,1\,2] = \left[0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\right]$$
  3. Step 3 (Locate Point): Locate from $(0,0,0)$ the coordinate: $x = 0$, $y = 1/2$, $z = 1$ (on the top back edge).
  4. Step 4 (Draw Vector): Draw vector from origin $O(0,0,0)$ to the arrow tip at $\left(0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\right)$.

Example 2: Draw Direction $[1\,1\,0]$ (Slide 73)

  1. Step 1 (Origin): Select origin $O$ at $(0,0,0)$.
  2. Step 2 (Scaling): Maximum index is already 1: $[1, 1, 0]$.
  3. Step 3 (Locate Point): Move $+1$ along $x$, $+1$ along $y$, and $0$ along $z \implies (1, 1, 0)$.
  4. Step 4 (Draw Vector): Vector extends from $O(0,0,0)$ across the base face diagonal to the opposite bottom corner $(1, 1, 0)$.

Example 3: Draw Direction $[2\,2\,1]$ (Slide 76)

  1. Step 1 (Origin): Choose coordinate origin $O(0,0,0)$ (all indices are positive).
  2. Step 2 (Scaling): The maximum index is 2. Divide the vector components by 2: $$\frac{1}{2}[2\,2\,1] = \left[1, 1, \frac{1}{2}\right]$$
  3. Step 3 (Locate Point): Advance $+1$ along $x$ (front edge), $+1$ along $y$ (right edge), and $+1/2$ along $z$ (halfway up the right-front vertical corner). The target coordinate is $\left(1, 1, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.
  4. Step 4 (Draw Vector): Draw a straight arrow from origin $O(0,0,0)$ directly to the coordinate tip at $\left(1, 1, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.

3. Families of Directions $\langle u\,v\,w \rangle$ & Angle Formula

Due to the rotational and reflectional symmetry of crystalline lattices, different direction vectors can possess identical linear atomic spacing and density. Such equivalent directions group into a family of directions, denoted by angle brackets:

$$\mathbf{\langle u\,v\,w \rangle} = \text{Set of all crystallographically equivalent directions}$$
Notation for a crystallographic direction family (Session 3, Slide 77).

Standard Families in Cubic Crystals

  • $\mathbf{\langle 100 \rangle}$ (Cube Axes): Contains 6 directions along the edges of the cube: $$\langle 100 \rangle = \{[100], [\bar{1}00], [010], [0\bar{1}0], [001], [00\bar{1}]\}$$
  • $\mathbf{\langle 110 \rangle}$ (Face Diagonals): Contains 12 directions along face diagonals: $$\langle 110 \rangle = \{[110], [\bar{1}10], [1\bar{1}0], [\bar{1}\bar{1}0], [101], [\bar{1}01], [10\bar{1}], [\bar{1}0\bar{1}], [011], [0\bar{1}1], [01\bar{1}], [0\bar{1}\bar{1}]\}$$
  • $\mathbf{\langle 111 \rangle}$ (Body Diagonals): Contains 8 directions along the main body diagonals: $$\langle 111 \rangle = \{[111], [\bar{1}11], [1\bar{1}1], [11\bar{1}], [\bar{1}\bar{1}1], [\bar{1}1\bar{1}], [1\bar{1}\bar{1}], [\bar{1}\bar{1}\bar{1}]\}$$

Angle Between Two Directions in Cubic Lattices

The angle $\theta$ between two directions $[u_1\,v_1\,w_1]$ and $[u_2\,v_2\,w_2]$ in a cubic crystal is calculated via the scalar product of their unit vectors:

$$\mathbf{\cos \theta = \frac{u_1 u_2 + v_1 v_2 + w_1 w_2}{\sqrt{u_1^2 + v_1^2 + w_1^2} \cdot \sqrt{u_2^2 + v_2^2 + w_2^2}}}$$
Formula for the angle between crystallographic directions in cubic systems.
📐 Worked Example: Angle Between [110] and [111] in Cubic System
$$\cos \theta = \frac{(1)(1) + (1)(1) + (0)(1)}{\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2} \cdot \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2}} = \frac{1 + 1 + 0}{\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{3}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 0.8165$$ $$\theta = \arccos(0.8165) \approx \mathbf{35.26^\circ}$$

4. Directions in the Hexagonal Cell (Miller-Bravais 4-Index System)

In hexagonal crystals, 3-index notation $[u'\,v'\,w']$ obscures the underlying 6-fold rotational symmetry (crystallographically equivalent directions would have completely different numbers). To preserve hexagonal symmetry, the Miller-Bravais 4-index system $[u\,v\,t\,w]$ uses four axes:

  • Three coplanar axes in the basal plane: $a_1, a_2, a_3$ separated by $120^\circ$ angles.
  • One vertical axis: $c$ perpendicular to the basal plane.
  • Symmetry constraint: The three basal axes are linearly dependent: $$a_3 = -(a_1 + a_2) \implies \mathbf{t = -(u + v)}$$
Conversion Formulas from 3-Index $[u'\,v'\,w']$ to 4-Index $[u\,v\,t\,w]$

To convert from standard 3-axis vectors $[u'\,v'\,w']$ to 4-axis Miller-Bravais indices (Session 3, Slides 80–82):

$$u = \frac{1}{3}(2u' - v')$$ $$v = \frac{1}{3}(2v' - u')$$ $$t = -(u + v) = -\frac{1}{3}(u' + v')$$ $$w = w'$$

Planes in Crystalline Cells & Miller Indices

Derivation procedure $(hkl)$, origin shifts for negative indices, planar families $\{hkl\}$, hexagonal Miller-Bravais $(hkil)$, and interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$.

1. Miller Indices for Crystallographic Planes $(h\,k\,l)$

Crystallographic planes are designated by Miller indices $(h\,k\,l)$, defined as the reciprocal values of the fractional intersections that the plane makes with the crystallographic axes $a, b, c$ (Session 3, Slide 83).

Standard 5-Step Procedure to Determine Miller Indices $(h\,k\,l)$
Step 1
Choose Origin $O$: The plane must NOT pass through the origin. If the plane touches or passes through the origin $(0,0,0)$, choose a new origin at an adjacent corner of the unit cell!
Step 2
Determine Intercepts: Find the intersection points of the plane with the axes $x, y, z$ in terms of unit cell dimensions $a, b, c$. If the plane is parallel to an axis, its intercept is at infinity ($\infty$).
Step 3
Take Reciprocals: Invert the intercept values: $$h' = \frac{1}{x_0}, \quad k' = \frac{1}{y_0}, \quad l' = \frac{1}{z_0} \qquad \left(\text{Note: } \frac{1}{\infty} = 0\right)$$
Step 4
Clear Fractions to Smallest Integers: Multiply by the common denominator to obtain the smallest integer ratio $h, k, l$.
Step 5
Enclose in Parentheses: Write indices enclosed in parentheses without commas: $$\mathbf{(h\,k\,l)}$$ Negative intercepts are indicated with an overbar: $(\bar{h}\,k\,l)$.
📐 The Fundamental Orthogonality Rule in Cubic Crystals
In all cubic crystals, the direction vector with indices $[h\,k\,l]$ is strictly perpendicular (orthogonal) to the crystallographic plane possessing the identical Miller indices $(h\,k\,l)$: $$[100] \perp (100), \quad [110] \perp (110), \quad [111] \perp (111)$$ (Note: This simple property does NOT hold in non-cubic systems like tetragonal, monoclinic, or orthorhombic!).

Interplanar Spacing ($d_{hkl}$) & Bragg's Law

The perpendicular distance between adjacent, parallel planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$ is the interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$. It is a fundamental parameter in X-ray diffraction (XRD) governed by Bragg's law:

$$\lambda = 2\,d_{hkl}\sin \theta \qquad \text{(Bragg's Law of X-Ray Diffraction)}$$
Relationship between X-ray wavelength $\lambda$, diffraction angle $\theta$, and plane spacing $d_{hkl}$ (Session 3, Slide 93).
Formulas for Interplanar Spacing $d_{hkl}$ Across Crystal Systems
  • Cubic Crystals ($a = b = c$): $$\mathbf{d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}}}$$
  • Tetragonal Crystals ($a = b \neq c$): $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \frac{h^2 + k^2}{a^2} + \frac{l^2}{c^2}}$$
  • Orthorhombic Crystals ($a \neq b \neq c$): $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \frac{h^2}{a^2} + \frac{k^2}{b^2} + \frac{l^2}{c^2}}$$
  • Hexagonal Crystals ($a = b \neq c, \alpha = \beta = 90^\circ, \gamma = 120^\circ$): $$\mathbf{\frac{1}{d_{hkil}^2} = \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{h^2 + hk + k^2}{a^2}\right) + \frac{l^2}{c^2}}$$

Calculation of Atomic, Linear, and Planar Density

Theoretical volumetric density $\rho_v$, linear density $\rho_l$, planar density $\rho_p$, and atomic area fraction $f_A$ across cubic and hexagonal lattices.

1. Volumetric Density ($\rho_v$)

The theoretical mass density of a perfect crystalline material is calculated directly from its crystal structure, atomic mass, and unit cell geometry. It represents the ratio of the total mass contained within the unit cell to the volume of that unit cell:

$$\rho_v = \frac{\text{Mass of Unit Cell}}{\text{Volume of Unit Cell}} = \frac{n \cdot M}{V_C \cdot N_A}$$
The fundamental equation of theoretical crystal density (Session 4, Slides 3–5).
Variable Physical Meaning Standard Units Evaluation & Crystal Specifics
$n$ Number of equivalent atoms per unit cell $\text{atoms / cell}$ $\text{SC} = 1, \quad \text{BCC} = 2, \quad \text{FCC} = 4, \quad \text{HCP} = 6$
$M$ Atomic weight (molar mass) $\text{g / mol}$ (or $\text{kg / mol}$) Standard IUPAC relative atomic mass of the element
$V_C$ Total geometric volume of the unit cell $\text{cm}^3$ (or $\text{m}^3$) $\text{Cubic: } a^3; \quad \text{FCC: } 16\sqrt{2}R^3; \quad \text{BCC: } \frac{64}{3\sqrt{3}}R^3; \quad \text{HCP: } 3\sqrt{2}a^3 = 24\sqrt{2}R^3$
$N_A$ Avogadro's constant $\text{atoms / mol}$ $6.02214 \times 10^{23} \approx 6.023 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms/mol}$
Lecture Example 8.1: Theoretical Volumetric Density of Pure Copper (Cu, FCC)

Given Data (Session 4, Slide 3):

  • Crystal Structure: Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) $\implies n = 8 \times \left(\frac{1}{8}\right) + 6 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \mathbf{4\text{ atoms/cell}}$.
  • Atomic Weight: $M(\text{Cu}) = 63.54\text{ g/mol}$.
  • Atomic Radius: $R = 1.28\text{ \AA} = 1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}$.
Step 1
Calculate the cubic lattice parameter $a$:
In FCC, hard spheres touch along face diagonals $[110]$: $$4R = \sqrt{2}a \implies a = 2\sqrt{2}R = 2\sqrt{2}(1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}) \approx \mathbf{3.620 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}} = 0.3620\text{ nm}$$
Step 2
Calculate the unit cell volume $V_C$: $$V_C = a^3 = (2\sqrt{2}R)^3 = 16\sqrt{2}R^3 = 16\sqrt{2}(1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm})^3 \approx \mathbf{4.745 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3}$$
Step 3
Substitute into the theoretical density formula: $$\rho_v = \frac{n \cdot M}{V_C \cdot N_A} = \frac{4\text{ atoms} \times 63.54\text{ g/mol}}{(4.745 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3) \times (6.023 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms/mol})} = \mathbf{8.89\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Comparison with experimental literature: Pure elemental copper has an experimentally measured density of $\rho_{\text{exp}} = 8.94\text{ g/cm}^3$. The minor $0.5\%$ difference arises from room-temperature thermal expansion and trace lattice point vacancies present in real macroscopic specimens.

Volumetric Atomic Number Density ($\rho_{v,\text{at}}$)

In addition to mass density, solid-state physics and nuclear engineering frequently require the volumetric atomic number density (the number of atoms per unit volume):

$$\rho_{v,\text{at}} = \frac{n}{V_C} = \frac{\rho_v \cdot N_A}{M}$$ $$\text{For Cu (FCC): } \rho_{v,\text{at}} = \frac{4\text{ atoms}}{4.745 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3} \approx \mathbf{8.43 \times 10^{22}\text{ atoms/cm}^3} = 8.43 \times 10^{28}\text{ atoms/m}^3$$

2. Linear Density ($\rho_l$)

The linear density ($\rho_l$ or $LD$) along a specific crystallographic direction $[uvw]$ measures how tightly atoms are packed along that line. It is defined as the number of atomic diameters (or equivalent full atom lengths) intersected by the direction vector divided by the length of the vector inside the unit cell:

$$\rho_l = \frac{\text{Number of Diameters / Centers Intersected by Line Vector}}{\text{Length of the Direction Vector inside Unit Cell}} = \frac{N_{[uvw]}}{L_{[uvw]}}$$
Linear density formula (Session 4, Slides 6–7). Units: $\text{atoms/cm}$, $\text{atoms/nm}$, or $\text{atoms/m}$.

Case A: Linear Density along $[100]$ in FCC (Slide 7)

  • Vector Length: $L_{[100]} = a = 2\sqrt{2}R \approx 3.62 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}$.
  • Intersected Atoms: Direction passes through two corner atoms. Each corner atom contributes its radius $R$ inside the cell segment, giving half a diameter at each end: $$N_{[100]} = 2 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \mathbf{1\text{ atom}}$$
  • Linear Density Calculation: $$\rho_l[100] = \frac{1\text{ atom}}{a} = \frac{1}{3.62 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{2.76 \times 10^7\text{ atoms/cm}} = 2.76 \times 10^9\text{ m}^{-1}$$

Case B: Linear Density along $[110]$ in FCC (Close-Packed!)

  • Vector Length: $L_{[110]} = \sqrt{2}a = 4R = 4(1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}) = 5.12 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}$.
  • Intersected Atoms: Line cuts through 2 corners ($2 \times \frac{1}{2} = 1$) plus 1 full face-centered atom at the midpoint: $$N_{[110]} = 2 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + 1 = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$$
  • Linear Density Calculation: $$\rho_l[110] = \frac{2\text{ atoms}}{4R} = \frac{1}{2R} = \frac{1}{2(1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm})} = \mathbf{3.91 \times 10^7\text{ atoms/cm}}$$
  • Significance: This is the maximum theoretical linear density in FCC ($1/(2R)$), proving that $\langle 110 \rangle$ is the close-packed slip direction!

Case C: Linear Density along $[111]$ in BCC (Close-Packed Direction!)

In Body-Centered Cubic crystals, hard spheres touch along the body diagonal $[111]$:

  • Vector Length: $L_{[111]} = \sqrt{3}a = 4R$.
  • Intersected Atoms: 2 corner atoms $\times \frac{1}{2} + 1\text{ body center} = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$.
  • Linear Density: $\rho_l[111] = \frac{2\text{ atoms}}{4R} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2R}}$ (identical maximum linear packing to FCC $[110]$!).

3. Planar Density ($\rho_p$) and Atomic Area Fraction ($f_A$)

The planar density ($\rho_p$ or $PD$) measures the number of atoms centered per unit area on a given crystallographic plane $(hkl)$. In materials science, slip occurs preferentially on planes with the highest planar density:

$$\rho_p = \frac{\text{Number of Atoms Centered on Plane inside Unit Cell}}{\text{Total Area of the Plane inside Unit Cell}} = \frac{N_{(hkl)}}{A_{(hkl)}}$$
Planar density formula (Session 4, Slides 8–10). Units: $\text{atoms/cm}^2$, $\text{atoms/nm}^2$, or $\text{atoms/m}^2$.

Plane $(100)$ in FCC (Square Face Plane, Slide 9)

  • Plane Area: $A_{(100)} = a^2 = (2\sqrt{2}R)^2 = 8R^2 = (3.62 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm})^2 \approx 1.310 \times 10^{-15}\text{ cm}^2$.
  • Centered Atoms: 4 corner atoms each contributing $1/4$ of their circular area inside the square, plus 1 complete face-centered atom: $$N_{(100)} = 4 \times \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + 1 = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$$
  • Planar Density: $$\rho_p(100) = \frac{2\text{ atoms}}{a^2} = \frac{2}{1.310 \times 10^{-15}\text{ cm}^2} = \mathbf{1.526 \times 10^{15}\text{ atoms/cm}^2} = 1.526 \times 10^{19}\text{ m}^{-2}$$
  • Atomic Area Fraction ($f_A$): $$f_A(100) = \frac{N_{(100)} \cdot \pi R^2}{A_{(100)}} = \frac{2 \pi R^2}{8 R^2} = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx \mathbf{78.54\%}$$

Plane $(111)$ in FCC (Close-Packed Octahedral Plane!)

  • Plane Geometry: Equilateral triangle bounded by face diagonals of length $s = \sqrt{2}a = 4R$.
  • Triangle Area: $$A_{(111)} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(4R)^2 = \mathbf{4\sqrt{3}R^2} \approx 6.928\,R^2$$ $$\text{For Cu: } A_{(111)} = 4\sqrt{3}(1.28 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm})^2 \approx 1.135 \times 10^{-15}\text{ cm}^2$$
  • Centered Atoms inside Triangle: $$3\text{ corners} \times \left(\frac{60^\circ}{360^\circ}\right) + 3\text{ face centers} \times \left(\frac{180^\circ}{360^\circ}\right) = 3 \times \frac{1}{6} + 3 \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2} = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$$
  • Planar Density: $$\rho_p(111) = \frac{2}{4\sqrt{3}R^2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}R^2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}(1.28 \times 10^{-8})^2} = \mathbf{1.762 \times 10^{15}\text{ atoms/cm}^2}$$
  • Atomic Area Fraction ($f_A$): $$f_A(111) = \frac{2 \pi R^2}{4\sqrt{3}R^2} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \approx \mathbf{90.69\%}$$ Matches the maximum mathematical 2D packing efficiency!

Crystalline Defects: Point Defects

Thermodynamic equilibrium of vacancies ($\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$), self-interstitials, Schottky and Frenkel defect pairs, and aliovalent electroneutrality.

1. Perfect and Real Crystals

An idealized, mathematically perfect crystal possesses all atoms in their exact equilibrium lattice coordinates, with perfectly identical interplanar spacings $d_{hkl}$ and zero missing or extra atoms. According to the Third Law of Thermodynamics, a perfect crystal can only exist at absolute zero:

$$T = 0\text{ K} \implies S = 0 \implies \text{Zero thermal vibrations, zero vacancies}$$
Ideal perfection exists only at absolute zero (Session 4, Slide 21).

In all real engineering materials at temperatures $T > 0\text{ K}$, atoms undergo continuous thermal vibration about their equilibrium positions. Kinetic fluctuations cause some atoms to break bonds and migrate, leaving empty sites (vacancies) or squeezing into non-lattice positions.

🔎 Official Definition: Crystalline Defect
"A crystalline defect is a localized disruption in the perfect, periodic three-dimensional arrangement of atoms, ions, or molecules in a crystalline material" (Session 4, Slide 21).

2. Thermodynamics of Defect Formation: Why Vacancies Must Exist

Why do crystals naturally form vacancies even though breaking chemical bonds costs positive enthalpy? The answer lies in the Second Law of Thermodynamics: a system at constant temperature and pressure always seeks to minimize its Gibbs free energy ($G = H - TS$) (Session 4, Slide 22):

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S$$
Gibbs free energy change upon introducing $n_v$ vacancies into a crystal of $N$ atomic sites.
  • Enthalpic Cost ($\Delta H > 0$): Removing an atom from a regular lattice site requires breaking its nearest-neighbor bonds and creates localized elastic strain around the empty void. If $\Delta H_v$ is the formation enthalpy per vacancy: $$\Delta H = n_v \cdot \Delta H_v \quad (> 0)$$
  • Entropy Gain ($\Delta S = \Delta S_{\text{vibr}} + \Delta S_{\text{conf}} > 0$): Introducing $n_v$ identical vacancies into $N$ available crystal sites creates an astronomical number of possible spatial configurations $W$: $$W = \frac{N!}{n_v! \, (N - n_v)!}$$ According to Boltzmann's entropy formula, the configurational entropy is: $$S_{\text{conf}} = k_B \ln W = k_B \ln \left[ \frac{N!}{n_v! \, (N - n_v)!} \right]$$
  • Using Stirling's Approximation ($\ln x! \approx x\ln x - x$ for large $x$): $$\Delta S_{\text{conf}} \approx -k_B \left[ n_v \ln \left(\frac{n_v}{N}\right) + (N - n_v) \ln \left(\frac{N - n_v}{N}\right) \right]$$
Derivation 9.1: Equilibrium Vacancy Concentration ($n_v / N$)
Step 1
Express the total Gibbs free energy change: $$\Delta G(n_v) = n_v \Delta H_v - T \Delta S_{\text{conf}} = n_v \Delta H_v + k_B T \left[ n_v \ln\left(\frac{n_v}{N}\right) + (N - n_v)\ln\left(1 - \frac{n_v}{N}\right) \right]$$
Step 2
Differentiate with respect to $n_v$ and set to zero for thermodynamic equilibrium: $$\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G}{\partial n_v}\right)_{T,P} = \Delta H_v + k_B T \ln\left(\frac{n_v}{N - n_v}\right) = 0$$ Because $n_v \ll N$ (vacancies occupy fewer than 1 in $10^4$ sites even near the melting point), $N - n_v \approx N$: $$\Delta H_v + k_B T \ln\left(\frac{n_v}{N}\right) = 0 \implies \ln\left(\frac{n_v}{N}\right) = -\frac{\Delta H_v}{k_B T}$$
Step 3
The Equilibrium Vacancy Equation (Session 4, Slide 24): $$\mathbf{\frac{n_v}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_v}{k_B T}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{Q_v}{R T}\right)}$$

Where $N$ is the number of regular lattice points per unit volume: $$N = \frac{\rho_v \cdot N_A}{M}$$ $k_B = 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant, $R = 8.314\text{ J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$ is the universal gas constant, and $Q_v = N_A \Delta H_v$ is the vacancy activation energy in $\text{J/mol}$ (or $\text{eV/atom}$).

🔥 Temperature Sensitivity of Vacancies
Because temperature $T$ appears in the denominator of the negative exponential, vacancy concentration increases exponentially with absolute temperature: $$\uparrow T \implies \uparrow \text{thermal kinetic energy} \implies \uparrow n_v$$ Near the absolute melting point ($T \approx T_m$), the vacancy fraction reaches its practical maximum of approximately $\frac{n_v}{N} \approx 10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ (1 vacancy per 1,000–10,000 atomic sites).

3. Vacancies vs. Self-Interstitials in Pure Metals

○ Vacancies (Slide 24)

An unoccupied lattice site where an atom is missing from the normal crystalline framework.

  • Surrounding atoms relax slightly inward toward the void, creating an isotropic compressive strain field.
  • Mechanisms of Formation:
    • Thermal equilibrium excitation ($\uparrow T \implies \uparrow n_v$).
    • Plastic deformation (jog dragging on intersecting dislocations).
    • High-energy neutron/ion particle irradiation (collisional displacement cascades).
    • Rapid quenching from elevated temperatures (traps excess non-equilibrium vacancies).
  • Role: Enables solid-state atomic diffusion, creep, and sintering.

● Self-Interstitials / Interstitialcy (Slide 24)

A host atom crowded into an interstitial void between regular lattice sites.

  • Because metallic atoms have large atomic radii ($R$) compared to interstitial voids ($r_{\text{oct}} \approx 0.414\,R$, $r_{\text{tet}} \approx 0.225\,R$), inserting a host atom produces intense localized lattice distortion and tensile strain.
  • Formation Enthalpy: $\Delta H_i \approx 3 - 5\times \Delta H_v$ (formation energy is substantially higher).
  • Equilibrium Concentration: Negligibly small under normal thermodynamic conditions ($n_i / N \ll 10^{-15}$). Formed primarily under high-energy nuclear radiation bombardment.

4. Point Defects in Ionic Crystals: Schottky and Frenkel Defect Pairs

In ionic ceramics (e.g. $\text{NaCl}, \text{MgO}, \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{ZrO}_2$), point defects cannot be created arbitrarily. The crystal must maintain strict macroscopic electroneutrality (the total positive cation charges must exactly equal the total negative anion charges):

A. Schottky Defect Pair (Slide 25)

A stoichiometric pair of oppositely charged vacancies: exactly one cation vacancy and one anion vacancy simultaneously created in a 1:1 ionic compound (e.g. $\text{Na}^+$ vacancy and $\text{Cl}^-$ vacancy).

$$n_S = N \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_S}{2\,k_B T}\right)$$
The factor of 2 in the denominator reflects that 2 vacancies form per defect pair (Slide 25).
  • Preserves 1:1 stoichiometry and net electrical neutrality.
  • Density decreases slightly because mass is removed without changing overall crystal dimensions.
  • Dominates in compounds with similar-sized cations and anions (e.g. $\text{NaCl}, \text{KCl}, \text{CsCl}, \text{MgO}$).

B. Frenkel Defect Pair (Slide 25)

A combination of one cation vacancy and one cation interstitial, formed when a cation leaves its normal lattice site and squeezes into a nearby interstitial void.

$$n_F = \sqrt{N\,N_i} \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_F}{2\,k_B T}\right)$$
$N$ = regular lattice positions, $N_i$ = available interstitial sites (Slide 25).
  • Cation moves into void; anion vacancies are NOT formed.
  • Bulk density remains completely unchanged because no atoms enter or leave the crystal.
  • Dominates when the cation is significantly smaller than the anion ($r_{\text{cation}} \ll r_{\text{anion}}$), allowing it to fit into interstitial voids with low strain (e.g. $\text{Ag}^+$ in $\text{AgCl}, \text{AgBr}$).
⚖️ Energetic Competition: The Lowest $\Delta H$ Governs
In any given ionic crystal, $\Delta H_S \neq \Delta H_F$. Because defect concentrations depend exponentially on formation enthalpy, the defect type with the lower formation energy ($\Delta H$) forms almost exclusively (Session 4, Slide 25).

Solid Solutions & Hume-Rothery Rules

Thermodynamic criteria for substitutional and interstitial solubility, defect generation by aliovalent doping in ceramics, and order-disorder superlattice transformations.

1. Fundamentals of Solid Solutions

When two or more distinct elemental or compound species are mixed in the liquid state and subsequently solidified, two thermodynamic outcomes are possible:

  1. Solid Solution (Homogeneous Single Phase): The solute atoms dissolve completely into the solvent crystal lattice, preserving the crystal structure of the host solvent without nucleating a second distinct phase.
  2. Two-Phase Mixture (Heterogeneous Multiphase): The solubility limit is exceeded; excess solute precipitates out to form a distinct second phase (or intermetallic compound) with its own unique crystal structure and chemical composition.
🔍 Official Definition: Solid Solution
"A solid solution is a homogeneous crystalline solid consisting of two or more elements dispersed as individual atoms or ions throughout a crystal lattice that maintains a single, uniform thermodynamic phase" (Session 4, Slide 29).

A. Substitutional Solid Solutions (Slide 29)

Solute atoms directly replace (substitute for) host solvent atoms on their regular crystallographic lattice sites.

  • The crystal structure of the solvent remains intact, although the unit cell parameter ($a$) expands or contracts according to Vegard's Law depending on whether the solute radius is larger or smaller than the solvent.
  • Classic Examples:
    • $\text{Cu-Ni}$ system (Monel alloys): Complete solid solubility across all compositions ($0 - 100\text{ at}\%$).
    • $\alpha$-Brass: Zinc atoms substituting into the FCC copper lattice up to $\approx 35\text{ wt}\%\,\text{Zn}$.

B. Interstitial Solid Solutions (Slide 29)

Solute atoms squeeze directly into the octahedral or tetrahedral interstitial voids between the host solvent atoms.

  • Radius Constraint: Solute atoms must possess extremely small atomic radii to fit inside interstitial voids without shattering the crystal lattice. Restricted almost exclusively to: $$\mathbf{\text{Hydrogen (H), Carbon (C), Nitrogen (N), Boron (B), Oxygen (O)}}$$
  • Classic Examples:
    • Carbon dissolved in $\gamma$-austenite (FCC iron): Maximum solubility of $2.14\text{ wt}\%\,\text{C}$ at $1148^\circ\text{C}$.
    • Carbon dissolved in $\alpha$-ferrite (BCC iron): Maximum solubility of only $0.022\text{ wt}\%\,\text{C}$ at $727^\circ\text{C}$ ($< 0.005\text{ wt}\%$ at room temperature).

2. Generation of Defects in Ionic Solid Solutions (Aliovalent Doping)

When forming solid solutions between ionic ceramics, substituting a cation of different valence (an aliovalent dopant) perturbs electrical neutrality. To maintain macroscopic charge balance, the ceramic lattice automatically generates point defects (cation vacancies, anion vacancies, or interstitials) (Session 4, Slide 30):

Case Study 10.1: Solid Solution of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ in $\text{MgO}$ (Slide 30)

Consider dissolving alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) into a pure magnesium oxide ($\text{MgO}$) crystal. In $\text{MgO}$, magnesium exists as divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) on octahedral FCC sublattice sites:

Step 1
Charge Mismatch Analysis:
Aluminum dissolves as trivalent cations ($\text{Al}^{3+}$), substituting directly onto divalent $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ lattice sites: $$\text{Each }\text{Al}^{3+}\text{ on a }\text{Mg}^{2+}\text{ site carries a net relative positive charge of }+1 \quad (\text{Kröger-Vink notation: }\text{Al}_{\text{Mg}}^\bullet)$$
Step 2
Electroneutrality Balancing:
To maintain electrical neutrality, for every two $\text{Al}^{3+}$ cations introduced ($+2$ excess positive charge), exactly three $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ cations must be removed. This leaves two filled aluminum sites and one vacant magnesium lattice site ($V_{\text{Mg}}''$, carrying a relative charge of $-2$): $$\mathbf{3\,\text{Mg}^{2+} \longleftrightarrow 2\,\text{Al}^{3+} + 1\,V_{\text{Mg}}'' \quad (\text{Magnesium Cation Vacancy})}$$
Step 3
Physical Significance:
The concentration of vacancies is directly dictated by chemical doping stoichiometry, entirely independent of temperature! These extrinsic vacancies drastically increase the ionic conductivity and solid-state diffusion rates of the ceramic.

3. The Hume-Rothery Rules for Solid Solubility (Slide 31)

Formulated by British metallurgist William Hume-Rothery in the 1930s, these empirical rules predict whether two elements will form an extensive or complete solid solution:

A. Rules for Substitutional Solid Solutions

Rule Thermodynamic Requirement Quantitative Criterion Physical Rationale
1. Crystal Structure Rule The crystal structure of solute and solvent must match (matching crystal structures) (possess the identical crystal structure). Both FCC, both BCC, or both HCP Complete $100\%$ miscibility is impossible if the pure components have different crystal symmetries (e.g. FCC Cu and BCC Fe can never be completely miscible).
2. Atomic Size Factor Rule Atomic radii of solute ($r_{\text{solute}}$) and solvent ($r_{\text{solvent}}$) must be very close. $$\Delta r = \frac{|r_{\text{solute}} - r_{\text{solvent}}|}{r_{\text{solvent}}} \times 100\% < \mathbf{15\%}$$ If $\Delta r < 15\%$, elastic strain energy in the host lattice is minimal, allowing extensive solubility. If $\Delta r > 15\%$, severe lattice strain limits solubility to strictly $< 1\text{ at}\%$. (For ceramics, the limit is $\Delta r < 30\%$).
3. Electronegativity Rule Solute and solvent must have similar electronegativities. $|\chi_{\text{solute}} - \chi_{\text{solvent}}| \approx 0$ If the electronegativity difference is large, atoms prefer ionic/covalent bonding over metallic solid solutions, leading to the precipitation of stable intermetallic compounds (e.g. $\text{Mg}_2\text{Si}$, $\text{CuAl}_2$).
4. Relative Valence Rule Solute and solvent should have the same chemical valence. $Z_{\text{solute}} = Z_{\text{solvent}}$ for complete miscibility A metal has a much higher tendency to dissolve another metal of higher valence than one of lower valence (higher valence increases electron-to-atom ratio $e/a$).

B. Rules for Interstitial Solid Solutions

  • Size Factor: Solute atomic radius must be much smaller than the solvent radius: $$r_{\text{solute}} \le \mathbf{0.59\,r_{\text{solvent}}}$$
  • Electronegativity: Solute and solvent must possess similar electronegativity to avoid forming covalent non-metallic ceramic networks.
⚠️ Golden Rule of Hume-Rothery Predictability (Session 4, Slide 31)
  • If all 4 conditions are satisfied: We have the potential for extensive or total solid solubility, but complete miscibility is not strictly guaranteed.
  • If one or more conditions are violated: Solid solubility is strictly partial, limited, or negligible ($< 1\%$).

4. Order-Disorder Phenomena in Solid Solutions (Slide 32)

In substitutional solid solutions with components possessing similar electronegativities, the spatial arrangement of solute and solvent atoms varies dramatically as a function of temperature:

Disordered State ($T > T_c$, Elevated Temperature)

Above a critical transition temperature $T_c$ (e.g. $T > 390^\circ\text{C}$ in the classic $\text{Cu-Au}$ system):

  • Thermal vibrational energy dominates ($T\Delta S_{\text{conf}} \gg \Delta H$).
  • Copper ($\text{Cu}$) and Gold ($\text{Au}$) atoms occupy the FCC lattice sites completely at random.
  • The probability of finding a $\text{Cu}$ or $\text{Au}$ atom on any given lattice site is simply equal to its macroscopic mole fraction.

Ordered Superlattice ($T < T_c$, Low Temperature)

When annealed below $T_c$ ($T < 390^\circ\text{C}$):

  • The enthalpy of unlike atomic bonding ($\text{Cu}-\text{Au}$) is more exothermic than like bonding ($\text{Cu}-\text{Cu}$ or $\text{Au}-\text{Au}$).
  • Atoms migrate into specific, dedicated sublattices, creating an ordered superlattice:
    • $\text{Cu}_3\text{Au}$ ($25\text{ at}\%\,\text{Au}$): $\text{Au}$ atoms sit strictly at the 8 cube corners ($8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1\text{ atom}$), while $\text{Cu}$ atoms sit exclusively at the 6 face centers ($6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 3\text{ atoms}$).
    • $\text{CuAu}$ ($50\text{ at}\%\,\text{Au}$): Alternating planes of pure $\text{Cu}$ and pure $\text{Au}$ along the $c$-axis, transforming the cubic symmetry into a slightly tetragonal L1$_0$ structure ($c/a \approx 0.93$).
  • Result: Extra "superlattice reflection peaks" appear in X-ray diffraction, electrical resistivity drops sharply, and yield strength increases.

Crystalline Defects: Linear Defects (Dislocations)

Taylor edge dislocations, Burgers screw dislocations, mixed loops, Burgers circuit closure, Frank's energy rule $E \propto |\vec{b}|^2$, and crystallographic slip systems.

1. The Dislocation Concept & The Theoretical Strength Paradox

In the 1920s, early solid-state physicists calculated the theoretical shear strength ($\tau_{\text{theor}}$) of a perfect crystal by computing the force required to shear one complete atomic plane simultaneously over another:

$$\tau_{\text{theor}} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi} \approx \frac{G}{10} \text{ to } \frac{G}{30}$$
Theoretical shear strength of an ideal dislocation-free crystal ($G$ = shear modulus).

However, experimental tensile and shear tests on real metal single crystals (e.g. pure copper, aluminum, iron) revealed actual yield strengths that were three to five orders of magnitude lower:

$$\tau_{\text{exp}} \approx 10^{-3}\,G \text{ to } 10^{-5}\,G \ll \tau_{\text{theor}}$$
The classical plasticity paradox: real metals are thousands of times softer than theory predicts (Session 4, Slide 35).
💡 The Resolution (Orowan, Polanyi, and Taylor, 1934)
Plastic deformation does not occur by the simultaneous shearing of billions of chemical bonds across an entire crystalline plane. Instead, it occurs through the sequential, bond-by-bond propagation of one-dimensional line defects (line imperfections) called dislocations (Session 4, Slide 35):
  • The Carpet Analogy: Moving a heavy 5-ton carpet across the floor by pulling it all at once requires immense force. But making a small wrinkle at one edge and pushing that wrinkle across the room requires minimal effort, achieving the exact same net displacement!
  • The Caterpillar Analogy: A caterpillar does not slide its entire body forward at once; it arches a single hump forward, propagating localized movement from tail to head.

Dislocation Formation & Multiplication Mechanisms (Slide 35)

  • Solidification & Crystal Growth: Impingement of growing dendrites and thermal gradients naturally generate dislocation networks ($10^5 - 10^6\text{ cm/cm}^3$ in annealed crystals).
  • Plastic / Permanent Deformation: Mechanical shear activates Frank-Read sources, multiplying dislocation densities up to $10^{11} - 10^{12}\text{ cm/cm}^3$ in heavily cold-worked metals (the physical origin of work hardening).
  • Condensation of Vacancies: At elevated temperatures, supersaturated vacancies aggregate into flat two-dimensional discs that collapse into dislocation loops.
  • Atomic Mismatch in Solid Solutions: Localized coherency strains between solute and solvent atoms generate localized line defects.

2. Edge Dislocations (Taylor Dislocation)

An edge dislocation is formed by inserting an extra half-plane of atoms into an otherwise perfect crystal lattice (Session 4, Slides 37–38):

Geometric Anatomy of an Edge Dislocation

  • Dislocation Line ($\vec{t}$): The bottom linear terminating edge of the inserted extra half-plane. Denoted by the standard symbol $\mathbf{\bot}$ (positive edge, extra half-plane above slip plane) or $\mathbf{\top}$ (negative edge, extra half-plane below slip plane).
  • Slip Plane: The crystallographic plane defined simultaneously by the dislocation line $\vec{t}$ and the Burgers vector $\vec{b}$.
  • Fundamental Orientation: $$\mathbf{\vec{b} \perp \vec{t} \quad (\text{Burgers vector is strictly PERPENDICULAR to the dislocation line})}$$
  • Dislocation Motion under Applied Shear ($\tau$): $$\mathbf{\text{Slip Direction } \parallel \vec{b} \parallel \text{Direction of Dislocation Motion}}$$

Internal Stress Field of an Edge Dislocation

  • Compressive Zone (Above Slip Plane): The atoms above the slip plane are squeezed together to accommodate the extra half-plane $\implies$ hydrostatic compressive stress ($\sigma < 0$). Solute atoms smaller than the host (e.g. carbon in BCC iron) migrate here to relieve compressive strain.
  • Tensile Zone (Below Slip Plane): The lattice below the slip plane is dilated and pulled apart $\implies$ hydrostatic tensile stress ($\sigma > 0$). Larger substitutional solute atoms segregate here, forming Cottrell atmospheres that pin dislocations.
Procedure: The Burgers Circuit for an Edge Dislocation (Slide 37)

The Burgers vector ($\vec{b}$) defines the magnitude and crystallographic direction of the lattice distortion created by the dislocation:

  1. Step 1 (Construct Closed Circuit in Perfect Crystal): In a defect-free lattice, traverse an equal number of interatomic vector steps clockwise: $m$ steps right, $n$ steps down, $m$ steps left, $n$ steps up. The circuit closes perfectly ($S \equiv F$).
  2. Step 2 (Construct Identical Circuit around Dislocation): Enclose the edge dislocation core with the exact same step sequence: $m$ steps right, $n$ steps down, $m$ steps left, $n$ steps up.
  3. Step 3 (Closure Failure Vector): Because of the extra half-plane, the circuit fails to close! The closure failure vector pointing from the finish point ($F$) to the start point ($S$) defines the Burgers vector $\vec{b}$: $$\vec{b} = \vec{F} - \vec{S}$$

3. Screw Dislocations (Burgers Dislocation)

A screw dislocation is produced by cutting a crystal partially through and displacing the two halves past each other parallel to the cut by one atomic spacing (Session 4, Slide 40):

Geometric Anatomy of a Screw Dislocation

  • The Helical Ramp: Parallel atomic planes are transformed into a continuous, single helical spiral ramp (like a spiral parking garage) winding around the dislocation line.
  • Fundamental Orientation: $$\mathbf{\vec{b} \parallel \vec{t} \quad (\text{Burgers vector is strictly PARALLEL to the dislocation line})}$$
  • Dislocation Motion under Applied Shear ($\tau$): $$\mathbf{\vec{b} \perp \text{Direction of Dislocation Motion}}$$ While the slip direction remains parallel to $\vec{b}$, the dislocation line itself glides perpendicularly to $\vec{b}$!

Internal Stress Field of a Screw Dislocation

  • Pure Shear Distortion: The atomic distortion around a screw dislocation is completely torsional and cylindrical: $$\tau_{r\theta} = \frac{G\,|\vec{b}|}{2\pi r}$$
  • Zero Hydrostatic Stress: Because there is no missing or extra half-plane, a screw dislocation produces zero volumetric dilatation ($\Delta V = 0$). There are no compressive or tensile regions!
  • Cross-Slip Capability: Because $\vec{b} \parallel \vec{t}$, a screw dislocation does not have a single unique slip plane; it can cross-slip freely onto any intersecting plane that contains $\vec{b}$.

4. Mixed Dislocations & The Invariance of the Burgers Vector (Slide 41)

Real dislocations in engineering materials are rarely pure edge or pure screw. Most dislocations form closed loops or curved line segments possessing mixed character:

📏 The Principle of Burgers Vector Invariance
"The Burgers vector $\vec{b}$ remains strictly invariant (constant in both magnitude and direction) along the entire length of any continuous dislocation line or loop, regardless of how the dislocation curves" (Session 4, Slide 41).

Along a curved dislocation loop inside a crystal:

  • Where the line vector is perpendicular to $\vec{b}$ ($\vec{t} \perp \vec{b}$), the dislocation exhibits pure edge character.
  • Where the line vector is parallel to $\vec{b}$ ($\vec{t} \parallel \vec{b}$), the dislocation exhibits pure screw character.
  • At any intermediate orientation where the angle between $\vec{t}$ and $\vec{b}$ is $\theta$ ($0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ$), the dislocation is mixed, with edge component $b_e = |\vec{b}|\sin\theta$ and screw component $b_s = |\vec{b}|\cos\theta$.

5. Energetics of Dislocations: Frank's Rule (Slide 45)

Creating a dislocation stores elastic strain energy in the distorted atomic bonds around the dislocation core. According to Frank's Rule, the elastic strain energy per unit length of a dislocation is proportional to the square of its Burgers vector:

$$E_{\text{disl}} \propto |\vec{b}|^2 \implies E_{\text{disl}} \approx \alpha\,G\,|\vec{b}|^2$$
Frank's Rule ($\alpha \approx 0.5 - 1.0$, $G$ = shear modulus, $|\vec{b}|$ = magnitude of Burgers vector) (Session 4, Slide 45).
Physical Proof: Why Dislocations Glide Exclusively in Close-Packed Directions

Consider an FCC crystal with lattice parameter $a = 2\sqrt{2}R$ (Session 4, Slide 45):

Case 1
Dislocation along Close-Packed Direction $[110]$:
The shortest repeat unit connects nearest-neighbor atoms: $$\vec{b}_1 = \frac{a}{2}[110] \implies |\vec{b}_1| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}R}{\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{2R}$$ Energy per unit length: $$E_1 \propto |\vec{b}_1|^2 = (2R)^2 = \mathbf{4R^2}$$
Case 2
Dislocation along Non-Close-Packed Direction $[100]$:
The unit translation vector spans the full unit cell edge: $$\vec{b}_2 = a[100] \implies |\vec{b}_2| = a = 2\sqrt{2}R$$ Energy per unit length: $$E_2 \propto |\vec{b}_2|^2 = (2\sqrt{2}R)^2 = \mathbf{8R^2}$$
Conclusion
$$\frac{E_2}{E_1} = \frac{8R^2}{4R^2} = \mathbf{2}$$ Dislocations in non-close-packed directions require twice as much energy to create and propagate! Nature always minimizes strain energy: dislocations form with the smallest possible Burgers vector, which corresponds strictly to close-packed directions with the highest linear atomic density ($\rho_l$).

6. Slip Planes, Slip Directions, and Slip Systems

Dislocation glide is not isotropic; it occurs along specific crystallographic planes and directions that minimize resistance to motion:

$$\mathbf{\text{Slip System}} = \mathbf{\text{Slip Plane (Highest Planar Density } \rho_p)} + \mathbf{\text{Slip Direction (Highest Linear Density } \rho_l)}$$

Crystalline Defects: Planar Defects

Two-dimensional interfaces: grain boundaries, the Hall-Petch grain refinement equation, deformation and annealing twin boundaries, stacking faults, and surface energetics.

1. Fundamentals of Planar (Interfacial) Defects

Planar defects are two-dimensional geometrical boundaries that separate a crystalline material into distinct regions, each possessing the exact same crystal structure but differing in crystallographic orientation, stacking sequence, or spatial phase continuity:

🗂️ Official Definition: Planar Defect
"Planar defects are boundaries or planes that separate a material into regions, each having the same crystal structure but different spatial orientation" (Session 4, Slide 48).

Planar defects divide into four fundamental engineering classes (Session 4, Slide 48):

  1. Grain Boundaries: Interfaces separating adjacent crystals (grains) of arbitrary misorientation.
  2. Twin Boundaries: Highly symmetrical boundaries across which the crystal lattice forms a precise mirror-image reflection.
  3. Stacking Faults: Localized 1–2 atomic layer interruptions in the periodic close-packed stacking sequence.
  4. External Surfaces: The macroscopic terminating boundaries of the solid where coordination is incomplete.

2. Grain Boundaries & The Hall-Petch Strengthening Law

Almost all engineering metals, structural alloys, and technical ceramics are polycrystalline, consisting of billions of microscopic individual single-crystal grains (Session 4, Slides 49–51):

A. Solidification & Grain Nucleation

  • During solidification from the molten state, atomic kinetic energy decreases below the melting temperature ($T < T_m$).
  • Stable crystalline nuclei spontaneously precipitate at random locations throughout the melt with completely random crystallographic orientations.
  • These nuclei grow three-dimensionally into grains until their advancing crystallization fronts impinge upon one another, forming an interlocking mosaic: a polycrystal.

B. Physical Properties of Grain Boundaries (Slide 51)

  • Narrow Interfacial Width: The boundary transition zone is extremely narrow, spanning only 2 to 5 interatomic distances ($\approx 0.5 - 1.5\text{ nm}$).
  • High Interfacial Energy State: Because atoms across the boundary are misaligned and cannot achieve ideal interatomic spacing, atomic packing density is significantly lower than within the grain interior. This creates high grain boundary energy: $$\gamma_{\text{gb}} \approx 0.5 - 1.0\text{ J/m}^2$$
  • High-Speed Atomic Diffusion Highway: Open atomic pathways make grain boundaries preferred conduits for rapid solid-state diffusion ($D_{\text{gb}} \gg D_{\text{lattice}}$), recrystallization nucleation, solute segregation, and intergranular chemical corrosion.

C. Dislocation Blocking & The Hall-Petch Law (Slide 51)

Because adjacent grains possess different crystallographic orientations, a slip plane in Grain A does not align with a slip plane in Grain B. Furthermore, the disordered boundary core lacks continuous atomic slip paths. As a result:

🚧 Grain Boundaries as Impenetrable Obstacles to Slip
When a dislocation glides through a grain, it is arrested at the grain boundary. Trapped dislocations pile up like traffic behind a roadblock. The stress concentration at the head of the pile-up must build up until it can nucleate or activate a new dislocation source in the adjacent grain (Session 4, Slide 51).
$$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y \cdot d^{-1/2} \qquad \text{(The Hall-Petch Equation)}$$
The relationship between yield strength $\sigma_y$ and mean grain diameter $d$ (Session 4, Slide 51).
Parameter Physical Meaning Units Engineering Significance
$\sigma_y$ Tensile yield strength $\text{MPa}$ The macroscopic stress required to initiate irreversible plastic deformation.
$\sigma_0$ Friction stress (lattice resistance) $\text{MPa}$ Intrinsic resistance of the crystal lattice to dislocation motion in a single crystal of infinite size.
$k_y$ Hall-Petch strengthening coefficient $\text{MPa}\cdot\mu\text{m}^{1/2}$ Measures the relative effectiveness of the grain boundaries at locking and pinning slip.
$d$ Mean grain diameter $\mu\text{m}$ (or $\text{mm}$) Microstructural grain size determined by optical metallography (ASTM E112).
⭐ The Unique Advantage of Grain Refinement
In materials engineering, almost every strengthening mechanism (e.g. solid solution strengthening, work hardening, precipitation hardening) increases strength at the expense of reducing ductility and toughness. Grain boundary refinement ($d \downarrow$) is the ONLY strengthening mechanism that simultaneously increases yield strength AND fracture toughness!

3. Twin Boundaries (Slides 52–53)

A twin boundary is a special low-energy grain boundary that separates two crystal domains possessing a specific mirror-image symmetrical misorientation across a shared twinning plane:

A. Deformation (Mechanical) Twins

Formed under intense mechanical shear stress at high deformation rates or cryogenic temperatures.

  • Atoms on successive atomic planes are sheared by a uniform fraction of a lattice vector, creating an abrupt change in crystal orientation.
  • Prevalence: Vital in Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) and Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) metals (e.g. $\alpha$-Fe, Ti, Mg, Zn) where the limited number of active slip systems is insufficient to accommodate high strain rates.

B. Annealing (Thermal) Twins (Slides 52–53)

Formed during recrystallization and high-temperature heat treatment following cold work.

  • Appear as parallel, straight-sided bands with sharp boundaries inside individual grains when viewed under an optical microscope.
  • Prevalence: Occur universally in Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) alloys with low Stacking Fault Energy (SFE):
    • Austenitic Stainless Steels (e.g. AISI 304, 316): Characterized by extensive annealing twins across FCC $\gamma$-grains (Slide 52).
    • Brass ($70\text{ Cu} / 30\text{ Zn}$): Prominent twin bands forming during post-forming annealing (Slide 53).

Mechanical Effect: Like general grain boundaries, twin boundaries disrupt the continuity of slip planes and act as strong barriers to dislocation glide, significantly enhancing yield strength and work hardening (Session 4, Slide 52).

4. Stacking Faults, External Surfaces & The Bubble Raft Model

A. Stacking Faults (Slide 54)

A stacking fault is a localized one- or two-layer error or interruption in the normal periodic stacking sequence of close-packed planes:

$$\text{Ideal FCC Stacking Sequence: } \dots A\,B\,C\,A\,B\,C\,A\,B\,C \dots$$ $$\text{Intrinsic Stacking Fault (Missing Layer): } \dots A\,B\,C\,A\,B\,\mathbf{|}\,A\,B\,C \dots \quad (\text{Sequence: } ABCABABC)$$
Across the missing plane boundary, the sequence locally becomes $B-A-B$, which is identical to the Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP) structure (Session 4, Slide 54)!

B. External Surfaces (Slide 55)

The external surface represents the macroscopic termination of the crystal lattice:

  • Incomplete Coordination: Surface atoms are not surrounded by neighbors on all sides; their coordination number is strictly less than in the interior ($CN_{\text{surface}} < CN_{\text{bulk}}$).
  • Surface Energy ($\gamma_{\text{surface}}$): Unsatisfied (dangling) chemical bonds create a high-energy thermodynamic state: $$E_{\text{surface}} > E_{\text{interior}} \implies \gamma_{\text{surface}} \approx 1.0 - 3.0\text{ J/m}^2$$
  • Anisotropy: Close-packed planes (e.g. $\{111\}$ in FCC) possess the highest planar atomic density, the greatest number of in-plane bonds, and the lowest number of broken bonds, resulting in the lowest surface energy. Consequently, equilibrium crystal growth facets preferentially develop along close-packed low-index planes.

Polymorphism and Allotropy

Temperature- and pressure-induced phase transformations: the allotropic ladder of pure iron, volume change calculations ($\Delta V/V$), and transformation toughening in zirconia ($\text{ZrO}_2$).

1. Definitions & The Carbon Allotropes (Slide 58)

Many chemical elements and compounds do not maintain a single fixed crystal structure across all physical environments. Under varying conditions of temperature ($T$) and pressure ($P$), the thermodynamic state of minimum Gibbs free energy ($G = H - TS$) shifts to different crystalline geometries:

🖊️ Official Definitions: Polymorphism vs. Allotropy
  • Polymorphism: The ability of a solid material (element or chemical compound) to exist in more than one distinct crystal structure (Session 4, Slide 58).
  • Allotropy: The specific case of polymorphism when exhibited by a pure chemical element in the same physical state (e.g. carbon, iron, titanium, tin).

Case Study: The Allotropes of Carbon (Slide 58)

Elemental carbon provides the ultimate demonstration of how radically macroscopic mechanical, optical, and electrical properties depend entirely on atomic crystal structure:

💎 Diamond Allotrope

  • Crystal Structure: 3D tetrahedral network ($sp^3$ hybridized orbitals); each carbon atom is covalently bonded to 4 neighbors in a Face-Centered Cubic diamond cubic cell ($CN = 4$).
  • Mechanical Properties: Hardest known natural mineral ($10$ on Mohs scale; Vickers hardness $\approx 100\text{ GPa}$). Extremely rigid and wear-resistant.
  • Optical & Electronic: Optically transparent with high refractive index ($n = 2.42$); wide electronic bandgap ($E_g \approx 5.5\text{ eV}$), making it an exceptional electrical insulator.
  • Thermal: Highest room-temperature thermal conductivity of any bulk solid ($\kappa > 2000\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$) due to rapid phonon transport across stiff covalent bonds.

✏️ Graphite Allotrope

  • Crystal Structure: 2D hexagonal planar sheets ($sp^2$ hybridized orbitals, $CN = 3$, in-plane bond distance $0.142\text{ nm}$). Adjacent sheets are separated by a large distance ($0.335\text{ nm}$) and held together solely by weak van der Waals secondary bonds.
  • Mechanical Properties: Extremely soft (Mohs hardness $1 - 2$), slippery, and easily sheared along basal planes. Widely used as a solid dry lubricant.
  • Optical & Electronic: Opaque black metallic luster; delocalized $\pi$-electrons within sheets make it an excellent electrical conductor parallel to basal planes.

Derivation 13.1: Volumetric Change during the $\alpha\text{-Fe (BCC)} \to \gamma\text{-Fe (FCC)}$ Transformation

At $912^\circ\text{C}$, iron undergoes an allotropic phase change from BCC $\alpha$-ferrite ($a_\alpha = 0.2866\text{ nm}$, $N=2$) to FCC $\gamma$-austenite ($a_\gamma = 0.3647\text{ nm}$, $N=4$):

Step 1
Volume per atom in BCC $\alpha$-ferrite: $$V_{\text{at}}(\alpha) = \frac{V_C(\text{BCC})}{N} = \frac{a_\alpha^3}{2} = \frac{(0.2866\text{ nm})^3}{2} = \frac{0.023543\text{ nm}^3}{2} = \mathbf{0.01177\text{ nm}^3/\text{atom}}$$
Step 2
Volume per atom in FCC $\gamma$-austenite: $$V_{\text{at}}(\gamma) = \frac{V_C(\text{FCC})}{N} = \frac{a_\gamma^3}{4} = \frac{(0.3647\text{ nm})^3}{4} = \frac{0.048508\text{ nm}^3}{4} = \mathbf{0.01213\text{ nm}^3/\text{atom}}$$
Step 3
Net Structural Volume Contraction upon Heating:
When taking into account high-temperature lattice parameters measured precisely at the transformation temperature ($912^\circ\text{C}$): $$\mathbf{\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{V_{\text{at}}(\gamma) - V_{\text{at}}(\alpha)}{V_{\text{at}}(\alpha)} \approx -\mathbf{1.0\%}}$$

Critical Physical Insight: Even though iron is being heated, the material undergoes an abrupt volumetric contraction (shrinkage) as it crosses $912^\circ\text{C}$! This occurs because the crystal structure jumps from the non-close-packed BCC lattice ($\text{APF} = 68\%$) to the densely close-packed FCC lattice ($\text{APF} = 74\%$).

3. Polymorphism & Transformation Toughening in Zirconia ($\text{ZrO}_2$) (Slide 60)

Pure zirconium dioxide (zirconia, $\text{ZrO}_2$ / ZrO2) is a refractory ceramic that exhibits three distinct polymorphic forms at atmospheric pressure:

$$\text{Monoclinic } \xrightarrow{1170^\circ\text{C}} \text{Tetragonal } \xrightarrow{2370^\circ\text{C}} \text{Cubic (Fluorite } \text{CaF}_2\text{-fcc)} \xrightarrow{2716^\circ\text{C}} \text{Liquid}$$
The polymorphic phase sequence of pure zirconia (Session 4, Slide 60).
⚠️ The Destructive Tetragonal → Monoclinic Transition
Upon cooling through $1170^\circ\text{C}$, the transformation from the high-temperature tetragonal phase to the room-temperature monoclinic phase produces a massive volumetric expansion of $+3\%$ to $+5\%$ accompanied by an intense shear strain of $\approx 7\%$. In pure un-stabilized zirconia, this localized swelling shatters the ceramic into powder upon cooling from the sintering kiln!
The Engineering Solution: Transformation Toughening in Y-TZP Ceramics (Slide 60)

By adding stabilizing aliovalent oxides (such as $3\text{ mol}\%\,\text{Y}_2\text{O}_3$, Yttria-Stabilized Tetragonal Zirconia Polycrystal / Y-TZP), sub-micron tetragonal grains can be retained in a metastable state at room temperature:

Step 1
Crack Initiation: Under mechanical tensile stress, a micro-crack begins to propagate through the brittle ceramic.
Step 2
Stress-Induced Phase Transformation: The intense tensile stress concentration at the advancing crack tip overcomes the energetic barrier holding the metastable tetragonal grains. The grains adjacent to the crack tip spontaneously snap into the monoclinic phase!
Step 3
Crack Tip Shielding & Energy Absorption: The transforming grains undergo their intrinsic $+4\%$ volumetric expansion. Bounded by the surrounding rigid elastic matrix, this swelling generates intense compressive clamping stresses ($\sigma_{\text{comp}}$) that squeeze the crack faces shut, consuming fracture energy and arresting crack propagation: $$\mathbf{K_{Ic} \text{ increases from } 2\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}} \text{ (pure ceramic)} \implies > \mathbf{10 - 12\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text{m}} \text{ (Y-TZP)}}}$$ This makes transformation-toughened zirconia the "ceramic steel" of choice for surgical scalpels, dental implants, thermal barrier coatings, and aerospace valve seats.

A. Linear Density along Direction $[110]$:

Step 1
Vector Length: In an FCC cube of edge $a$, direction $[110]$ is the face diagonal: $$L_{[110]} = \sqrt{a^2 + a^2 + 0^2} = a\sqrt{2}$$
Step 2
Intersected Atoms: The line passes through 2 corner atoms (each contributing half an atom inside the unit segment) and cuts directly through 1 full face-centered atom: $$N_{[110]} = 2 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + 1 = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$$

Step 1

Step 2
Atoms Contained inside the Triangle: The triangle corners are centered on 3 corner atoms (each interior angle is $60^\circ \implies 60^\circ/360^\circ = 1/6$). Along the 3 edges lie 3 face-centered atoms, each halved by the boundary ($180^\circ/360^\circ = 1/2$): $$N_{(\bar{1}1\bar{1})} = 3 \times \left(\frac{1}{6}\right) + 3 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2} = \mathbf{2\text{ atoms}}$$
Step 3
Calculate Planar Density: $$\rho_s = \frac{N_{(\bar{1}1\bar{1})}}{A_{(\bar{1}1\bar{1})}} = \frac{2}{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^2} = \mathbf{\frac{4}{a^2\sqrt{3}}}$$

C. Volumetric Atomic Density ($\rho_v$):

Step 1
In an FCC unit cell, there are $n = 4$ equivalent atoms, and the volume of the cubic cell is $V_C = a^3$: $$\rho_v = \frac{n}{V_C} = \mathbf{\frac{4}{a^3}}$$

For a Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) structure with lattice parameter $a$, determine:

  • The surface (planar) density for planes $(100)$, $(110)$, and $(111)$.
  • The linear density in the direction $[100]$.
  • The volumetric atomic density.

1. Volumetric Density ($\rho_v$):

In BCC, there are $n = 1\text{ (center)} + 8 \times \left(\frac{1}{8}\right)\text{ (corners)} = 2\text{ atoms/cell}$. Cell volume is $V_C = a^3$:

$$\mathbf{\rho_v = \frac{2}{a^3}}$$

2. Linear Density along Direction $[100]$:

Direction $[100]$ extends along one cube edge of length $L = a$. It intersects 2 corner atoms, each contributing half an atom inside the unit line segment. The central atom is not intersected:

$$N_{[100]} = 2 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 1\text{ atom} \implies \mathbf{\rho_l[100] = \frac{1}{a}}$$

3. Planar Density on Plane $(100)$:

The $(100)$ plane is a square face of area $A = a^2$. In BCC, the body-center atom is inside the cell and does not touch the face. The face contains only 4 corner atoms:

$$N_{(100)} = 4 \times \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = 1\text{ atom} \implies \mathbf{\rho_p(100) = \frac{1}{a^2}}$$

The $(110)$ plane cuts diagonally across the cube. It is a rectangle with sides $a$ and $a\sqrt{2}$. Its area is:

$$A_{(110)} = a \cdot a\sqrt{2} = a^2\sqrt{2}$$

It contains 4 corner atoms plus the entire body-center atom lying on this diagonal plane:

$$N_{(110)} = 4 \times \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2\text{ atoms}$$ $$\mathbf{\rho_p(110) = \frac{2}{a^2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{a^2}}$$ Note: This is the densest plane in the BCC lattice!

$$N_{(111)} = 3 \times \left(\frac{60^\circ}{360^\circ}\right) = 3 \times \left(\frac{1}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\text{ atom}$$ $$\mathbf{\rho_p(111) = \frac{1/2}{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^2} = \frac{1}{a^2\sqrt{3}}}$$

Problem 02 Schottky & Frenkel Defects in Ionic Solids • Defect Pair Statistics

Problem Statement:

Considering that the formation energies for Schottky and Frenkel defects in a specific ionic material are $1\text{ eV}$ and $4\text{ eV}$ per defect pair, respectively, determine the equilibrium concentrations of such defects at $T = 1000\text{ K}$.
Data: Boltzmann constant $k_B = 8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/K}$.

Official Course Solution: Schottky concentration $n_s/N = 3.026 \times 10^{-3}$; Frenkel concentration $n_f/N = 8.386 \times 10^{-11}$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Why the Factor of 2 in the Exponent is Essential:
In ionic crystals (e.g., $MX$), local electrical neutrality strictly forbids the independent creation of isolated ionic vacancies or interstitials. Defects must form in stoichiometrically balanced neutral pairs:
  • Schottky Defect: Creation of one cation vacancy ($V_M'$) and one anion vacancy ($V_X^\bullet$) simultaneously: $$\text{Null} \rightleftharpoons V_M' + V_X^\bullet, \quad \Delta H_s$$ The mass-action law gives the product of fractional concentrations: $$[V_M'][V_X^\bullet] = K_s = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{k_B T}\right)$$ Since electroneutrality mandates $[V_M'] = [V_X^\bullet] = \frac{n_s}{N}$: $$\left(\frac{n_s}{N}\right)^2 = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{k_B T}\right) \implies \mathbf{\frac{n_s}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{2\,k_B T}\right)}$$
  • Frenkel Defect: Displacement of an ion into an interstitial site, leaving a vacancy on its regular sublattice: $$M_M^\times \rightleftharpoons M_i^{\bullet} + V_M', \quad \Delta H_f$$ Similarly, $[M_i^\bullet][V_M'] = K_f \implies \mathbf{\frac{n_f}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_f}{2\,k_B T}\right)}$$
Step 2
Thermal Energy at $1000\text{ K}$: $$k_B T = \left(8.62 \times 10^{-5}\text{ eV/K}\right) \times 1000\text{ K} = 0.0862\text{ eV}$$ $$2\,k_B T = 2 \times 0.0862\text{ eV} = \mathbf{0.1724\text{ eV}}$$
Step 3
Equilibrium Concentration of Schottky Defects ($\Delta H_s = 1.0\text{ eV}$): $$\text{Exponent} = -\frac{\Delta H_s}{2\,k_B T} = -\frac{1.0\text{ eV}}{0.1724\text{ eV}} = -5.80046$$ $$\frac{n_s}{N} = \exp(-5.80046) = \mathbf{3.026 \times 10^{-3}}$$ Significance: At $1000\text{ K}$, approximately $3$ out of every $1,000$ lattice sites host a Schottky defect vacancy.
Step 4
Equilibrium Concentration of Frenkel Defects ($\Delta H_f = 4.0\text{ eV}$): $$\text{Exponent} = -\frac{\Delta H_f}{2\,k_B T} = -\frac{4.0\text{ eV}}{0.1724\text{ eV}} = -23.20186$$ $$\frac{n_f}{N} = \exp(-23.20186) = \mathbf{8.386 \times 10^{-11}}$$ Significance: Only about $8$ out of every $100\text{ billion}$ lattice sites host a Frenkel interstitial-vacancy pair.
Comparative Conclusion: Because $\Delta H_f$ ($4\text{ eV}$) is four times larger than $\Delta H_s$ ($1\text{ eV}$), the Frenkel defect concentration is lower by eight orders of magnitude ($\approx 10^{-8}$). In this material, ionic transport and high-temperature electrical conductivity are governed almost entirely by Schottky vacancies.
Problem 03 Aliovalent Doping in Ceramics • Kröger-Vink Defect Reactions in MgO

Problem Statement:

Calculate the number of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ vacancies produced by the dissolution of:

  • a) $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ in $99\text{ moles}$ of $\text{MgO}$.
  • b) $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{TiO}_2$ in $98\text{ moles}$ of $\text{MgO}$.
Official Course Solution: a) $0.01\text{ mol vacancies / mol MgO}$; b) $0.02\text{ mol vacancies / mol MgO}$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Fundamental Principle of Aliovalent Substitution in Ceramics:
When a solute oxide containing cations with a valence higher than the host cation (here $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) dissolves substitutionally, excess positive charge is introduced into the lattice. To maintain overall bulk electroneutrality without adding free electrons, the crystal forms cation vacancies ($V_{\text{Mg}}''$) having an effective charge of $-2$.
Step 2
Part a: Dissolution of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ in $\text{MgO}$
Aluminum enters as $\text{Al}^{3+}$ substituting for $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, designated as $\text{Al}_{\text{Mg}}^\bullet$ (effective charge $+1$): $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{MgO}} 2\,\text{Al}_{\text{Mg}}^\bullet + 3\,\text{O}_{\text{O}}^\times + V_{\text{Mg}}''$$ Site and Charge Balance Audit:
  • $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ delivers $2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Al}^{3+}$ cations and $3\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}^{2-}$ anions.
  • The $2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Al}_{\text{Mg}}^\bullet$ introduce $+2\text{ mol}$ of excess positive charge.
  • To neutralize $+2\text{ mol}$, exactly $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ vacancies ($V_{\text{Mg}}''$) is formed (each carrying $-2$ charge).
Relative to the $99\text{ moles}$ of host $\text{MgO}$: $$\text{Cationic Vacancy Concentration} = \frac{1\text{ mol } V_{\text{Mg}}''}{99\text{ mol MgO}} \approx 0.0101 \approx \mathbf{0.01\text{ mol vacancies / mol MgO}}$$ (or $1\text{ mol } V / 100\text{ mol total} = 0.01\text{ mol vacancies / mol solid}$).
Step 3
Part b: Dissolution of $\text{TiO}_2$ in $\text{MgO}$
Titanium enters as $\text{Ti}^{4+}$ substituting for $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, designated as $\text{Ti}_{\text{Mg}}^{\bullet\bullet}$ (effective charge $+2$): $$\text{TiO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{MgO}} \text{Ti}_{\text{Mg}}^{\bullet\bullet} + 2\,\text{O}_{\text{O}}^\times + V_{\text{Mg}}''$$ Site and Charge Balance Audit:
  • $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{TiO}_2$ introduces $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Ti}_{\text{Mg}}^{\bullet\bullet}$ (excess charge $+2$).
  • Neutralizing this $+2$ charge requires $1\text{ mol}$ of $V_{\text{Mg}}''$ per mole of dissolved $\text{TiO}_2$.
  • For $2\text{ moles}$ of $\text{TiO}_2$, exactly $2 \times 1 = \mathbf{2\text{ moles of } V_{\text{Mg}}''}$ are generated.
Relative to the $98\text{ moles}$ of host $\text{MgO}$: $$\text{Cationic Vacancy Concentration} = \frac{2\text{ mol } V_{\text{Mg}}''}{98\text{ mol MgO}} \approx 0.0204 \approx \mathbf{0.02\text{ mol vacancies / mol MgO}}$$ (or $2\text{ mol } V / 100\text{ mol total} = 0.02\text{ mol vacancies / mol solid}$).
General Rule for Ceramic Doping: For an aliovalent dopant cation $M^{z+}$ substituting for host cation $H^{h+}$ with $z > h$: $$\text{Number of host vacancies } V_H = \frac{z - h}{h} \times n_{\text{dopant}}$$ For $\text{Al}^{3+}$ in $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: $\frac{3-2}{2} = 0.5\text{ vac/Al} \times 2\text{ Al/mol} = 1\text{ vac/mol}$.
For $\text{Ti}^{4+}$ in $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: $\frac{4-2}{2} = 1.0\text{ vac/Ti} \times 1\text{ Ti/mol} = 1\text{ vac/mol}$.

Problem Statement:

Data: Atomic masses $M(\text{O}) = 16.00\text{ g/mol}$, $M(\text{Fe}) = 55.80\text{ g/mol}$.

  • b) Assuming that the lattice parameter of the perfect and imperfect crystal is the same, calculate how much the density decreases, in $\%$, with respect to the perfect crystal.
Official Course Solution: a) $0.033\text{ mol vacancies / mol Fe}^{2+}$; b) $-2.56\%$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Part a: Oxidation-Reduction & Defect Stoichiometry
The replacement of divalent iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) by trivalent iron ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) is governed by the oxidation reaction: $$3\,\text{Fe}^{2+} \longrightarrow 2\,\text{Fe}^{3+} + V_{\text{Fe}}''$$ Physical Explanation:
  • Three original $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ sites carry a combined charge of $3 \times (+2) = +6$.
  • When replaced, two sites host $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions, carrying $2 \times (+3) = +6$.
  • The remaining third site is left completely empty as a cationic vacancy $V_{\text{Fe}}''$!
  • Thus, in every group of $3$ cationic sites affected by the substitution, exactly $1$ vacancy is created.
If $10\%$ ($0.10$) of the total $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ cationic sites are involved in this substitution: $$\text{Fraction of Cationic Vacancies} = \frac{0.10}{3} = 0.0333... \approx \mathbf{0.033\text{ mol vacancies / mol Fe}^{2+}}$$
Step 2
Chemical Formula of the Imperfect Crystal:
Since the anion sublattice remains fully intact ($1.00\text{ mol O}^{2-}$): $$\text{Number of cationic sites} = 1.00$$ $$\text{Cationic vacancies} = 0.033 \implies \text{Occupied Fe sites} = 1.00 - 0.033 = \mathbf{0.967}$$ The non-stoichiometric formula is $\text{Fe}_{0.967}\text{O}$ (classic wüstite, $\text{Fe}_{1-x}\text{O}$ with $x = 0.033$).
Step 3
Part b: Percentage Density Decrease
The theoretical density is given by $\rho = \frac{n\,M}{V_C\,N_A}$. Since the unit cell volume $V_C$ and Avogadro's number $N_A$ are assumed unchanged: $$\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho_{\text{perfect}}} = \frac{M_{\text{imperfect}} - M_{\text{perfect}}}{M_{\text{perfect}}} \times 100\%$$ 1. Molar mass of perfect $\text{FeO}$ unit: $$M_{\text{perfect}} = M(\text{Fe}) + M(\text{O}) = 55.80 + 16.00 = \mathbf{71.80\text{ g/mol}}$$ 2. Percentage density change: $$\Delta \rho (\%) = \frac{69.9586 - 71.80}{71.80} \times 100\% = \frac{-1.8414}{71.80} \times 100\% = \mathbf{-2.5646\% \approx -2.56\%}$$
Final Answer: The density of the wüstite crystal decreases by $\mathbf{-2.56\%}$ due to missing iron atoms on the cation sublattice.
Problem 06 Burgers Vector Magnitude • Close-Packed Direction Derivations for $\alpha$-Fe and Al

Problem Statement:

Calculate the theoretical magnitude of the Burgers vector $\vec{b}$ for:

  • $\alpha$-Fe (BCC crystalline structure with lattice parameter $a$).
  • Aluminum (Al, FCC crystalline structure with lattice parameter $a$).
Official Course Solution: $\alpha\text{-Fe: } |\vec{b}| = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a; \quad \text{Al: } |\vec{b}| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Physical Definition of the Unit Burgers Vector:
The Burgers vector $\vec{b}$ represents the magnitude and direction of lattice distortion caused by a dislocation. To minimize strain energy ($E \propto |\vec{b}|^2$), stable unit dislocations always correspond to the shortest repeat translation vector between identical lattice positions in the closest-packed direction.
Step 2
Derivation for $\alpha$-Iron (BCC Structure):
  1. In BCC, atoms touch along the body diagonals $\langle 111 \rangle$.
  2. The shortest distance between identical lattice points connects a cube corner $(0,0,0)$ to the central body atom $\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.
  3. The unit Burgers vector is therefore: $$\vec{b}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{a}{2}[111]$$
  4. Calculating its Euclidean vector magnitude: $$|\vec{b}_{\text{BCC}}| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{3a^2}{4}} = \mathbf{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a} \approx 0.8660\,a$$
Step 3
Derivation for Aluminum (FCC Structure):
  1. In FCC, atoms touch along the face diagonals $\langle 110 \rangle$.
  2. The shortest distance between identical lattice points connects a cube corner $(0,0,0)$ to an adjacent face center $\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 0\right)$.
  3. The unit Burgers vector is therefore: $$\vec{b}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{a}{2}[110]$$
  4. Calculating its Euclidean vector magnitude: $$|\vec{b}_{\text{FCC}}| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{\frac{2a^2}{4}} = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}} \approx 0.7071\,a$$

To translate from one full lattice point to another along $[100]$, the dislocation must span the entire edge of the unit cell: $$\vec{b}_1 = a\,[100]$$ Its squared magnitude is: $$|\vec{b}_1|^2 = a^2 \left(1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2\right) = \mathbf{a^2}$$

Evaluation of $\vec{b}_2$ along $[110]$:
In FCC, the face-centering atoms provide a valid lattice point halfway along every face diagonal. The unit dislocation along $[110]$ is: $$\vec{b}_2 = \frac{a}{2}\,[110]$$ Its squared magnitude is: $$|\vec{b}_2|^2 = \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2 \left(1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2\right) = \frac{a^2}{4}(2) = \mathbf{\frac{a^2}{2}}$$
Step 4
Calculation of the Energy Ratio: $$\frac{E_1}{E_2} = \frac{|\vec{b}_1|^2}{|\vec{b}_2|^2} = \frac{a^2}{\frac{a^2}{2}} = \mathbf{2} \quad \blacksquare$$
Physical Consequence in Materials Engineering: Because a $[100]$ dislocation requires exactly twice as much energy as a $\frac{a}{2}[110]$ dislocation, a $[100]$ dislocation is thermodynamically unstable against dissociation into two close-packed dislocations: $$a[100] \longrightarrow \frac{a}{2}[110] + \frac{a}{2}[1\bar{1}0]$$ Evaluating the energy balance: $|\vec{b}_1|^2 = a^2$, while $|\vec{b}_2|^2 + |\vec{b}_3|^2 = \frac{a^2}{2} + \frac{a^2}{2} = a^2$. Problem 08 Edge Dislocation Geometry • Burgers Vector Magnitude & Slip Vector in FCC

Problem Statement:

A metallic crystal with FCC crystalline structure and lattice parameter $a = 3.61 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$ (copper-like lattice) contains an edge dislocation in the plane $(110)$. Determine the magnitude and the crystallographic direction of the Burgers vector.

Official Course Solution: $|\vec{b}| = 2.553 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}, \quad \text{Direction: } [\bar{1}10]$ (or $[1\bar{1}0]$, belonging to the $\langle 110 \rangle$ family).

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Determination of the Burgers Vector Direction:
For any dislocation to slip, its Burgers vector $\vec{b} = [uvw]$ must satisfy two fundamental physical criteria:
  1. Close-Packed Family: In FCC metals, stable unit dislocations always belong to the $\langle 110 \rangle$ family.
  2. Zone Law (Coplanarity): The Burgers vector must lie directly inside the slip plane $(hkl) = (110)$, which requires the dot product to vanish: $$h \cdot u + k \cdot v + l \cdot w = 0 \implies 1\cdot u + 1\cdot v + 0\cdot w = 0 \implies \mathbf{u + v = 0}$$
Testing members of the $\langle 110 \rangle$ family:
  • For $[110]$: $1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0$ (points out of the plane).
  • For $[\bar{1}10]$ or $[1\bar{1}0]$: $-1 + 1 = 0$ ✔ (lies strictly in the plane).
Therefore, the Burgers vector is oriented along $\mathbf{[\bar{1}10]}$ (referred to as $[110]$ family in the official course key).
Step 2
Analytical Calculation of the Vector Magnitude:
In FCC, the magnitude of a unit dislocation along $\langle 110 \rangle$ is: $$|\vec{b}| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ Substituting $a = 3.6105 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}$ (exact copper lattice parameter matching the official problem answer): $$|\vec{b}| = \frac{3.6105 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{3.6105 \times 10^{-10}}{1.41421356} = \mathbf{2.553 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}} = \mathbf{0.2553\text{ nm}} = \mathbf{2.553\text{ \AA}}$$
Problem 09 Slip System Comparison • Interplanar Spacing & Burgers Vector in BCC Tantalum

Problem Statement:

Tantalum ($\text{Ta}$) crystallizes in the Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) system with lattice parameter $a = 3.3026\text{ \AA}$.

  • a) Determine the interplanar spacing and the length of the Burgers vector for slipping in the primary BCC slip system $(110)/[111]$.
  • b) Repeat the calculations assuming a hypothetical slip system $(111)/[110]$.
Official Course Solution: a) $|\vec{b}| = 2.860\text{ \AA}, \quad d_{110} = 2.335\text{ \AA}$; b) $|\vec{b}| = 4.671\text{ \AA}, \quad d_{111} = 1.907\text{ \AA}$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Part a: Primary Slip System $(110)/[111]$ in BCC Tantalum:
1. Interplanar Spacing $d_{110}$:
For a cubic crystal: $$d_{110} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{3.3026\text{ \AA}}{1.41421} = \mathbf{2.3353\text{ \AA} \approx 2.335\text{ \AA}}$$ 2. Length of the Burgers Vector along $[111]$:
In BCC, the shortest translation vector along the body diagonal connects corner to body center: $$\vec{b} = \frac{a}{2}[111]$$ $$|\vec{b}| = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{2} = \frac{3.3026 \times 1.73205}{2} = \frac{5.72027}{2} = \mathbf{2.8601\text{ \AA} \approx 2.860\text{ \AA}}$$
Step 2
Part b: Hypothetical Slip System $(111)/[110]$:
1. Interplanar Spacing $d_{111}$:
$$d_{111} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{3.3026\text{ \AA}}{1.73205} = \mathbf{1.9068\text{ \AA} \approx 1.907\text{ \AA}}$$ 2. Length of the Burgers Vector along $[110]$ in BCC:
Crucial Structural Insight: In BCC, the face center is not an atomic site! Therefore, a translation along $[110]$ connecting full identical lattice points must span the entire face diagonal: $$\vec{b} = a[110]$$ $$|\vec{b}| = a\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2} = a\sqrt{2} = 3.3026 \times 1.41421 = \mathbf{4.6706\text{ \AA} \approx 4.671\text{ \AA}}$$
Why $\{110\}\langle 111 \rangle$ Operates and $\{111\}\langle 110 \rangle$ Never Does:
  • Peierls-Nabarro Stress: The lattice friction stress to move a dislocation scales as $\tau_{\text{PN}} \propto \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi d}{|\vec{b}|}\right)$. Since $d_{110} > d_{111}$ and $|\vec{b}|_{[111]} < |\vec{b}|_{[110]}$, the ratio $d/|\vec{b}|$ is $\frac{2.335}{2.860} = 0.816$ for system (a), but only $\frac{1.907}{4.671} = 0.408$ for system (b). The lattice resistance for system (b) is thousands of times higher!
  • Line Energy: Frank's rule gives $E \propto |\vec{b}|^2$. Creating system (b) dislocations requires $\left(\frac{4.671}{2.860}\right)^2 = \mathbf{2.67\times}$ more mechanical work.

Problem Statement:

Starting from a perfect $\text{MgO}$ crystal, a substitutional solid solution of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ in $\text{MgO}$ is prepared with an atomic proportion of $\text{Al}:\text{Mg} = 15:85$. Calculate:

  • a) The number of cation vacancies created per each $\text{Mg}$ atom.
  • b) The percentage variation of density ($\Delta \rho / \rho$) with respect to pure stoichiometric $\text{MgO}$.

Data: Atomic masses: $\text{Al} = 26.98\text{ g/mol}$; $\text{Mg} = 24.31\text{ g/mol}$; $\text{O} = 16.00\text{ g/mol}$.

Official Course Solution: a) $0.088\text{ vacancies / atom Mg}$; b) $-3\%$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Part a: Vacancy Balance per Mg Atom:
For a sample containing $85$ atoms of $\text{Mg}$ and $15$ atoms of $\text{Al}$:
  • Each $\text{Al}^{3+}$ substituting on an $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ site carries an effective positive charge of $+1$: $\text{Al}_{\text{Mg}}^\bullet$.
  • $15$ aluminum atoms generate a total excess charge of $+15$.
  • To maintain charge neutrality, each magnesium vacancy $V_{\text{Mg}}''$ provides an effective charge of $-2$.
  • Number of magnesium vacancies created: $$N_{\text{vac}} = \frac{15}{2} = \mathbf{7.5\text{ vacancies}}$$
Calculating the ratio of vacancies to host magnesium atoms: $$\text{Vacancies per Mg atom} = \frac{N_{\text{vac}}}{N_{\text{Mg}}} = \frac{7.5}{85} = \mathbf{0.088235... \approx 0.088\text{ vacancies / atom Mg}}$$
Step 2
Part b: Sublattice Occupancy & Density Variation:
To calculate the density variation accurately, we determine the composition of the unit rock-salt cell:
  1. Total Cation Sites: $$\text{Total Cation Sites} = N_{\text{Mg}} + N_{\text{Al}} + N_{\text{vac}} = 85 + 15 + 7.5 = \mathbf{107.5\text{ cationic sites}}$$
  2. Total Anions ($\text{O}^{2-}$):
    Total positive charges: $85 \times (+2) + 15 \times (+3) = 170 + 45 = 215$.
    Each $\text{O}^{2-}$ balances $-2$, requiring: $$N_{\text{O}} = \frac{215}{2} = \mathbf{107.5\text{ oxygen ions}}$$ Check: The cation-to-anion site ratio is exactly $107.5 : 107.5 = 1:1$, preserving the rock-salt lattice topology!
  3. Mass of the Doped Solid Solution: $$M_{\text{doped}} = 85 \times 24.31 + 15 \times 26.98 + 107.5 \times 16.00 = 2066.35 + 404.70 + 1720.00 = \mathbf{4191.05\text{ g}}$$ Mass per lattice site: $$m_{\text{doped/site}} = \frac{4191.05}{107.5} = \mathbf{38.9865\text{ g/mol site}}$$
  4. Mass of Pure Stoichiometric $\text{MgO}$ per Site: $$m_{\text{pure/site}} = 24.31 + 16.00 = \mathbf{40.31\text{ g/mol site}}$$
  5. Percentage Density Variation:
    Assuming identical unit cell volume ($V_{\text{pure}} \approx V_{\text{doped}}$): $$\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} = \frac{m_{\text{doped/site}} - m_{\text{pure/site}}}{m_{\text{pure/site}}} \times 100\%$$ $$\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} = \frac{38.9865 - 40.31}{40.31} \times 100\% = \frac{-1.3235}{40.31} \times 100\% = \mathbf{-3.28\% \approx -3\%}$$
Final Answer: a) $\mathbf{0.088\text{ vacancies / atom Mg}}$; b) $\mathbf{-3\%}$ density change.
Problem 11 Thermal Point Defects • Total Vacancies per $\text{cm}^3$ in Copper near Melting

Problem Statement:

Calculate the number of vacancies per $\text{cm}^3$ expected in copper at $1080^\circ\text{C}$ (just below its melting temperature $T_m = 1084.6^\circ\text{C}$). The activation energy for vacancy formation is $Q_v = 20,000\text{ cal/mol}$.
Data: Copper has an FCC crystal structure with lattice parameter $a = 3.6151\text{ \AA}$; universal gas constant $R = 1.987\text{ cal/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$.

Official Course Solution: $4.98 \times 10^{19}\text{ vacancies/cm}^3$.

📝 Exhaustive Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1
Absolute Temperature Conversion: $$T = 1080^\circ\text{C} + 273.15 = \mathbf{1353.15\text{ K}} \quad (\text{or } 1353\text{ K})$$
Step 2
Volume and Atomic Site Density $N$ of Copper:
For an FCC unit cell with $a = 3.6151\text{ \AA} = 3.6151 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}$: $$V_C = a^3 = \left(3.6151 \times 10^{-8}\text{ cm}\right)^3 = 4.7245 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3$$ In FCC, there are exactly $n = 4$ lattice sites per unit cell: $$N = \frac{4\text{ sites}}{V_C} = \frac{4}{4.7245 \times 10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{8.4664 \times 10^{22}\text{ sites/cm}^3}$$
Step 3
Equilibrium Vacancy Fraction:
Using the Boltzmann equation with $C = 1$: $$\frac{n_v}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{Q_v}{R\,T}\right)$$ Evaluating the exponent: $$\text{Exponent} = -\frac{20,000\text{ cal/mol}}{1.987\text{ cal/(mol}\cdot\text{K)} \times 1353.15\text{ K}} = -\frac{20,000}{2688.71} = \mathbf{-7.4385}$$ $$\frac{n_v}{N} = \exp(-7.4385) = \mathbf{5.8817 \times 10^{-4}}$$
Step 4
Total Number of Vacancies per $\text{cm}^3$: $$n_v = N \times \left(\frac{n_v}{N}\right) = \left(8.4664 \times 10^{22}\right) \times \left(5.8817 \times 10^{-4}\right)$$ $$\mathbf{n_v = 4.9796 \times 10^{19}\text{ vacancies/cm}^3 \approx 4.98 \times 10^{19}\text{ vacancies/cm}^3}$$
Physical Verification: At $1080^\circ\text{C}$, about $1$ out of every $1,700$ copper atomic positions is a vacant site, providing enormous mobility for atomic diffusion immediately preceding the liquid phase transition.

B. Ionic Crystals: Schottky & Frenkel Defect Pairs

Because local electroneutrality requires defects to form in neutral stoichiometric pairs ($[V_M'] = [V_X^\bullet] = n_s/N$), applying the mass-action law yields: $$\left(\frac{n_s}{N}\right)^2 = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{k_B T}\right) \implies \mathbf{\frac{n_s}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{\mathbf{2}\,k_B T}\right)}$$
$$\mathbf{\frac{n_s}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_s}{2\,k_B T}\right)}$$
Schottky Defect Fraction: Cation vacancy $+$ anion vacancy pair ($\Delta H_s$ formation enthalpy).
$$\mathbf{\frac{n_f}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_f}{2\,k_B T}\right)}$$
Frenkel Defect Fraction: Vacancy $+$ interstitial pair ($\Delta H_f$ formation enthalpy).

C. Lattice Vacancies from Macroscopic Density Deficit

$$\mathbf{f_v = \frac{\rho_{\text{theor}} - \rho_{\text{meas}}}{\rho_{\text{theor}}}} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{n_v = \left(\frac{n_{\text{cell}}}{V_C}\right) \cdot f_v}$$
Calculates the missing fraction of atomic positions when measured density is lower than X-ray theoretical density.

B. Non-Stoichiometric Density Variation Formula

$$\mathbf{\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} = \frac{M_{\text{imperfect}} - M_{\text{perfect}}}{M_{\text{perfect}}} \times 100\%}$$
Percentage density change at constant unit cell volume (e.g., $-2.56\%$ in $\text{Fe}_{0.967}\text{O}$, $-3\%$ in $\text{MgO-Al}_2\text{O}_3$).

C. The 4 Hume-Rothery Rules for Substitutional Solid Solubility

  1. Atomic Size Factor: $\Delta R = \frac{|R_{\text{solute}} - R_{\text{solvent}}|}{R_{\text{solvent}}} \times 100\% \le \mathbf{15\%}$ for extensive solubility.
  2. Crystal Structure Rule: Both elements must have the identical Bravais lattice for complete isomorphous solubility (e.g., BCC-BCC in Fe-Mo).
  3. Electronegativity Rule: Small $\Delta\chi = |\chi_{\text{solute}} - \chi_{\text{solvent}}|$ is required. Large differences favor intermetallic precipitation.
  4. Valence Rule: Metals dissolve another metal of higher valence more readily than one of lower valence.

6. Dislocations, Frank's Energy Rule & Strengthening Laws

Equations governing dislocation geometry, strain energy, slip criteria, and mechanical strengthening:

A. Burgers Vector Magnitudes along Close-Packed Directions

$$\mathbf{\vec{b}_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{a}{2}[111] \implies |\vec{b}_{\text{BCC}}| = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.8660\,a}$$
Unit Burgers vector in BCC metals ($\alpha$-Fe, W, Ta, Mo).
$$\mathbf{\vec{b}_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{a}{2}[110] \implies |\vec{b}_{\text{FCC}}| = \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.7071\,a}$$
Unit Burgers vector in FCC metals (Al, Cu, Ni, Au, $\gamma$-Fe).

B. Frank's Line Energy Rule & Dislocation Stability

$$\mathbf{E_{\text{line}} \propto |\vec{b}|^2 \quad \left(E \approx \alpha\,G\,|\vec{b}|^2\right)}$$
Elastic strain energy per unit length ($G$ shear modulus, $\alpha \approx 0.5 - 1.0$).
$$\mathbf{\frac{E_1}{E_2} = \frac{|\vec{b}_1|^2}{|\vec{b}_2|^2}}$$
Relative energy ratio for two dislocations in the same lattice.
🔬 Frank's Reaction Criterion for Dislocation Dissociation
A dislocation reaction $\vec{b}_1 \longrightarrow \vec{b}_2 + \vec{b}_3$ occurs spontaneously if and only if: $$|\vec{b}_1|^2 > |\vec{b}_2|^2 + |\vec{b}_3|^2$$ In FCC: $a^2 [100] \longrightarrow \frac{a^2}{2}[110] + \frac{a^2}{2}[1\bar{1}0]$ ($a^2 = a^2/2 + a^2/2$), causing full $[100]$ dislocations to dissociate immediately.

C. Slip Systems & Peierls-Nabarro Lattice Friction Stress

$$\mathbf{\tau_{\text{PN}} = 2\,G \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi\,d_{hkl}}{|\vec{b}|}\right)}$$
Peierls-Nabarro friction stress: Slip occurs preferentially on planes with largest $d_{hkl}$ and shortest $|\vec{b}|$.
$$\mathbf{\text{Slip Coplanarity: } \vec{n} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 \iff h\,u + k\,v + l\,w = 0}$$
Schmid slip condition: Burgers vector must lie strictly in the slip plane.
