\section{Introduction}
In this laboratory we study a small bead $P$ of mass $m$ that slides without friction along a circular wire of centre $C$ and radius $R$. The wire is in the vertical plane $Oxy$, and this plane turns back and forth about the vertical axis $Oy$ with angular velocity $\bm\Omega(t)=A\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\jj$, where $A$ and $\omega$ are known positive constants (\cref{fig:geom}). The position of the bead on the wire is given by the angle $\varphi$ between the axis $Ox$ and the vector $\overrightarrow{CP}$ \cite{guidelines}.

Gravity makes the bead swing along the wire like a pendulum, and at the same time the turning plane drags it sideways. The goal is to see what happens when both effects act together. For that, we compute the forces of the wire and the mechanical energy, draw the path seen from the ground, find when the bead goes over the top of a fixed loop, and check how good a linearized equation is.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Kinematics and degree of freedom}
\noindent\begin{minipage}[t]{0.57\textwidth}
We use two reference frames. $Oxyz$ turns with the plane of the wire: $\ii$ is horizontal along the diameter through $C$, $\jj$ points up along the axis and $\kk=\ii\times\jj$ is normal to the plane. $Ox_1y_1z_1$ is fixed to the ground and coincides with $Oxyz$ at $t=0$. As shown in \cref{fig:geom}, the axis $Oy$ touches the wire at $O$, so $\overrightarrow{OC}=R\,\ii$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Degree of freedom.} A free particle has three coordinates. The wire removes two of them, since the bead has to stay in the plane $Oxy$ and at a distance $R$ from $C$. So there is $3-2=1$ degree of freedom, and we parametrise it with $\varphi$. The plane turns with a known law, so the position of the bead seen from the ground depends on $\varphi$ and also explicitly on $t$.

\textbf{How the plane turns.} The angle $\theta$ turned by the plane satisfies $\dt\theta=\Omega$ with $\theta(0)=0$. Integrating with the change $u=\omega\tau$, $\dd u=\omega\,\dd\tau$, and applying Barrow's rule,
\begin{equation}
\theta(t)=\int_0^t A\omega\sin(\omega\tau)\,\dd\tau=A\int_0^{\omega t}\sin u\,\dd u=A\big[-\cos u\big]_0^{\omega t}=A\big(1-\cos\omega t\big).
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
This means that the plane swings between $\theta=0$ and $\theta=2A$. With $A=\pi$ it makes a full turn at $t=T/2$ and then turns back, $T=2\pi/\omega$ being the period of the plane. The two bases are related by a rotation of angle $\theta$ about the vertical axis, and the columns of
\begin{equation}
[{}_1R_0]=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&0&\sin\theta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\theta&0&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}
\label{eq:rot}
\end{equation}
are the components of $\ii$, $\jj$, $\kk$ in $\{\ii_1,\jj_1,\kk_1\}$. These columns are unit vectors perpendicular to each other, so $[{}_1R_0][{}_1R_0]^T=I$, and $\det[{}_1R_0]=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$. To check it, we differentiate $\ii=\cos\theta\,\ii_1-\sin\theta\,\kk_1$ and get $\dd\ii/\dd t=-\dt\theta(\sin\theta\,\ii_1+\cos\theta\,\kk_1)=-\Omega\,\kk$, which is exactly $\bm\Omega\times\ii$.

\textbf{Motion along the wire.} Knowing that the polar unit vectors are $\er=\cos\varphi\,\ii+\sin\varphi\,\jj$ and $\ephi=-\sin\varphi\,\ii+\cos\varphi\,\jj$, the position of the bead is
\begin{equation}
\bm r=\overrightarrow{OC}+\overrightarrow{CP}=R(1+\cos\varphi)\,\ii+R\sin\varphi\,\jj,\qquad x\equiv R(1+\cos\varphi),
\label{eq:pos}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is its distance to the axis of rotation. In $Oxyz$ only $\varphi$ changes with time. By the chain rule, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}[R(1+\cos\varphi)]=-R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}[R\sin\varphi]=R\dt\varphi\cos\varphi$, so
\begin{equation}
\bm v_{\text{rel}}=-R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi\,\ii+R\dt\varphi\cos\varphi\,\jj=R\dt\varphi\,\ephi .
\label{eq:vrel}
\end{equation}
Differentiating again with the product rule, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(-R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi)=-R(\ddt\varphi\sin\varphi+\dt\varphi^2\cos\varphi)$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(R\dt\varphi\cos\varphi)=R(\ddt\varphi\cos\varphi-\dt\varphi^2\sin\varphi)$, so
\begin{equation}
\bm a_{\text{rel}}=-R(\ddt\varphi\sin\varphi+\dt\varphi^2\cos\varphi)\,\ii+R(\ddt\varphi\cos\varphi-\dt\varphi^2\sin\varphi)\,\jj=R\ddt\varphi\,\ephi-R\dt\varphi^2\,\er .
\label{eq:arel}
\end{equation}
The first term is the acceleration along the wire and the second one the centripetal acceleration towards $C$.

\textbf{Motion seen from the ground.} Since $O$ is on the axis and never moves, the velocity and acceleration in the fixed frame are $\bm v=\bm v_{\text{rel}}+\bm\Omega\times\bm r$ and $\bm a=\bm a_{\text{rel}}+\dt{\bm\Omega}\times\bm r+\bm\Omega\times(\bm\Omega\times\bm r)+2\bm\Omega\times\bm v_{\text{rel}}$, with $\Omega=A\omega\sin\omega t$ and $\dt\Omega=A\omega^2\cos\omega t$. Using $\jj\times\ii=-\kk$, $\jj\times\jj=\bm 0$ and $\jj\times\kk=\ii$, the four extra terms are
\begin{align}
\bm\Omega\times\bm r&=\Omega\,x\,(\jj\times\ii)+\Omega R\sin\varphi\,(\jj\times\jj)=-\Omega x\,\kk,\qquad
\dt{\bm\Omega}\times\bm r=-\dt\Omega x\,\kk,\label{eq:prod1}\\
\bm\Omega\times(\bm\Omega\times\bm r)&=\Omega\,\jj\times(-\Omega x\,\kk)=-\Omega^2x\,(\jj\times\kk)=-\Omega^2x\,\ii,\label{eq:prod2}\\
2\bm\Omega\times\bm v_{\text{rel}}&=2\Omega R\dt\varphi\big[-\sin\varphi\,(\jj\times\ii)+\cos\varphi\,(\jj\times\jj)\big]=2\Omega R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi\,\kk .\label{eq:prod3}
\end{align}
Adding them, we get:
\begin{align}
\bm v&=R\dt\varphi\,\ephi-\Omega x\,\kk,\label{eq:vel}\\
\bm a&=\big[-R(\ddt\varphi\sin\varphi+\dt\varphi^2\cos\varphi)-\Omega^2x\big]\ii+R(\ddt\varphi\cos\varphi-\dt\varphi^2\sin\varphi)\,\jj+\big[-\dt\Omega x+2\Omega R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi\big]\kk .\label{eq:acc}
\end{align}
As $\ephi\perp\kk$, the speed is $|\bm v|^2=R^2\dt\varphi^2+\Omega^2x^2$, the speed along the wire plus the speed at which the plane carries the bead around the axis.

\subsection{Forces and equation of motion}
The only real forces on the bead are its weight and the reaction of the wire. The wire is smooth, so the reaction has no component along its tangent $\ephi$:
\begin{equation}
\bm W=-mg\,\jj=-mg\sin\varphi\,\er-mg\cos\varphi\,\ephi,\qquad \bm N=N_r\,\er+N_z\,\kk ,
\label{eq:forces}
\end{equation}
where we used $\jj=\sin\varphi\,\er+\cos\varphi\,\ephi$. Since Newton's law is written in the turning frame, the inertia forces have to be added. From \cref{eq:prod1,eq:prod2,eq:prod3},
\begin{align}
\bm F_{\text{cf}}&=-m\bm\Omega\times(\bm\Omega\times\bm r)=m\Omega^2x\,\ii=mA^2\omega^2R\sin^2(\omega t)(1+\cos\varphi)\big[\cos\varphi\,\er-\sin\varphi\,\ephi\big],\label{eq:Fcf}\\
\bm F_{\text{Eul}}&=-m\dt{\bm\Omega}\times\bm r=m\dt\Omega x\,\kk=mA\omega^2R\cos(\omega t)(1+\cos\varphi)\,\kk,\label{eq:Feul}\\
\bm F_{\text{Cor}}&=-2m\bm\Omega\times\bm v_{\text{rel}}=-2m\Omega R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi\,\kk=-2mA\omega R\dt\varphi\sin(\omega t)\sin\varphi\,\kk ,\label{eq:Fcor}
\end{align}
with $\ii=\cos\varphi\,\er-\sin\varphi\,\ephi$. Newton's second law in $Oxyz$ is then $m\bm a_{\text{rel}}=\bm W+\bm N+\bm F_{\text{cf}}+\bm F_{\text{Eul}}+\bm F_{\text{Cor}}$. The reaction has no component along $\ephi$ and the Euler and Coriolis forces point along $\kk$, so the $\ephi$ component has no unknown force, $mR\ddt\varphi=-mg\cos\varphi-m\Omega^2x\sin\varphi$. And solving for $\ddt\varphi$,
\begin{equation}
\ddt\varphi=-\frac{g}{R}\cos\varphi-A^2\omega^2\sin^2(\omega t)\,(1+\cos\varphi)\sin\varphi .
\label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
The other two components give the reaction. Along $\er$, $-mR\dt\varphi^2=N_r-mg\sin\varphi+m\Omega^2x\cos\varphi$, and along $\kk$, $0=N_z+m\dt\Omega x-2m\Omega R\dt\varphi\sin\varphi$. Solving for $N_r$ and $N_z$,
\begin{align}
N_r&=-mR\dt\varphi^2+mg\sin\varphi-mA^2\omega^2R\sin^2(\omega t)(1+\cos\varphi)\cos\varphi ,\label{eq:Nr}\\
N_z&=mA\omega R\big[2\dt\varphi\sin(\omega t)\sin\varphi-\omega\cos(\omega t)(1+\cos\varphi)\big].\label{eq:Nz}
\end{align}
By the equation obtained, \cref{eq:eom}, it can be deduced that:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item If the plane is at rest ($A=0$), the bead is a pendulum, $\ddt\varphi=-(g/R)\cos\varphi$ (cosine and not sine because $\varphi$ is measured from the horizontal). The stable equilibrium is at the bottom, $\varphi=-\pi/2$, and the unstable one at the top, $\varphi=\pi/2$.
\item Only the centrifugal force changes the motion along the wire. The Euler and Coriolis forces are normal to the plane, so they only load the wire through $N_z$. In item 5 the centrifugal term is at most $(A\omega)^2/(g/R)\approx1\%$ of gravity, so we expect the bead to move almost like a pendulum on a fixed loop.
\item The bead cannot leave the wire, so $\bm N$ can take any sign. Unlike the string of Lab~1, there is no event that stops the integration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanical energy}
The kinetic energy has to use the velocity seen from the ground, \cref{eq:vel}:
\begin{equation}
E_k=\tfrac12m\big(R^2\dt\varphi^2+\Omega^2x^2\big),\qquad E_p=mgR\sin\varphi,\qquad E=E_k+E_p .
\label{eq:E}
\end{equation}
The weight is already included in $E$, so $E$ can only change because of the work of the reaction. Using \cref{eq:forces,eq:vel} and $\er\cdot\ephi=0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd E}{\dd t}=\bm N\cdot\bm v=\big(N_r\,\er+N_z\,\kk\big)\cdot\big(R\dt\varphi\,\ephi-\Omega x\,\kk\big)=-\Omega\,x\,N_z .
\label{eq:Edot}
\end{equation}
On a fixed wire the reaction is always perpendicular to the velocity and does no work. Here the wire moves with the plane and pushes the bead along $\kk$, so it does work whenever $\Omega\neq0$. With this, $E$ is only conserved for $A=0$.

\subsection{Fixed loop: when does the bead go over the top?}\label{sec:crit}
For $A=0$ the energy is conserved, and this is enough to answer the question. Multiplying \cref{eq:eom} by $\dt\varphi$ we get $\dt\varphi\ddt\varphi+(g/R)\cos\varphi\,\dt\varphi=0$. By the chain rule, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(\tfrac12\dt\varphi^2)=\dt\varphi\ddt\varphi$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\sin\varphi=\cos\varphi\,\dt\varphi$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big[\tfrac12\dt\varphi^2+\frac gR\sin\varphi\Big]=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad \tfrac12\dt\varphi^2+\frac gR\sin\varphi=\tfrac12\dt\varphi_0^2+\frac gR\sin\varphi_0 ,
\label{eq:first}
\end{equation}
which is $E/(mR^2)$ = constant. The highest potential energy is at the top, $\varphi=\pi/2$, where $E_p=mgR$. If the bead arrives there with some speed left, it goes over and $\varphi$ keeps growing. If not, it stops before the top and comes back. Imposing $\dt\varphi^2>0$ at $\varphi=\pi/2$ in \cref{eq:first}, we get the criterion
\begin{equation}
|\dt\varphi_0|>\dt\varphi_c=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{R}\big(1-\sin\varphi_0\big)} .
\label{eq:crit}
\end{equation}
If both sides are equal, the bead is on the separatrix $\dt\varphi=\pm\sqrt{(2g/R)(1-\sin\varphi)}$, so it moves up to the top but takes an infinite time to reach it. Below the separatrix $\varphi$ oscillates between the two angles where $\dt\varphi=0$, which are given by $\sin\varphi=\sin\varphi_0+R\dt\varphi_0^2/(2g)$. \Cref{tab:crit} applies the criterion to the nine initial conditions of item 7.

\subsection{Linearized equation}\label{sec:lin}
Now we assume that the bead stays close to the bottom, $\varphi=\delta-\pi/2$ with $|\delta|\ll1$, and that the rotation is weak, $A^2\ll g/(R\omega^2)$. Then $\cos\varphi=\cos(\delta-\pi/2)=\sin\delta\approx\delta$, $\sin\varphi=-\cos\delta\approx-1$, $1+\cos\varphi\approx1+\delta$ and $\ddt\varphi=\ddt\delta$. Substituting in \cref{eq:eom},
\begin{equation}
\ddt\delta=-\frac gR\,\delta+\Omega^2(1+\delta)=-\frac gR\,\delta+\Omega^2+\Omega^2\delta .
\end{equation}
As $\Omega^2\le A^2\omega^2\ll g/R$, the term $\Omega^2\delta$ is small compared with $(g/R)\delta$ and we drop it. Using $\sin^2(\omega t)=\tfrac12[1-\cos(2\omega t)]$, we get:
\begin{equation}
\ddt\delta+\omega_n^2\,\delta=\Omega^2=F_0\big[1-\cos(2\omega t)\big],\qquad \omega_n=\sqrt{g/R},\qquad F_0=\tfrac12A^2\omega^2 .
\label{eq:lin}
\end{equation}
This is an undamped oscillator of natural frequency $\omega_n$, forced at the frequency $2\omega$ \cite{notes}. The forcing is the centrifugal force. It is zero when the plane stops and maximum when it turns fastest, which happens twice in each period of the plane. Neither the statement nor the problem sheet \cite{problems} gives initial conditions for item 8, so we took the bead at rest at the bottom, $\delta(0)=0$ and $\dt\delta(0)=0$, so that all its motion comes from the loop. The particular solution is a constant $F_0/\omega_n^2$ plus $C\cos(2\omega t)$, and substituting the second one, $(\omega_n^2-4\omega^2)C=-F_0$. Adding the homogeneous solution $a\cos\omega_nt+b\sin\omega_nt$ and imposing the initial conditions ($\dt\delta(0)=b\,\omega_n=0$),
\begin{equation}
\delta(t)=\frac{F_0}{\omega_n^2}-\frac{F_0\cos(2\omega t)}{\omega_n^2-4\omega^2}+\Big(\frac{F_0}{\omega_n^2-4\omega^2}-\frac{F_0}{\omega_n^2}\Big)\cos(\omega_nt),\qquad 2\omega\neq\omega_n .
\label{eq:linsol}
\end{equation}
So the bead oscillates about $\delta_s=F_0/\omega_n^2=A^2R\omega^2/(2g)$, a bit outwards from the bottom, because the centrifugal force pushes it away from the axis. If $2\omega=\omega_n$, the denominator in \cref{eq:linsol} is zero. This is resonance, since each push arrives in phase with the natural swing. In that case the particular solution is $-\frac{F_0}{2\omega_n}t\sin(\omega_nt)$, which satisfies $\ddt\delta+\omega_n^2\delta=-F_0\cos(\omega_nt)$, and with the same initial conditions
\begin{equation}
\delta(t)=\frac{F_0}{\omega_n^2}\big(1-\cos\omega_nt\big)-\frac{F_0}{2\omega_n}\,t\sin(\omega_nt),\qquad 2\omega=\omega_n .
\label{eq:res}
\end{equation}
Now the amplitude grows linearly with time, so at some point $\delta\ll1$ is no longer true and the linearization stops being valid.

\subsection{Numerical method}
Since \texttt{ode45} only integrates first order systems \cite{matlab}, \cref{eq:eom} is written for the state vector $\bm X=[\varphi,\dt\varphi]^T$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\bm X}{\dd t}=\begin{pmatrix}X_2\\[2pt] -\dfrac gR\cos X_1-\Omega(t)^2\,(1+\cos X_1)\sin X_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bm X(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_0\\ \dt\varphi_0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Omega(t)=A\omega\sin(\omega t).
\label{eq:system}
\end{equation}
This system is the MATLAB function \texttt{diffeq(t,X,g,w,R,A)}, and \cref{eq:lin} is \texttt{diffeqlin}, with the state $[\delta,\dt\delta]^T$. The tolerances are $10^{-8}$ (relative) and $10^{-10}$ (absolute). In item 5 the solution is stored at \num{10000} instants of $[0,3T]$, which is about 125 points per swing \cite{guidelines}. Forces and energies are computed afterwards from the stored $\varphi$ and $\dt\varphi$ with exactly \cref{eq:Fcf,eq:Feul,eq:Fcor,eq:Nr,eq:Nz,eq:E}. To check \cref{eq:Edot} without adding a third state, the work of the wire is computed from the power $P_i=-\Omega_ix_iN_{z,i}$ with the trapezoidal rule,
\begin{equation}
W_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\tfrac12\,(P_i+P_{i+1})(t_{i+1}-t_i),
\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
and compared with $E-E_0$. The position in the fixed frame comes from \cref{eq:theta,eq:rot}, $x_1=x\cos\theta$, $y_1=R\sin\varphi$, $z_1=-x\sin\theta$. In all the cases $g=\SI{9.81}{m/s^2}$ and $m=\SI{1}{kg}$. Items 5 and 6 use $A=\pi$, $R=\SI{1}{m}$, $\omega=\SI{0.1}{rad/s}$ and $\varphi_0=\dt\varphi_0=0$ over $[0,3T]$, with $T=\SI{62.83}{s}$. Item 7 uses $A=0$, $R=\SI{1}{m}$ and the nine conditions of \cref{tab:crit}, over \SI{4}{s}, which is longer than the longest period of the closed curves (\SI{3.36}{s}). Item 8 uses $A=0.1$, $R=\SI{0.01}{m}$, $\omega=\omega_n,\ \omega_n/2,\ \omega_n/4$ with $\omega_n=\SI{31.32}{rad/s}$, and $\delta_0=\dt\delta_0=0$, over \SI{10}{s}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Oscillating loop: angle, reaction and inertia forces}
At $t=0$ the plane is at rest, so \cref{eq:eom} gives $\ddt\varphi(0)=-g/R=\SI{-9.81}{rad/s^2}$, as the code prints. The bead falls from the end of the horizontal diameter and swings between $\varphi=0$ and $\varphi=-\pi$ (\cref{fig:loop}a). At $\varphi=-\pi$ it passes through $O$, on the axis. Each swing takes \SI{2.37}{s}, so there are 79 swings in $[0,3T]$. This is longer than the small-oscillation period $2\pi\sqrt{R/g}=\SI{2.006}{s}$, because an amplitude of $90^\circ$ around the bottom is not small at all. As observed in \cref{fig:loop}a, the amplitude stays the same over the three periods of the plane, which makes sense since the centrifugal term is only 1\% of gravity. The highest angle is $\varphi=\SI{0.0118}{rad}$, so the energy given by the wire lifts the bead about \SI{1}{cm} above where it started.

The radial reaction (\cref{fig:loop}b) is zero at the turning points and reaches \SI{-29.43}{N}, which is $-3mg$, at the bottom. There \cref{eq:first} gives $\dt\varphi^2=2g/R$, and plugging it into \cref{eq:Nr}, $N_r=-2mg-mg$. The wire holds the weight and also pulls the bead towards $C$ to make it turn. The normal reaction (\cref{fig:loop}c) reaches $\pm\SI{2.78}{N}$ and has almost the same shape as the Coriolis force (\cref{fig:inertia}c) with the opposite sign, because the wire has to balance it. The maximum of the Coriolis force is $2mA\omega R\dt\varphi_{\max}=2\pi(0.1)(4.43)=\SI{2.78}{N}$. It is large because the bead moves fast along the wire ($\dt\varphi$ up to \SI{4.4}{rad/s}) while the plane turns, and it almost vanishes at $t=0,\,T/2,\,T,\dots$ ($t=0$, 31, 63, \dots\ s), when the plane stops to turn back. In \cref{fig:inertia} each inertia force has its own axis, since their sizes differ by two orders of magnitude \cite{guidelines}. The centrifugal force (\cref{fig:inertia}a) always points away from the axis, and its maximum is $m(A\omega)^2(2R)=\SI{0.197}{N}$, reached when the plane turns fastest, at $t=T/4,\,3T/4,\dots$ The Euler force (\cref{fig:inertia}b) follows $\cos\omega t$. It stays below \SI{0.063}{N} and is largest when the plane stops, because the rate of the plane changes slowly ($|\dt\Omega|\le A\omega^2=\SI{0.031}{rad/s^2}$).

\subsection{Energy}
In every swing $E_k$ and $E_p$ exchange about \SI{9.8}{J} (\cref{fig:energy}), as in any pendulum. Their sum is not constant, but its changes are too small to see at the scale of the figure; the code gives $E-E_0$ between \SI{-0.082}{J} and \SI{0.313}{J}. While the plane speeds up, the wire pushes the bead with it and gives it energy, and while the plane slows down the wire takes that energy back. The plane changes its speed slowly compared with one swing ($T=\SI{62.8}{s}$ against \SI{2.37}{s}), so the energy goes back to $E_0$ every time the plane stops, and after $3T$ the net gain is only \SI{0.0006}{J}. This explains why the amplitude in \cref{fig:loop}a does not drift.

The work of the wire computed with \cref{eq:W} agrees with $E-E_0$ within \SI{1.5e-4}{J}, 0.05\% of the range of $E$, which is the error of the trapezoidal rule. With this, we can conclude that \cref{eq:Edot} and the expressions of $N_z$ and $E$ in the code are consistent. With tighter tolerances ($10^{-11}$, $10^{-12}$), $\varphi$ changes by at most \SI{1.4e-4}{rad}.

\subsection{Trajectory in the fixed frame}
Seen from the ground (\cref{fig:traj}), the bead always stays on the surface swept by the wire when it turns about $Oy$. This surface is a torus without a hole, because the circle touches the axis at $O$. Each swing draws one petal: the bead goes out to $x=2R$ when $\varphi=0$ and back to the axis when $\varphi=-\pi$. The petals are spread around the axis because the plane turns up to $\theta=2\pi$ at $t=T/2$ and goes back to 0 at $t=T$ (\cref{eq:theta}). The three periods draw almost the same petals, close to each other but not on top of each other, since \SI{2.37}{s} does not divide $T$ exactly. As a check, $\sqrt{x_1^2+z_1^2}$ equals $x$ within \SI{2e-16}{m}, since a rotation about the axis cannot change the distance to it.

The script also saves an animation of the first period, \texttt{trajectory\_P.avi}, with the wire turned by $\theta(t)$, as suggested in the guidelines \cite{guidelines}.

\subsection{Fixed loop: phase portrait}
\Cref{fig:phase} shows the nine cases of \cref{tab:crit} together with the separatrix of \cref{eq:crit}. The colour gives $\varphi_0$, the line style $\dt\varphi_0$, and a dot marks where each curve starts. The five cases $(0,0)$, $(0,1.5)$, $(0,3)$, $(\pi/4,0)$ and $(\pi/4,1.5)$ are closed curves inside the separatrix, around the stable equilibrium $(-\pi/2,0)$, so the bead oscillates. The period goes from \SI{2.37}{s} to \SI{3.36}{s} as the curve gets closer to the separatrix, because the bead slows down near the top. The cases $(\pi/4,3)$, $(\pi/2,1.5)$ and $(\pi/2,3)$ start above the separatrix and give open curves: the bead keeps going round, slowest at the top and fastest at the bottom, and we plot one turn since the curves repeat every $2\pi$. The case $(\pi/2,0)$ starts on the unstable equilibrium and stays there as a single point, since numerically $\cos(\pi/2)\approx6\times10^{-17}$ is far too small to move it in \SI{4}{s}. As a conclusion, all nine cases behave as \cref{tab:crit} predicts, and in all of them \cref{eq:first} holds to better than $2\times10^{-7}\,mgR$.

\subsection{Linearized and full equation}
\Cref{fig:lin} shows the percentage error $100\,|\delta_{\text{lin}}-\delta_{\text{full}}|/\max|\delta_{\text{full}}|$. We divide by the amplitude of $\delta$ and not by $\delta$ itself, because $\delta$ crosses zero. The error goes from 0.01\% to almost 30\%, so the vertical axis is logarithmic, as the guidelines suggest \cite{guidelines}. The downward spikes are the instants where both solutions cross. The \texttt{ode45} solution of \cref{eq:lin} matches \cref{eq:linsol,eq:res} within \SI{2e-8}{rad}, so the differences come from the model, not from the integration (numbers in \cref{tab:lin}).

For $\omega=\omega_n$ (solid line), $\delta$ stays small but the error grows steadily from the start, up to 21\%. The cause is the term $\Omega^2\delta$ dropped in \cref{sec:lin}, which lowers the stiffness of the full equation from $g/R=\SI{981}{s^{-2}}$ by about $A^2\omega^2/2=\SI{4.9}{s^{-2}}$ on average. So the full solution oscillates 0.25\% slower and falls behind, by about \SI{0.8}{rad} of phase after \SI{10}{s}.

For $\omega=\omega_n/2$ (dashed line), $2\omega=\omega_n$, which is the resonance of \cref{eq:res} \cite{notes}. Both solutions grow like $F_0t/(2\omega_n)$, up to \SI{0.196}{rad} ($11^\circ$) at \SI{10}{s}. At first they agree, so the error starts as the smallest of the three, but as $\delta$ grows $\sin\delta\approx\delta$ gets worse, the full pendulum slows down and gets out of phase with the forcing, and the error ends as the largest, 27.8\%.

For $\omega=\omega_n/4$ (dotted line), the forcing is far from resonance and 16 times weaker than in the first case: $\delta$ stays below \SI{8e-4}{rad} and the error below 2\%, so here the linearization works.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
In the fixed frame the energy is not conserved because the wire moves (\cref{eq:Edot}). In the turning frame the reaction and the Euler and Coriolis forces are perpendicular to $\bm v_{\text{rel}}$ and do no work, but the centrifugal potential $-\tfrac12m\Omega^2x^2$ depends on time, so $\tfrac12mR^2\dt\varphi^2+E_p-\tfrac12m\Omega^2x^2$ is not conserved either. Only a constant $\Omega$, like $A=0$ in item 7, gives a conserved energy. In item 8, with $\delta(0)\neq0$ a free oscillation would change the size of the errors, but not the phase lag or the resonance.

%==============================================================
\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The bead on the oscillating loop has one degree of freedom, $\varphi$, and in the turning frame its equation of motion only contains gravity and the centrifugal force. In item 5 the bead swings like a pendulum of $90^\circ$ amplitude and period \SI{2.37}{s}. The wire makes up to $3mg$ in its plane and up to \SI{2.78}{N} normal to it, almost all against the Coriolis force. This normal push does work, so the energy is not conserved, but the work cancels every time the plane stops.

With the loop fixed, energy conservation gives $\dt\varphi_c=\sqrt{(2g/R)(1-\sin\varphi_0)}$, and all cases follow it. The linearized equation, a forced oscillator, works for weak forcing far from resonance, drifts in phase because of the dropped term $\Omega^2\delta$, and fails at resonance, $\omega=\omega_n/2$, where $\delta$ keeps growing.
