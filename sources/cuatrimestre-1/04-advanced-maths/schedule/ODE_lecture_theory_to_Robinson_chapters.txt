Ch. 27, Examples 27.2 and 27.3 (p. 264)

Ch. 27, end of Sec. 27.2 (p. 261) with Examples 27.1, 27.4, 27.5

  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.1 (p. 270-271)
  - Case 1) two distinct real eigenvalues
  - General solution x(t) = A e^(lambda1 t) v1 + B e^(lambda2 t) v2,
    Eq. (28.1), with Example 28.1 (p. 270-271)
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.2 (p. 271-272) and Appendix B (p. 384)
  - Proof that the two solutions are linearly independent. The book
    states that eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly
    independent (p. 272) and proves it in Appendix B, "Linear
    independence of eigenvectors" (p. 384). Example 28.2 (p. 274-275)
    checks it directly: alpha v1 + beta v2 = 0 forces alpha = beta = 0
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.2 (p. 271-274)
  - Diagonalizable matrix and change of basis: write x = P x~ with

    becomes dx~/dt = P^(-1) A P x~ = diag(lambda1, lambda2) x~,
    Eq. (28.7)-(28.9), which is uncoupled. Example 28.2 (p. 274-276)
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.3 (p. 276-278)

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.4 (p. 279)

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.4, Example 28.3 (p. 279-280, Fig. 28.7-28.8)

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.1 (p. 285-286)
  - Complex pair lambda = rho +/- i omega, eigenvectors
    eta = v1 +/- i v2. Real solution x(t) = 2 Re[C e^(lambda t) eta],
    Eq. (29.1), which expands to
    x(t) = e^(rho t)[(A cos wt + B sin wt) v1 + (B cos wt - A sin wt) v2],
    Eq. (29.2). Example 29.1 (A = [2 5; -2 0], lambda = 1 +/- 3i)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.2 (p. 287-288)
  - General solution via change of basis: taking real and imaginary
    parts of A eta = lambda eta gives A v1 = rho v1 - omega v2 and
    A v2 = omega v1 + rho v2, Eq. (29.3). With x = x~ v1 + y~ v2,
    Eq. (29.4), i.e. x~ = [v1 v2]^(-1) x, the system becomes
    dx~/dt = [rho omega; -omega rho] x~, Eq. (29.5). Example 29.2
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.2 (p. 289-291, Fig. 29.1-29.4)
  - Phase portrait in the new coordinates: in polar coordinates
    (Eq. 29.6-29.8) r' = rho r and theta' = -omega, so
    r = R e^(rho t), theta = T - omega t.
    rho < 0: stable spiral (Fig. 29.2); rho > 0: unstable spiral
    (Fig. 29.3); rho = 0: centre, closed orbits (Fig. 29.4)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.3 (p. 291-293, Fig. 29.5-29.6)
  - Converting back to x: the type (spiral or centre) does not change;
    the sense of rotation is read from the sign of x' on the positive
    y-axis (x = 0, y > 0). Example 29.3, Eq. (29.9)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 30 (p. 295)
  - Theory for a repeated real eigenvalue: two subcases. (A repeated
    eigenvalue zero is only treated in Exercise 30.5.)
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.1 (p. 295-296, Fig. 30.1)
  - Two linearly independent eigenvectors: A = lambda I, every vector
    is an eigenvector, x(t) = e^(lambda t) x(0); trajectories are rays.
    Stable star (lambda < 0) or unstable star (lambda > 0)
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.2 (p. 295-297)
  - Only one independent eigenvector v: decomposing A. Choose any v2
    not parallel to v; then v1 = (A - lambda I) v2 is an eigenvector,
    so A v2 = v1 + lambda v2, Eq. (30.1). (The footnote points to the
    Cayley-Hamilton route, Exercises 30.2-30.3.)
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.2 (p. 297)
  - Change of variable x~ = [v1 v2]^(-1) x (lecture: z = Q^(-1) x):
    dx~/dt = [lambda 1; 0 lambda] x~, Eq. (30.2)-(30.3). Solve
    y~ = A e^(lambda t) first, then x~ by the integrating factor
    e^(-lambda t) (Ch. 9): x~ = B e^(lambda t) + A t e^(lambda t)
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.2, Eq. (30.4) (p. 297)
  - General solution in x:
    x(t) = [A t e^(lambda t) + B e^(lambda t)] v1 + A e^(lambda t) v2,
    with v1 = (A - lambda I) v2
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.2 (p. 298, Fig. 30.2) and Example 30.1 (p. 298-299,
Fig. 30.3)
  - Improper node: phase diagram in x~ ('S'-shaped trajectories; x~'
    changes sign on the line y~ = -lambda x~, Fig. 30.2), then in x.
    Example 30.1 is the concrete example the lecture still owed:
    A = [5 -4; 1 1], lambda = 3 (repeated), single eigenvector (2, 1),
    Eq. (30.5); x' = -4y < 0 on the positive y-axis fixes the sense
    of the 'S'. Stable if lambda < 0, unstable if lambda > 0
  [Oct 15]

Ch. 31 (p. 301-305)
  - Not covered yet: summary of all linear phase portraits and the

  [Nov 3; notes p. 1-2]

Ch. 33, Sec. 33.2-33.3 (p. 331-337)

    y' = y(bx - s) is the book's "Predator-prey models II"

    (p. 331) is the version with a logistic prey
  [Nov 3; notes p. 2]

Ch. 33, Exercise 33.2 (p. 339)
  - Cooperation models x' = x(A - ax + by), y' = y(B + cx - dy). The
    book has them only as an exercise, not in the text
  [Nov 3; notes p. 2]

Ch. 34, Sec. 34.1 (p. 341-343)
  - Newtonian dynamics: particle of mass m in a potential V(x),
    E = (1/2) m x'^2 + V(x); dE/dt = 0 gives m x'' = -V'(x); reduction
    of order with y = x' gives the system x' = y, m y' = -V'(x)
  [Nov 3; notes p. 2-3]

Ch. 34, Sec. 34.3 (p. 347-349)
  - With friction proportional to velocity: m x'' = -k x' - V'(x),
    system x' = y, m y' = -k y - V'(x)
  [Nov 3; notes p. 3]

Ch. 35, Sec. 35.1 and 35.2 (p. 352 and 356)
  - Simple pendulum theta'' = -w^2 sin(theta): system theta' = v,
    v' = -w^2 sin(theta); damped: v' = -k v - w^2 sin(theta)
  [Nov 3; notes p. 3-4]

Ch. 7, Sec. 7.2 (p. 48), one-dimensional version only
  - Definitions: critical (equilibrium) point, isolated critical
    point, trajectory tending to a point, stable, asymptotically
    stable, unstable. The book defines stability only for one
    equation (Sec. 7.2); the planar definitions with discs of radius
    r and R are not in the book
  [Nov 3; notes p. 5-6]

Ch. 31 (p. 301)
  - Reminder: stability type of the origin for linear systems
    (nodes, saddle, stars and improper nodes, spirals, centre). The

  [Nov 3; notes p. 6-7]

Ch. 32, Sec. 32.4 and 32.4.1 (p. 314)

  [Nov 3; notes p. 7]

Ch. 32, Sec. 32.4.2 (p. 314-316)

Haberman Ch. 2, Sec. 2.3 (p. 31-49)
  - Heat equation u_t = u_xx on 0 < x < L with u(x, 0) = f(x) and zero
    Dirichlet conditions u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0. Separation of
    variables u = phi(x) T(t), separation constant -lambda,
    T = C e^(-lambda t); eigenvalue problem phi'' = -lambda phi,
    phi(0) = phi(L) = 0, with the three cases lambda > 0, = 0, < 0;
    eigenvalues (n pi / L)^2, eigenfunctions sin(n pi x / L)
  [Nov 12; notes p. 1-5]

Haberman Ch. 2, Sec. 2.2 (p. 29) and Sec. 2.3 (p. 31-49)
  - Superposition principle; the infinite series
    u = sum b_n sin(n pi x / L) e^(-(n pi / L)^2 t); the initial
    condition must be a Fourier sine series
  [Nov 12; notes p. 5-6]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.1 (p. 75-77, Fig. 3.1.4 on p. 77)
  - General Fourier series a_0 + sum a_n cos + sum b_n sin; it is
    2L-periodic, so f is replaced by its periodic extension (for
    f(x) = x the extension is not continuous)
  [Nov 12; notes p. 7]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.2 (p. 77-78), Eq. (3.2.1)-(3.2.2); orthogonality
in Appendix to Sec. 2.3 (p. 49)
  - The three questions (convergence to f, term-by-term
    differentiation, coefficients); orthogonality of sines and cosines
    on [-L, L]; coefficients a_0 = (1/2L) int f, a_n = (1/L) int f cos,
    b_n = (1/L) int f sin
  [Nov 12; notes p. 7-10]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.2 (p. 79, Eq. 3.2.5)
  - Example: f = 1 on one side of the interval, 0 on the other (the
    book's example has the jump at x = L/2, the notes at x = 0);
    result 1/2 + sum (1 - (-1)^m)/(m pi) sin(m pi x / L)
  [Nov 12; notes p. 10-11]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.3 (p. 81-98)
  - Even f gives a cosine series, odd f a sine series; a function given
    on [0, L] is extended periodically and as an odd or even function
  [Nov 12; notes p. 11-12]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.2-3.3 (p. 77-98)
  - Example f(x) = x on [-L, L]: a_0 = a_n = 0,
    b_n = 2L (-1)^(n+1) / (n pi)
  [Nov 12; notes p. 11-12]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.2 (p. 77-78)
  - Piecewise smooth functions and the convergence theorem: the series
    converges to f where f is continuous and to the average of the two
    side limits at a jump
  [Nov 12; notes p. 12-13]

Haberman Ch. 3, Sec. 3.4 (p. 99-100, Fig. 3.4.1)
  - Term-by-term differentiation is not always allowed. Counterexample:
    the sine series of x on 0 <= x < L differentiated gives
    1 = 2 sum (-1)^(n+1) cos(n pi x / L), which is false. Theorem: a
    continuous Fourier series (f(-L) = f(L)) with f' piecewise smooth
    can be differentiated term by term
  [Nov 12; notes p. 13-14]

======================================================================
NOTES ON DIFFERENCES BETWEEN LECTURE AND BOOK
======================================================================

- Order of presentation: the lecture runs ahead of the book in places.
  The mass-spring example (Sep 22) is in Ch. 13, after the Ch. 12
  solution methods, and logistic growth is in Ch. 8 although it was
  seen together with the Ch. 7 phase portraits.

- Terminology: the book never says "Picard's theorem" or "well-posed".
  It states the result as Theorem 6.2 and discusses existence and
  uniqueness in Sec. 6.1. The book calls the method "undetermined
  coefficients".

- Free fall: the book derives F = ma in Sec. 5.3 but treats free fall
  only as Exercise 5.6 (an apple falling from height h), so the
  derivation seen in class comes from Sec. 5.3.

- Variation of constants (Oct 1): Ch. 18 is a starred chapter, which
  the book marks as optional material. It assumes you already know two
  independent solutions of the homogeneous equation, so it builds on
  Ch. 11 and Ch. 17 (reduction of order). Ch. 18 has no numbered
  sections.

- Systems of ODEs (Sep 8 and Oct 6): the book only mentions them in
  passing in Ch. 3. Their treatment is Part V (Ch. 25 onwards), which
  the lecture reached on Oct 6.

- Systems (Oct 6): the lecture develops the theory of linear systems
  (Wronskian, fundamental set, homogeneous + particular) in a general
  way, which the book does not do. Robinson is concerned with two
  equations only (n = 2) and moves quickly to explicit solutions and
  phase portraits. Ch. 25 is starred (optional in the book), and
  Ch. 26 has no numbered sections.

- Order of the two solution methods: the book presents "convert to a
  second order ODE" first (Ch. 26) and the eigenvalue method after it
  (Ch. 27-28), the same order as the lecture. Ch. 27 only sets up the
  eigenvalue problem; the explicit solutions by eigenvectors are in
  Ch. 28 (distinct real), Ch. 29 (complex) and Ch. 30 (repeated).

- Eigenvalues and phase portraits (Oct 8-15): the lecture has covered
  the three cases, distinct real (Ch. 28), complex (Ch. 29) and
  repeated (Ch. 30). Not seen yet: the summary of phase portraits and
  the Jordan form (Ch. 31) and Sec. 28.5 on stable and unstable
  manifolds (p. 281).

- Change of basis (Oct 13, Oct 15): the lecture writes z = Q^(-1) x;
  the book writes x~ = [v1 v2]^(-1) x, with v1, v2 the real and
  imaginary parts of the eigenvector (Ch. 29) or v1 = (A - lambda I) v2
  and v2 any vector not parallel to the eigenvector (Ch. 30). The
  canonical matrices are [rho omega; -omega rho] (Eq. 29.5) and
  [lambda 1; 0 lambda] (Eq. 30.2); if Q orders its columns differently
  the off-diagonal entries move or change sign.

- Exercises: most items of the course sheets copy Robinson's
  end-of-chapter exercises (same items and data), so the book's
  surrounding sections are the reading for each class exercise. The
  items not taken from the book are listed as "not in the book" in
  the exercises table.

- Terminology (Oct 8): the book never says "diagonalizable" or
  "triangular". It describes the same ideas as "changing coordinates"
  (Sec. 28.2) and as matrices of the form [lambda1 b; 0 lambda2] (the
  upper-triangular case, Example 27.3). It writes the new variable as
  x~ (x with a tilde) where the lecture uses z, so z = P^(-1) x
  corresponds to x~ = P^(-1) x, Eq. (28.5).

- "Saddle node": the book only says "saddle point" or "saddle". The
  word "node" is reserved for stable and unstable nodes (Sec. 28.3),
  so "saddle node" in the lecture notes is probably "saddle".

- Linear independence (Oct 8): the book proves independence of the
  eigenvectors, not of the vector solutions e^(lambda t) v directly,
  and it does not use the Wronskian for systems (see the Oct 6 note
  above).

- Nonlinear systems (Nov 3-10): the lecture follows Robinson Sec. 32.4
  step by step (find the stationary points, linearise, local study,
  global picture). The notes use "critical point" and "stationary
  point" for the same thing, and call the linearisation theorem
  "Poincare"; the book names it Hartman-Grobman (Sec. 32.4.3) and its
  wording is the same as the "Key theorem" in the notes.

- Stability of a planar critical point (Nov 3): the definitions of
  stable, asymptotically stable and unstable for two dimensions (discs
  of radius r and R) and the trace-determinant diagram are not in the
  book. Robinson only gives the one-dimensional definition (Sec. 7.2,
  p. 48) and the list of linear types (Ch. 31, p. 301).

- Fourier series (Nov 10-12): Robinson stops at the ODEs (Ch. 38 only
  points to PDEs), so the PDE part uses Haberman (BookPDE's.pdf). The
  lecture's order is the book's: Sec. 2.3 (heat equation by separation
  of variables) first, then Ch. 3 (Fourier series, convergence,
  sine and cosine series, term-by-term differentiation).
