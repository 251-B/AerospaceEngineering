  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.1 (p. 270-271)
  - Case 1) two distinct real eigenvalues
  - General solution x(t) = A e^(lambda1 t) v1 + B e^(lambda2 t) v2,
    Eq. (28.1), with Example 28.1 (p. 270-271)
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.2 (p. 271-272) and Appendix B (p. 384)
  - Proof that the two solutions are linearly independent. The book
    states that eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly
    independent (p. 272) and proves it in Appendix B, "Linear
    independence of eigenvectors" (p. 384). Example 28.2 (p. 274-275)
    checks it directly: alpha v1 + beta v2 = 0 forces alpha = beta = 0
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.2 (p. 271-274)
  - Diagonalizable matrix and change of basis: write x = P x~ with
    P = [v1 v2], so x~ = P^(-1) x, Eq. (28.4)-(28.5). The equation
    becomes dx~/dt = P^(-1) A P x~ = diag(lambda1, lambda2) x~,
    Eq. (28.7)-(28.9), which is uncoupled. Example 28.2 (p. 274-276)
  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.3 (p. 276-278)

  [Oct 8]

Ch. 28, Sec. 28.4 (p. 279)

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.1 (p. 285-286)
  - Complex pair lambda = rho +/- i omega, eigenvectors
    eta = v1 +/- i v2. Real solution x(t) = 2 Re[C e^(lambda t) eta],
    Eq. (29.1), which expands to
    x(t) = e^(rho t)[(A cos wt + B sin wt) v1 + (B cos wt - A sin wt) v2],
    Eq. (29.2). Example 29.1 (A = [2 5; -2 0], lambda = 1 +/- 3i)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.2 (p. 287-288)
  - General solution via change of basis: taking real and imaginary
    parts of A eta = lambda eta gives A v1 = rho v1 - omega v2 and
    A v2 = omega v1 + rho v2, Eq. (29.3). With x = x~ v1 + y~ v2,
    Eq. (29.4), i.e. x~ = [v1 v2]^(-1) x, the system becomes
    dx~/dt = [rho omega; -omega rho] x~, Eq. (29.5). Example 29.2
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.2 (p. 289-291, Fig. 29.1-29.4)
  - Phase portrait in the new coordinates: in polar coordinates
    (Eq. 29.6-29.8) r' = rho r and theta' = -omega, so
    r = R e^(rho t), theta = T - omega t.
    rho < 0: stable spiral (Fig. 29.2); rho > 0: unstable spiral
    (Fig. 29.3); rho = 0: centre, closed orbits (Fig. 29.4)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 29, Sec. 29.3 (p. 291-293, Fig. 29.5-29.6)
  - Converting back to x: the type (spiral or centre) does not change;
    the sense of rotation is read from the sign of x' on the positive
    y-axis (x = 0, y > 0). Example 29.3, Eq. (29.9)
  [Oct 13]

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.1 (p. 295-296, Fig. 30.1)

  - Only one independent eigenvector v: decomposing A. Choose any v2

    y~ = A e^(lambda t) first, then x~ by the integrating factor

Ch. 30, Sec. 30.2, Eq. (30.4) (p. 297)

    x(t) = [A t e^(lambda t) + B e^(lambda t)] v1 + A e^(lambda t) v2,

======================================================================
NOTES ON DIFFERENCES BETWEEN LECTURE AND BOOK
======================================================================

- Order of presentation: the lecture runs ahead of the book in places.
  The mass-spring example (Sep 22) is in Ch. 13, after the Ch. 12
  solution methods, and logistic growth is in Ch. 8 although it was
  seen together with the Ch. 7 phase portraits.

- Terminology: the book never says "Picard's theorem" or "well-posed".
  It states the result as Theorem 6.2 and discusses existence and
  uniqueness in Sec. 6.1. The book calls the method "undetermined
  coefficients".

- Free fall: the book derives F = ma in Sec. 5.3 but treats free fall
  only as Exercise 5.6 (an apple falling from height h), so the
  derivation seen in class comes from Sec. 5.3.

- Variation of constants (Oct 1): Ch. 18 is a starred chapter, which
  the book marks as optional material. It assumes you already know two
  independent solutions of the homogeneous equation, so it builds on
  Ch. 11 and Ch. 17 (reduction of order). Ch. 18 has no numbered
  sections.

- Systems of ODEs (Sep 8 and Oct 6): the book only mentions them in
  passing in Ch. 3. Their treatment is Part V (Ch. 25 onwards), which
  the lecture reached on Oct 6.

- Systems (Oct 6): the lecture develops the theory of linear systems
  (Wronskian, fundamental set, homogeneous + particular) in a general
  way, which the book does not do. Robinson is concerned with two
  equations only (n = 2) and moves quickly to explicit solutions and
  phase portraits. Ch. 25 is starred (optional in the book), and
  Ch. 26 has no numbered sections.

- Order of the two solution methods: the book presents "convert to a
  second order ODE" first (Ch. 26) and the eigenvalue method after it
  (Ch. 27-28), the same order as the lecture. Ch. 27 only sets up the
  eigenvalue problem; the explicit solutions by eigenvectors are in
  Ch. 28 (distinct real), Ch. 29 (complex) and Ch. 30 (repeated).

- Eigenvalues and phase portraits (Oct 8-15): the lecture has covered
  the three cases, distinct real (Ch. 28), complex (Ch. 29) and
  repeated (Ch. 30). Not seen yet: the summary of phase portraits and
  the Jordan form (Ch. 31) and Sec. 28.5 on stable and unstable
  manifolds (p. 281).

- Change of basis (Oct 13, Oct 15): the lecture writes z = Q^(-1) x;
  the book writes x~ = [v1 v2]^(-1) x, with v1, v2 the real and
  imaginary parts of the eigenvector (Ch. 29) or v1 = (A - lambda I) v2
  and v2 any vector not parallel to the eigenvector (Ch. 30). The
  canonical matrices are [rho omega; -omega rho] (Eq. 29.5) and
  [lambda 1; 0 lambda] (Eq. 30.2); if Q orders its columns differently
  the off-diagonal entries move or change sign.

- Exercises: most items of the course sheets copy Robinson's
  end-of-chapter exercises (same items and data), so the book's
  surrounding sections are the reading for each class exercise. The
  items not taken from the book are listed as "not in the book" in
  the exercises table.

- Terminology (Oct 8): the book never says "diagonalizable" or
  "triangular". It describes the same ideas as "changing coordinates"
  (Sec. 28.2) and as matrices of the form [lambda1 b; 0 lambda2] (the
  upper-triangular case, Example 27.3). It writes the new variable as
  x~ (x with a tilde) where the lecture uses z, so z = P^(-1) x
  corresponds to x~ = P^(-1) x, Eq. (28.5).

- "Saddle node": the book only says "saddle point" or "saddle". The
  word "node" is reserved for stable and unstable nodes (Sec. 28.3),
  so "saddle node" in the lecture notes is probably "saddle".

- Linear independence (Oct 8): the book proves independence of the
  eigenvectors, not of the vector solutions e^(lambda t) v directly,
  and it does not use the Wronskian for systems (see the Oct 6 note
  above).
