Subchapter 3.5 · Thermodynamics & Energy Balances

Total Energy Conservation & Thermodynamic Applications

First Law of Thermodynamics for moving control volumes, enthalpy grouping, stagnation enthalpy $h_0$, and engineering shaft work.

1. The Master Total Energy Conservation Equation

The First Law of Thermodynamics for a closed material fluid system $V_f(t)$ states that the rate of increase of total energy (internal energy $e$ plus macroscopic kinetic energy $v^2/2$) equals the net mechanical power done on the fluid plus the net rate of heat transferred into the fluid:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho\left(e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right) dV\right] = \dot{W}_{\text{ext}} + \dot{Q} $$
[Eq. 3.45] First Law of Thermodynamics for a closed material fluid system.

Substituting intensive total energy $b = e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}$ into the universal Reynolds Transport Theorem yields the Master Integral Total Energy Equation for any moving control volume $V_c(t)$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho\left(e+\frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right)dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)}\rho\left(e+\frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right)[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}]\,d\sigma = \dot{W}_{\text{ext}} + \dot{Q}} $$
[Eq. 3.46] Master Integral Total Energy Conservation Equation.

Here, the external power $\dot{W}_{\text{ext}}$ done on the fluid by boundary forces and body forces breaks down into:

$$ \dot{W}_{\text{ext}} = -\int_{\Sigma_c} p(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n})\,d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c} \vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}\,d\sigma + \int_{V_c} \rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v}\,dV + \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} $$

and total thermal heat transfer $\dot{Q}$ combines boundary conduction (Fourier's law $\vec{q} = -k\nabla T$) and internal sources (chemical combustion $Q_c$ and radiative absorption $Q_r$):

$$ \dot{Q} = -\int_{\Sigma_c} \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}\,d\sigma + \int_{V_c} (Q_c + Q_r)\,dV $$

2. Flow Work, Enthalpy, and Stagnation Enthalpy ($h_0$)

A crucial step in fluid thermodynamics is recognizing that the normal pressure work term $-\int_{\Sigma_c} p(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}) d\sigma$ can be written as:

$$ -\int_{\Sigma_c} p(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_c} \frac{p}{\rho} \, \rho (\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma $$

Moving this "flow work" (the work done by pressure pushing fluid packets across the control surface) to the left-hand side of the energy balance groups it seamlessly with internal energy $e$ to define specific enthalpy $h$:

$$ h = e + \frac{p}{\rho} $$

Combining specific enthalpy with kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy $gz$ defines the Stagnation (Total) Enthalpy $h_0$:

$$ \mathbf{h_0 = h + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2} + gz} $$

For a calorically perfect gas ($h = c_p T$), the stagnation enthalpy is directly proportional to the stagnation temperature $T_0$:

$$ h_0 = c_p T_0 = c_p T + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2} $$

The Engineering Energy Equation for 1D Streams

For steady flow through devices with discrete inlets and outlets, negligible viscous work at open ports, and steady shaft work $\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}}$ (from pumps, compressors, or turbines):

$$ \mathbf{\dot{Q} + \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = \sum_j \dot{m}_{\text{out}, j} h_{0,\text{out}, j} - \sum_k \dot{m}_{\text{in}, k} h_{0,\text{in}, k}} $$
Special Cases in Aerospace Propulsion
  • Adiabatic Duct ($\dot{Q} = 0, \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = 0$): Stagnation enthalpy is strictly conserved: $h_{0,\text{out}} = h_{0,\text{in}}$. Kinetic energy increases only at the expense of static temperature ($T_{\text{out}} < T_{\text{in}}$).
  • Combustor ($\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = 0$): Combustion heat release raises stagnation enthalpy: $\Delta h_0 = q_{\text{combustion}}$.
  • Compressor ($\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} > 0$): Mechanical work input raises total pressure and stagnation temperature: $w_{\text{in}} = c_p(T_{0,\text{out}} - T_{0,\text{in}})$.
  • Turbine ($\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} < 0$): Fluid delivers shaft power to drive the engine fan/compressor: $w_{\text{out}} = c_p(T_{0,\text{in}} - T_{0,\text{out}})$.

Mixing Elbow Stream Exit Temperature $T_s$

Physical Setup: Two streams of an incompressible liquid (density $\rho$, specific heat $c_p$) enter an adiabatic, rigid mixing tee or elbow ($\dot{Q} = 0, \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = 0$). Stream 1 enters with mass flow rate $\dot{m}_1$ at temperature $T_1$ and speed $v_1$. Stream 2 enters with mass flow rate $\dot{m}_2$ at temperature $T_2$ and speed $v_2$. The mixed fluid exhausts into the atmosphere at state $s$ through an exit nozzle of area $A_s$.

Objective: Determine the exit mixed temperature $T_s$.

1Mass Balance

From steady continuity:

$$ \dot{m}_s = \dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2 $$

The exit discharge velocity is $v_s = \frac{\dot{m}_s}{\rho A_s}$.

2Total Energy Balance

For an adiabatic rigid mixer without shaft work ($\dot{Q} = 0, \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = 0$):

$$ \sum \dot{m}_{\text{in}} h_{0,\text{in}} = \dot{m}_s h_{0,s} \implies \dot{m}_1 h_{0,1} + \dot{m}_2 h_{0,2} = (\dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2) h_{0,s} $$

Expanding the stagnation enthalpies ($h_0 = c_p T + \frac{v^2}{2}$):

$$ \dot{m}_1 \left(c_p T_1 + \frac{v_1^2}{2}\right) + \dot{m}_2 \left(c_p T_2 + \frac{v_2^2}{2}\right) = (\dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2) \left(c_p T_s + \frac{v_s^2}{2}\right) $$
3Solve for Exit Temperature $T_s$

In low-speed liquid flow, kinetic energy differences are negligible compared to thermal energy ($v^2/2 \ll c_p T$). The energy equation reduces to the weighted calorimetric average:

$$ \mathbf{T_s = \frac{\dot{m}_1 T_1 + \dot{m}_2 T_2}{\dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2}} $$

When discharge kinetic energy is non-negligible (high-speed nozzle discharge):

$$ \mathbf{T_s = \frac{\dot{m}_1 T_1 + \dot{m}_2 T_2}{\dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2} + \frac{\dot{m}_1 v_1^2 + \dot{m}_2 v_2^2 - (\dot{m}_1+\dot{m}_2)v_s^2}{2 c_p (\dot{m}_1 + \dot{m}_2)}} $$

Ventilation Fan Nozzle Shaft Power $\dot{W}$

Physical Setup: An electric ventilation fan inside an adiabatic duct takes ambient air ($\rho, p_a, T_a$) from rest ($v_1 \approx 0$) and accelerates it through a contracting nozzle discharging into the room at high exit speed $v_e$, area $A_e$, at ambient pressure $p_e = p_a$. Total pressure loss due to friction across duct screens is $\Delta p_{\text{loss}}$.

Objective: Calculate the minimum mechanical shaft power $\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}}$ that must be supplied by the electric motor.

AnalysisEnergy Balance from Inlet to Exit

Mass flow rate through the nozzle: $\dot{m} = \rho A_e v_e$.

Applying the engineering energy equation across the fan and nozzle ($\dot{Q} = 0$, $z_1 \approx z_e$):

$$ \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = \dot{m}(h_{0,e} - h_{0,1}) = \dot{m}\left[ \left(h_e + \frac{v_e^2}{2}\right) - \left(h_1 + 0\right) \right] $$

For an incompressible air flow ($\rho = \text{const}$), enthalpy changes arise from internal energy generation via viscous dissipation ($e_e - e_1 = \Delta p_{\text{loss}}/\rho$) and pressure differences ($p_e = p_1 = p_a \implies \Delta(p/\rho) = 0$):

$$ h_e - h_1 = (e_e - e_1) + \frac{p_e - p_1}{\rho} = \frac{\Delta p_{\text{loss}}}{\rho} $$

Substituting into the shaft power expression:

$$ \dot{W}_{\text{shaft}} = \dot{m} \left( \frac{v_e^2}{2} + \frac{\Delta p_{\text{loss}}}{\rho} \right) = \mathbf{Q \left( \frac{1}{2}\rho v_e^2 + \Delta p_{\text{loss}} \right)} $$

where $Q = A_e v_e$ is the volumetric airflow rate ($\text{m}^3/\text{s}$).

Physical Energy Breakdown
The fan shaft power is partitioned into two clear physical duties:
  1. Kinetic energy flux ($\frac{1}{2}\rho Q v_e^2$): The rate of kinetic energy carried away by the high-speed discharge jet.
  2. Viscous dissipation power ($Q \Delta p_{\text{loss}}$): The mechanical work converted irrecoverably into microscopic internal thermal energy due to friction across fan blades and duct screens.
