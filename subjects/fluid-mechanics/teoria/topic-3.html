1. Introduction and the Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT)

In classical mechanics, the conservation laws of mass, linear momentum, angular momentum, and energy are formulated for closed material systems: invariant collections of matter continually formed by the exact same particles. In fluid mechanics, a material system occupies a deformable volume $V_f(t)$ bounded by a material surface $\Sigma_f(t)$ that moves and distorts continuously with the local flow.

Let $\Phi(t)$ denote any total extensive property of the fluid (total mass, momentum, or energy). This property is evaluated by integrating its associated volumetric density $\phi(\vec{x}, t)$ over the material volume:

$$ \Phi(t) = \int_{V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x},t) \, dV \qquad \text{with } \phi = \rho, \; \rho\vec{v}, \; \rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}], \; \rho\left(e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right) $$
[Eq. 3.1] Definition of a global extensive property from the intensive property $\phi(\vec{x},t)$.

Because both the integrand $\phi(\vec{x},t)$ and the spatial boundaries of the domain $V_f(t)$ vary simultaneously with time $t$, the global time derivative $\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$ does not coincide with the integral of the local partial derivative $\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}$. To evaluate it with mathematical rigor, we apply the analytical limit definition:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x},t) \, dV\right] = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[ \int_{V_f(t+\Delta t)} \phi(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) \, dV - \int_{V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x}, t) \, dV \right] $$
[Eq. 3.2] Formal limit definition of the time derivative of a material fluid volume.

Decomposing the domain at time $t+\Delta t$ into the original volume $V_f(t)$ and the incremental region swept by the moving boundary $V_f(t+\Delta t) - V_f(t)$, the limit splits into two independent physical contributions:

$$ \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t}\left[ \int_{V_f(t)} [\phi(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) - \phi(\vec{x}, t)] \, dV + \int_{V_f(t+\Delta t) - V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) \, dV \right] $$
[Eq. 3.3] Splitting into internal storage and moving-boundary terms.

Evaluating each term separately:

  1. Unsteadiness contribution: With $V_f(t)$ held fixed during the limiting process, applying a Taylor series expansion $\phi(t+\Delta t) - \phi(t) = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\Delta t + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$ yields:
    $$ \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \int_{V_f(t)} [\phi(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) - \phi(\vec{x}, t)] \, dV = \int_{V_f(t)} \frac{\partial \phi(\vec{x},t)}{\partial t} \, dV $$
    [Eq. 3.4] Intrinsic rate of change within the instantaneous volume.
  2. Fluid motion contribution (Convective sweeping): Over the infinitesimal interval $\Delta t$, a surface element $d\sigma$ of $\Sigma_f(t)$ with outward unit normal $\vec{n}$ moves at the fluid velocity $\vec{v}$. The volume swept out corresponds to an elementary oblique cylinder with base $d\sigma$ and height $(\vec{v}\Delta t)\cdot\vec{n}$, such that $dV = (\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n})\Delta t \, d\sigma$. Integrating across the entire closed surface $\Sigma_f(t)$:
    $$ \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \int_{V_f(t+\Delta t) - V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) \, dV = \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \phi \, \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma $$
    [Eq. 3.5] Net convective flux leaving the deformable boundary.

Summing both contributions yields the canonical form of the Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) for a material fluid volume:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \phi(\vec{x},t) \, dV\right] = \int_{V_f(t)} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} \, dV + \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \phi \, \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma} $$
[Eq. 3.6] Reynolds Transport Theorem for a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.

Extension to an Arbitrary Moving Control Volume $V_c(t)$

In aerospace engineering, one rarely tracks an individual fluid mass stretching downstream of a rocket engine or turbine blade. Instead, one defines a geometric control volume $V_c(t)$ bounded by a control surface $\Sigma_c(t)$ moving at an arbitrary, known spatial velocity $\vec{v}_c(\vec{x}, t)$. Applying the same derivation to $V_c(t)$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \phi(\vec{x},t) \, dV\right] = \int_{V_c(t)} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} \, dV + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \phi \, \vec{v}_c\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma $$
[Eq. 3.7] Rate of change of $\Phi$ stored within the geometric control volume $V_c(t)$.

Matching the control volume with the material volume instantaneously at time $t$ ($V_c(t) \equiv V_f(t)$ and $\Sigma_c(t) \equiv \Sigma_f(t)$), the volume integrals of $\partial\phi/\partial t$ in (3.6) and (3.7) are identical. Subtracting equation (3.7) from (3.6) yields the universal Reynolds transport relation:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \phi \, dV\right] = \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \phi \, dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \phi \, (\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma} $$
[Eq. 3.8] Universal Reynolds equation: material derivative expressed in terms of the moving control volume.
Universal Physical Interpretation of RTT
The material derivative of any extensive property contained within the fluid system $V_f(t)$ equals the time rate of storage within the control volume $V_c(t)$ plus the net convective flux escaping through the control surface $\Sigma_c(t)$, carried by the relative fluid velocity with respect to the control boundary $(\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)$.

2. Integral Conservation of Mass (Continuity)

In non-relativistic Newtonian mechanics, the mass of a closed material fluid system is neither created nor destroyed:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho \, dV\right] = 0 $$
[Eq. 3.9] Fundamental principle of mass conservation for a material fluid volume.

Substituting $\phi = \rho$ into the universal RTT (Eq. 3.8) yields the integral mass conservation equation for an arbitrary moving control volume $V_c(t)$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho \, dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \rho \, (\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = 0} $$
[Eq. 3.10] Integral continuity equation for a moving control volume $V_c(t)$.

Control Volume Fixed in Space ($V_0, \vec{v}_c = 0$)

For a rigid control volume fixed in the frame of reference ($\vec{v}_c \equiv 0$ and $V_c(t) \equiv V_0$), the exterior ordinary derivative becomes the local partial derivative inside the volume integral:

$$ \mathbf{\int_{V_0} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \, dV + \int_{\Sigma_0} \rho \, \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = 0} $$
[Eq. 3.11] Continuity equation for a fixed control volume $V_0$.

Two essential practical simplifications follow directly from this equation:

  • Steady state ($\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$): Net outgoing mass flow rate equals net incoming mass flow rate: $$ \int_{\Sigma_0} \rho \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = 0 \implies \sum \dot{m}_{\text{out}} = \sum \dot{m}_{\text{in}} $$
  • Incompressible fluid ($\rho = \text{constant}$): Density factors out of the integral, reducing to volumetric flow rate conservation: $$ \int_{\Sigma_0} \vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = 0 \implies \sum Q_{\text{out}} = \sum Q_{\text{in}} $$

3. Body (Mass) and Surface Forces

The dynamic force balance governing fluid acceleration requires distinguishing between long-range actions at a distance and contact forces:

A. Body or Mass Forces ($\vec{f}_m$) — Long Range

Possess an effective range vastly exceeding the intermolecular spacing $d$. They act on the bulk of every fluid particle of mass $dm = \rho dV$:

$$ d\vec{F}_m = \rho \vec{f}_m(\vec{x},t) \, dV $$
[Eq. 3.12] Infinitesimal body force acting on the fluid element.

In the general framework of a non-inertial reference frame undergoing translational acceleration of its origin $\vec{a}_0(t)$ and angular rotation $\vec{\Omega}(t)$, the specific body force $\vec{f}_m$ incorporates gravity alongside the classical inertial forces:

$$ \mathbf{\vec{f}_m(\vec{x},t) = \vec{g} - \vec{a}_0 - \frac{d\vec{\Omega}}{dt}\wedge\vec{x} - \vec{\Omega}\wedge(\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{x}) - 2\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{v}} $$
[Eq. 3.13] Net body force in a non-inertial frame: gravity + translational inertia + angular acceleration + centrifugal force + Coriolis force.

When the non-inertial frame undergoes steady rotation and constant translation ($\vec{a}_0 = \text{const}$, $\vec{\Omega} = \text{const}$), gravity, translational inertia, and centrifugal forces are conservative and derive from a unified scalar potential $U(\vec{x})$:

$$ \vec{g} - \vec{a}_0 - \vec{\Omega}\wedge(\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{x}) = -\nabla\left[ -\vec{g}\cdot\vec{x} + \vec{a}_0\cdot\vec{x} - \frac{1}{2}|\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{x}|^2 \right] = -\nabla U $$
[Eq. 3.14] Gradient of the unified potential of steady conservative body forces.

B. Surface Forces — Short Range

Arise from molecular collisions and intermolecular electromagnetic interactions at the scale $d \sim 10^{-9}\text{ m}$. Across an area element $d\sigma$ with outward unit normal $\vec{n}$, the fluid on the side toward which $\vec{n}$ points exerts a contact force:

$$ d\vec{F}_s = \vec{f}_n(\vec{n}, \vec{x}, t) \, d\sigma $$
[Eq. 3.15] Surface stress vector $\vec{f}_n$ (force per unit area, SI units $\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2$).

4. The Cauchy Stress Tensor

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved that the directional dependence of the stress vector $\vec{f}_n$ on the unit normal $\vec{n} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)$ is strictly linear. Consider an infinitesimal tetrahedron with three faces perpendicular to the coordinate axes having areas $dA_1, dA_2, dA_3$ and outward normals $-\vec{e}_1, -\vec{e}_2, -\vec{e}_3$, and an inclined face of area $dA$ with arbitrary outward unit normal $\vec{n}$:

$$ dA \, \vec{f}_n - dA_1 \vec{f}_1 - dA_2 \vec{f}_2 - dA_3 \vec{f}_3 = \rho dV \left(\frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} - \vec{f}_m\right) \to 0 $$
[Eq. 3.16] Dynamic equilibrium on Cauchy's tetrahedron as $h \to 0$ (surface area $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ dominates over volume $\mathcal{O}(h^3)$).

Using the geometric projections $dA_i = n_i dA$ and dividing by the inclined face area $dA$:

$$ \mathbf{\vec{f}_n = n_1 \vec{f}_1 + n_2 \vec{f}_2 + n_3 \vec{f}_3 = \vec{n}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}} = \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n}} $$
[Eq. 3.17] Cauchy's stress theorem: the stress vector is the projection of the stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}$.

Where the matrix of the Cauchy Stress Tensor is defined by its 9 components:

$$ \bar{\bar{\tau}} = \begin{bmatrix} \tau_{11} & \tau_{12} & \tau_{13} \\ \tau_{21} & \tau_{22} & \tau_{23} \\ \tau_{31} & \tau_{32} & \tau_{33} \end{bmatrix} $$
[Eq. 3.18] Stress tensor: $\tau_{ij}$ represents the force per unit area in direction $\vec{e}_j$ acting on a face with unit normal $\vec{e}_i$.

Symmetry of the Stress Tensor ($\tau_{ij} = \tau_{ji}$)

Evaluating angular momentum conservation for an infinitesimal cube $dx_1 dx_2 dx_3$ about its center of mass reveals that the moment of inertia and body forces scale as $\mathcal{O}(dx^5)$ and $\mathcal{O}(dx^4)$, whereas the torque produced by shear stresses scales as $(\tau_{12} - \tau_{21}) dx_1 dx_2 dx_3 \sim \mathcal{O}(dx^3)$. In the limit $dx \to 0$, preventing unbounded angular accelerations dictates that:

$$ \mathbf{\tau_{ij} = \tau_{ji} \implies \bar{\bar{\tau}} = \bar{\bar{\tau}}^T} \qquad \text{and therefore} \qquad \vec{f}_n = \begin{bmatrix} \tau_{11} & \tau_{12} & \tau_{13} \\ \tau_{12} & \tau_{22} & \tau_{23} \\ \tau_{13} & \tau_{23} & \tau_{33} \end{bmatrix}\cdot\vec{n} $$
[Eq. 3.19] Rigorous symmetry of the Cauchy stress tensor in the absence of distributed body couples.

Principal Directions and Principal Stresses

There exist three privileged spatial orientations along which the stress vector is strictly collinear with the normal ($\vec{f}_n \parallel \vec{n}$), causing shear stresses to vanish identically:

$$ \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n} = \lambda \vec{n} \qquad \text{[Eq. 3.20]} \qquad \iff \qquad |\bar{\bar{\tau}} - \lambda \bar{\bar{I}}| = 0 \qquad \text{[Eq. 3.21]} $$
[Eqs. 3.20–3.21] Eigenvalue problem and secular characteristic equation.

By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, the three principal stresses $(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)$ are strictly real, and their corresponding principal directions $(\vec{n}_1, \vec{n}_2, \vec{n}_3)$ are mutually orthogonal.

Global Surface Force and Cauchy Divergence

The global surface force acting on a closed surface $\Sigma$ enclosing volume $V$ is converted via the Gauss divergence theorem:

$$ \vec{F}_s = \int_\Sigma \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}) \, dV $$
[Eqs. 3.22–3.23] The divergence $\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}$ represents the net resultant surface force per unit volume.

Applying Newton's second law to an infinitesimal element $dV$ yields Cauchy's differential equation of motion:

$$ \rho dV \frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} = (\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}})dV + \rho\vec{f}_m dV \quad \implies \quad \mathbf{\rho \frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}} + \rho\vec{f}_m} $$
[Eqs. 3.24–3.25] Cauchy's equation of motion for any continuum medium.

5. The Navier-Poisson Constitutive Equation

To achieve dynamic closure of the equations of motion, the stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}$ must be linked to flow kinematics ($\vec{v}$) and the thermodynamic state ($p, T$).

Hydrostatic State vs. Fluid in Motion

In a fluid at rest or undergoing rigid-body motion ($\bar{\bar{T}}_d = 0$), shear stresses vanish identically, and the surface force is isotropic and purely compressive:

$$ \vec{f}_n = -p\vec{n} \quad \implies \quad \bar{\bar{\tau}} = -p\bar{\bar{I}} $$
[Eqs. 3.26–3.27] Hydrostatic stress tensor governed by the scalar thermodynamic pressure $p$.

In a flow with relative strain ($\bar{\bar{T}}_d \neq 0$), viscous friction generates additional stresses. The stress tensor is universally decomposed as:

$$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}} = -p\bar{\bar{I}} + \bar{\bar{\tau}}'} $$
[Ec. 3.28] Decomposition into hydrostatic spherical part and viscous stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}'$.

Isotropic Newtonian Fluid Hypothesis

Under the hypothesis of constitutive linearity ($\tau'_{ij} = \alpha_{ijkl}\gamma_{kl}$, Eq. 3.29) and spatial isotropy, the viscous stress tensor depends exclusively on two viscosity coefficients:

$$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu \bar{\bar{T}}_d + \lambda (\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}}} $$
[Eq. 3.30] Navier-Poisson relation in terms of dynamic shear viscosity $\mu$ and second viscosity $\lambda$.

Introducing the bulk (volumetric) viscosity $\mu_B = \lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu$, the constitutive equation assumes its canonical form:

$$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu \bar{\bar{T}}_d + \left(\mu_B - \frac{2}{3}\mu\right)(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}}} $$
[Eq. 3.31] Canonical Navier-Poisson constitutive equation.
Stokes Hypothesis ($\mu_B = 0$) and Incompressible Flow
  • Stokes Hypothesis (1845): Assumes $\mu_B \approx 0 \implies \lambda = -\frac{2}{3}\mu$. It is rigorously exact for monatomic gases and an extensively validated engineering standard in subsonic and supersonic aerodynamics.
  • Incompressible Flow ($\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$): The volumetric dilation term vanishes identically, reducing to: $$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu \bar{\bar{T}}_d} $$

Thermal Behavior: Air vs. Water

Dynamic viscosity $\mu(T)$ depends fundamentally on temperature:

  • Gases (Air): Viscous momentum transport occurs via molecular momentum exchange during thermal collisions ($v_{\text{th}}\propto \sqrt{T}$). Hence, $\mu$ increases with $T$. At $288\text{ K}$, $\mu_a \approx 1.78\times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.
  • Liquids (Water): Momentum transport is governed by cohesive intermolecular forces. As $T$ rises, thermal energy breaks molecular bonds and $\mu$ drops sharply. At $288\text{ K}$, $\mu_w \approx 1.14\times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.
Fluid Density $\rho$ ($288\text{ K}$) Dynamic Viscosity $\mu$ Kinematic Viscosity $\nu = \mu/\rho$ Temperature Dependence
Atmospheric Air $1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$ $1.78 \times 10^{-5}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ $\mathbf{1.45 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$ $\mu$ increases with temperature ($\propto T^{1/2}$ / Sutherland)
Liquid Water $1000\text{ kg/m}^3$ $1.14 \times 10^{-3}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ $\mathbf{1.14 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2/\text{s}}$ $\mu$ decreases exponentially with temperature

Note that the kinematic viscosity of air is nearly 13 times higher than that of water, diffusing momentum per unit mass considerably faster!

6. Forces and Moments on Submerged Bodies

When an aerodynamic body (airfoil, fuselage, turbine blade, or missile) is immersed in a moving fluid, the total aerodynamic action exerted by the fluid on its wetted surface $\Sigma$ arises solely from the distribution of pressure and viscous shear stresses.

Taking the outward unit normal vector $\vec{n}$ pointing into the fluid (out of the solid body):

$$ \mathbf{\vec{F} = -\int_\Sigma p\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_\Sigma \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma} $$
[Eq. 3.32] Net aerodynamic force: normal pressure integral + tangential viscous shear integral.
$$ \mathbf{\vec{M}_{\vec{x}_0} = -\int_\Sigma (\vec{x} - \vec{x}_0)\wedge(p\vec{n}) \, d\sigma + \int_\Sigma (\vec{x} - \vec{x}_0)\wedge(\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma} $$
[Eq. 3.33] Net aerodynamic moment about reduction point $\vec{x}_0$.

Projecting $\vec{F}$ onto relative wind axes directly yields aerodynamic lift $L$ (perpendicular to $\vec{v}_\infty$) and aerodynamic drag $D$ (parallel to $\vec{v}_\infty$), the latter splitting into pressure drag (form/wave) and skin-friction drag.

7. Integral Conservation of Linear Momentum

Newton's second law postulates that the time rate of change of the total linear momentum of a closed material fluid system $V_f(t)$ equals the resultant of all external forces (pressure, viscous stresses, and body forces):

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho\vec{v} \, dV\right] = -\int_{\Sigma_f(t)} p\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_f(t)} \rho\vec{f}_m \, dV $$
[Eq. 3.34] Linear momentum balance for a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.

Substituting $\phi = \rho\vec{v}$ into the Reynolds Transport Theorem (Eq. 3.8) yields the integral linear momentum equation for an arbitrary moving control volume $V_c(t)$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho\vec{v} \, dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \rho\vec{v}[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] \, d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_c(t)} p\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_c(t)} \rho\vec{f}_m \, dV} $$
[Eq. 3.35] Integral linear momentum conservation equation for a moving control volume $V_c(t)$.

Control Volume Fixed in Space ($V_0, \vec{v}_c = 0$)

$$ \mathbf{\int_{V_0} \frac{\partial(\rho\vec{v})}{\partial t} \, dV + \int_{\Sigma_0} \rho\vec{v}(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_0} p\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_0} \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_0} \rho\vec{f}_m \, dV} $$
[Eq. 3.36] Linear momentum conservation for a fixed control volume $V_0$.

Physical Breakdown of the 5 Terms in the Momentum Equation

  1. $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_c} \rho\vec{v} dV$ (Local Inertia / Storage): Time variation of momentum stored within the control cavity. Identically zero in steady state.
  2. $\int_{\Sigma_c} \rho\vec{v}[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] d\sigma$ (Net Outgoing Convective Flux): Net rate of momentum efflux across open inlet and outlet control boundaries carried by the relative mass flow.
  3. $-\int_{\Sigma_c} p\vec{n} d\sigma$ (Net External Pressure Force): Distributed normal compressive force over the control boundaries. For problems vented to ambient atmosphere, gauge pressure $(p - p_a)$ can be used directly.
  4. $\int_{\Sigma_c} \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} d\sigma$ (Net Viscous Force): Tangential friction shear stresses acting along wetted solid walls.
  5. $\int_{V_c} \rho\vec{f}_m dV$ (Body Forces): Total fluid weight within the volume ($\int \rho\vec{g} dV$) plus fictitious inertial forces if the reference frame is non-inertial.

8. Integral Conservation of Angular Momentum (Moment of Momentum)

The angular momentum balance about a fixed reference point $\vec{x}_0$ for a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$ states:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v} \, dV\right] = -\int_{\Sigma_f(t)} (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(p\vec{n}) \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma + \int_{V_f(t)} \rho(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{f}_m \, dV $$
[Eq. 3.37] Angular momentum balance for a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.

Applying the Reynolds Transport Theorem yields the general formulation for a moving control volume $V_c(t)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}] \, dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}][(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] \, d\sigma =} \\ \mathbf{-\int_{\Sigma_c(t)} (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(p\vec{n}) \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma + \int_{V_c(t)} \rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{f}_m] \, dV} \end{aligned} $$
[Eq. 3.38] Integral angular momentum equation for a moving control volume $V_c(t)$.
Key Aerospace Application: Euler's Turbomachinery Equation
In gas turbine rotors and aircraft compressors rotating at angular velocity $\vec{\Omega} = \Omega \vec{e}_z$, the steady mechanical shaft torque depends solely on the mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ and the change in specific angular momentum between exit ($r_2, v_{\theta 2}$) and inlet ($r_1, v_{\theta 1}$): $$ T_{\text{shaft}} = \dot{m}(r_2 v_{\theta 2} - r_1 v_{\theta 1}) \implies \mathbf{w_{\text{Euler}} = \frac{\dot{W}_{\text{shaft}}}{\dot{m}} = u_2 v_{\theta 2} - u_1 v_{\theta 1}} $$ where $u = \Omega r$ is the blade peripheral velocity.

9. Conductive Heat Transfer and Fourier's Law

The conductive heat flow rate across an infinitesimal surface element $d\sigma$ with outward unit normal $\vec{n}$ is given by:

$$ d\dot{Q}_{\text{cond}} = q_n(\vec{n}, \vec{x}, t) \, d\sigma $$
[Eq. 3.39] Conductive heat flux density in the direction of the unit normal $\vec{n}$.

Applying energy conservation to Cauchy's infinitesimal tetrahedron in the limit $h \to 0$:

$$ q_n dA = q_1 dA_1 + q_2 dA_2 + q_3 dA_3 \quad \implies \quad \mathbf{q_n = \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}} $$
[Eqs. 3.40–3.41] The scalar heat flux $q_n$ is the projection of the heat flux density vector $\vec{q} = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$.

For a closed surface $\Sigma$ bounding volume $V$, the total conductive heat loss to the surroundings and its divergence theorem equivalent are:

$$ \dot{Q}_{\text{net}} = \int_\Sigma \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\vec{q}) \, dV $$
[Eqs. 3.42–3.43] $\nabla\cdot\vec{q}$ represents the conductive heat loss rate per unit volume.

Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction

Molecular heat transport obeys Joseph Fourier's linear phenomenological law:

$$ \mathbf{\vec{q} = -k \nabla T} $$
[Eq. 3.44] Fourier's Law: linear proportionality to temperature gradient $\nabla T$ (the negative sign ensures heat flows from hot to cold regions).

Thermal Diffusivity ($\alpha$) and Prandtl Number ($\mathrm{Pr}$)

Molecular thermal diffusivity is given by $\alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c_p}$ (SI units $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$). The ratio of momentum diffusivity ($\nu = \mu/\rho$) to thermal diffusivity ($\alpha$) defines the dimensionless Prandtl number:

$$ \mathbf{\mathrm{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha} = \frac{\mu c_p}{k}} $$
Canonical definition of the Prandtl number.
Fluid Type Order of Magnitude of $\mathrm{Pr}$ Underlying Physical Mechanism Aerospace Example
Gases (Air) $\mathbf{\approx 0.72}$ Same molecular kinetic transport mechanism. Viscous and thermal boundary layers have comparable thickness ($\delta_v \approx \delta_T$). Flow over airfoils, fuselages, and propulsion nozzles.
Liquid Water $\mathbf{1.8 - 8.1}$ Hydrogen-bonded molecular structure. Strong temperature sensitivity ($\mathrm{Pr}=8.14$ at $288\text{ K}$, $\mathrm{Pr}=1.82$ at $368\text{ K}$). Hydrodynamic towing tanks and cavitation testing.
Liquid Metals $\mathbf{\ll 1} \; (0.005 - 0.03)$ Free conduction electrons transport heat with extraordinary rapidity. Thermal diffusion vastly dominates over viscous diffusion ($\delta_T \gg \delta_v$). Compact cooling in space nuclear reactors.
Lubricating Oils $\mathbf{\gg 1} \; (100 - 10,000)$ Large organic molecules with high internal friction and low thermal conductivity. Ultra-thin thermal boundary layer ($\delta_T \ll \delta_v$). High-speed shaft bearings in aviation turbojets.

10. Integral Conservation of Total Energy

The total energy per unit mass of a fluid particle comprises its microscopic internal energy $e$ and its macroscopic kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}|\vec{v}|^2$. In accordance with the First Law of Thermodynamics, the rate of change of energy in a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$ equals the mechanical power of external forces plus the net heat added:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho\left(e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right) dV\right] = & -\int_{\Sigma_f(t)} p\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_f(t)} \rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v} \, dV \\ & -\int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_f(t)} (Q_c + Q_r) \, dV \end{aligned} $$
[Eq. 3.45] First Law of Thermodynamics for a closed material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.

Applying the universal Reynolds Transport Theorem (Eq. 3.8) yields the master integral total energy conservation equation for an arbitrary moving control volume $V_c(t)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho\left(e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right) dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \rho\left(e + \frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2}\right)[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] \, d\sigma =} \\ \mathbf{-\int_{\Sigma_c(t)} p\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_c(t)} \rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v} \, dV} \\ \mathbf{-\int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n} \, d\sigma + \int_{V_c(t)} (Q_c + Q_r) \, dV} \end{aligned} $$
[Eq. 3.46] Master integral total energy conservation equation for a moving control volume $V_c(t)$.

Detailed Physical Breakdown of Energy Transfer Mechanisms

  • Unsteady storage: Rate of increase of internal and kinetic energy stored within $V_c(t)$.
  • Net outgoing convection: Convective energy flux across open boundaries with relative velocity $(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}$.
  • Flow pressure work ($-\int_{\Sigma_c} p\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n} d\sigma$): Mechanical power exerted by external pressure. Combined at flow cross-sections with convected internal energy $e$, this naturally gives rise to specific enthalpy $h = e + p/\rho$ and stagnation (total) enthalpy $h_0 = h + |\vec{v}|^2/2$.
  • Viscous stress work ($\int_{\Sigma_c} \vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} d\sigma$): Power transmitted by viscous shear across moving boundaries or dissipated within the boundary layer.
  • Body force work rate ($\int_{V_c} \rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v} dV$): Power developed by gravitational or inertial fields on the moving fluid.
  • Boundary heat conduction ($-\int_{\Sigma_c} \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n} d\sigma$): Heat conducted across solid boundaries according to Fourier's Law ($\vec{q} = -k\nabla T$).
  • Internal volumetric heat sources ($Q_c, Q_r$):
    • $Q_c$: Volumetric heat release rate from chemical reactions (combustion chambers, ramjets, afterburners).
    • $Q_r = -\nabla\cdot\vec{q}_r$: Net radiative heating or emission in high-temperature gases.

3.9 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)}\rho dV\right] = 0$ Fundamental principle of mass conservation for a closed material volume.
3.10 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)}\rho dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)}\rho(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma = 0$ Integral Mass Conservation for moving control volume $V_c(t)$.
3.11 $\int_{V_0}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}dV + \int_{\Sigma_0}\rho\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma = 0$ Integral mass conservation for fixed spatial control volume ($V_0$).
3.12 $d\vec{F}_m = \rho\vec{f}_m(\vec{x},t)dV$ Infinitesimal body (mass) force on a fluid particle.
3.13 $\vec{f}_m = \vec{g} - \vec{a}_0 - \frac{d\vec{\Omega}}{dt}\wedge\vec{x} - \vec{\Omega}\wedge(\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{x}) - 2\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{v}$ Net body force in non-inertial frame (gravity + 4 inertial forces).
3.14 $\vec{g} - \vec{a}_0 - \vec{\Omega}\wedge(\vec{\Omega}\wedge\vec{x}) = -\nabla U$ Unified potential $U$ for steady conservative body forces.
3.15 $d\vec{F}_s = \vec{f}_n(\vec{n}, \vec{x}, t)d\sigma$ Surface contact force: surface stress vector $\vec{f}_n$.
3.16 $dA\vec{f}_n - dA_1\vec{f}_1 - dA_2\vec{f}_2 - dA_3\vec{f}_3 = 0$ Force balance on Cauchy's tetrahedron in the limit $h \to 0$.
3.17 $\vec{f}_n = n_1\vec{f}_1 + n_2\vec{f}_2 + n_3\vec{f}_3 = \vec{n}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}} = \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n}$ Cauchy's Theorem: Linear projection of stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}$ onto normal $\vec{n}$.
3.18 $\bar{\bar{\tau}} = [\tau_{ij}]$ ($3\times 3$) Matrix representation of the Cauchy Stress Tensor.
3.19 $\tau_{ij} = \tau_{ji} \implies \bar{\bar{\tau}} = \bar{\bar{\tau}}^T$ Stress tensor symmetry via infinitesimal cube angular balance.
3.20 $\bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n} = \lambda\vec{n}$ Eigenvalue problem for principal directions and principal stresses (zero shear).
3.21 $|\bar{\bar{\tau}} - \lambda\bar{\bar{I}}| = 0$ Secular characteristic equation: guarantees three real, mutually orthogonal principal stresses.
3.22 $\vec{F}_s = \int_\Sigma \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma$ Global resultant surface force over control surface $\Sigma$.
3.23 $\int_\Sigma \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}})dV$ Gauss divergence theorem: $\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}$ is the net surface force per unit volume.
3.24 $\rho dV \frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} = (\nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}})dV + \rho\vec{f}_m dV$ Newton's 2nd law applied to differential fluid element $dV$.
3.25 $\rho \frac{D\vec{v}}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}} + \rho\vec{f}_m$ Cauchy's differential equation of motion for any continuum medium.
3.26 $\vec{f}_n = -p\vec{n}$ Purely normal hydrostatic stress in fluid at rest or in rigid-body motion.
3.27 $\bar{\bar{\tau}} = -p\bar{\bar{I}}$ Isotropic hydrostatic stress tensor.
3.28 $\bar{\bar{\tau}} = -p\bar{\bar{I}} + \bar{\bar{\tau}}'$ Decomposition of stress tensor into thermodynamic pressure and viscous stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}'$.
3.29 $\tau'_{ij} = \alpha_{ijkl}\gamma_{kl}$ Newtonian fluid hypothesis: linear constitutive relationship with strain rate.
3.30 $\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu\bar{\bar{T}}_d + \lambda(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}}$ Navier-Poisson equation with dynamic viscosity $\mu$ and second viscosity $\lambda$.
3.31 $\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu\bar{\bar{T}}_d + (\mu_B - \frac{2}{3}\mu)(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}}$ Canonical Navier-Poisson equation with bulk viscosity $\mu_B$ (Stokes: $\mu_B=0$).
3.32 $\vec{F} = -\int_\Sigma p\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_\Sigma \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma$ Net aerodynamic force on submerged body (pressure + skin friction).
3.33 $\vec{M}_{\vec{x}_0} = -\int_\Sigma (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(p\vec{n})d\sigma + \int_\Sigma (\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge(\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n})d\sigma$ Net aerodynamic moment on submerged body about $\vec{x}_0$.
3.34 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f}\rho\vec{v}dV\right] = -\int_{\Sigma_f}p\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_f}\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_f}\rho\vec{f}_m dV$ Newton's 2nd law for a closed material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.
3.35 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c}\rho\vec{v}dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c}\rho\vec{v}[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}]d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_c}p\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c}\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_c}\rho\vec{f}_m dV$ Integral Linear Momentum Conservation for moving control volume $V_c(t)$.
3.36 $\int_{V_0}\frac{\partial(\rho\vec{v})}{\partial t}dV + \int_{\Sigma_0}\rho\vec{v}(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n})d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_0}p\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_0}\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_0}\rho\vec{f}_m dV$ Linear momentum conservation for fixed spatial control volume ($V_0$).
3.37 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f}\rho(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}dV\right] = \sum \vec{M}_{\vec{x}_0, \text{ext}}$ Angular momentum conservation for material volume $V_f(t)$.
3.38 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c}\rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}]dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c}\rho[(\vec{x}-\vec{x}_0)\wedge\vec{v}][(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}]d\sigma = \sum \vec{M}_{\vec{x}_0, \text{ext}}$ Integral Angular Momentum Conservation for moving volume $V_c(t)$ (Turbomachinery).
3.39 $d\dot{Q}_{\text{cond}} = q_n(\vec{n}, \vec{x}, t)d\sigma$ Heat conduction per unit area in direction of normal $\vec{n}$.
3.40 $q_n dA = q_1 dA_1 + q_2 dA_2 + q_3 dA_3$ Thermal equilibrium in Cauchy's tetrahedron as $h \to 0$.
3.41 $q_n = \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}$ Heat flux density vector $\vec{q} = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$.
3.42 $\dot{Q}_{\text{cond}} = \int_\Sigma \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma$ Net conductive heat loss through finite surface $\Sigma$.
3.43 $\int_\Sigma \vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma = \int_V (\nabla\cdot\vec{q})dV$ Thermal Gauss theorem: $\nabla\cdot\vec{q}$ is heat loss per unit volume.
3.44 $\vec{q} = -k\nabla T$ Fourier's Law of heat conduction ($k$: thermal conductivity).
3.45 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f}\rho(e+\frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2})dV\right] = -\int_{\Sigma_f}p\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_f}\vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_f}\rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v}dV - \int_{\Sigma_f}\vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_f}(Q_c+Q_r)dV$ First Law of Thermodynamics for closed material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.
3.46 $\frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c}\rho(e+\frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2})dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c}\rho(e+\frac{|\vec{v}|^2}{2})[(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}]d\sigma = -\int_{\Sigma_c}p\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{\Sigma_c}\vec{v}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_c}\rho\vec{f}_m\cdot\vec{v}dV - \int_{\Sigma_c}\vec{q}\cdot\vec{n}d\sigma + \int_{V_c}(Q_c+Q_r)dV$ Integral Total Energy Conservation for moving control volume $V_c(t)$.
