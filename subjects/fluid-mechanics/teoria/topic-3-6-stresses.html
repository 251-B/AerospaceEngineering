Subchapter 3.6 · Viscous Stresses & Skin Friction

Stress Tensor, Navier-Poisson & Wall Shear Stress

Cauchy stress tensor decomposition, Newtonian constitutive relations, stress vector projection $\vec{f}_n = \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n}$, and friction drag integration.

1. Decomposition of the Cauchy Stress Tensor

In a fluid medium at rest (or moving as a rigid body without deformation), shear stresses are strictly zero and normal stresses are isotropic, characterized solely by the thermodynamic pressure $p$. The stress tensor is purely spherical:

$$ \bar{\bar{\tau}}_{\text{static}} = -p \bar{\bar{I}} $$
[Eq. 3.27] Isotropic hydrostatic stress tensor.

When the fluid undergoes continuous deformation ($\bar{\bar{T}}_d \neq \vec{0}$), molecular momentum diffusion gives rise to additional viscous stresses. The total Cauchy stress tensor decomposes universally into:

$$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}} = -p \bar{\bar{I}} + \bar{\bar{\tau}}'} $$
[Eq. 3.28] Decomposition into thermodynamic pressure $p$ and viscous deviatoric stress tensor $\bar{\bar{\tau}}'$.

By Cauchy's Stress Principle, the contact force per unit area (stress vector $\vec{f}_n$) acting on an imaginary or physical surface element with outward unit normal $\vec{n}$ is:

$$ \mathbf{\vec{f}_n = \bar{\bar{\tau}}\cdot\vec{n} = -p\vec{n} + \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}} $$
[Eq. 3.17] Stress vector on a surface with normal $\vec{n}$.
  • Normal stress: $\sigma_n = \vec{f}_n \cdot \vec{n} = -p + \vec{n}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n}$
  • Tangential (shear) stress vector: $\vec{\tau}_s = \vec{f}_n - \sigma_n \vec{n} = \bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n} - (\vec{n}\cdot\bar{\bar{\tau}}'\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n}$

2. The Navier-Poisson Constitutive Relations

For an isotropic Newtonian fluid (including air and water under standard conditions), viscous stress is linearly proportional to the rate-of-strain tensor $\bar{\bar{T}}_d$:

$$ \bar{\bar{T}}_d = \frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\vec{v} + (\nabla\vec{v})^T\right] \qquad \text{with } T_{d,ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i}\right) $$

The general isotropic linear tensor constitutive equation is the Navier-Poisson Equation:

$$ \bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu\bar{\bar{T}}_d + \lambda(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}} $$
[Eq. 3.30] Navier-Poisson relation with dynamic shear viscosity $\mu$ and second viscosity $\lambda$.

Expressing $\lambda$ in terms of the bulk (volume) viscosity $\mu_B = \lambda + \frac{2}{3}\mu$ yields the Canonical Navier-Poisson Equation:

$$ \mathbf{\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu\bar{\bar{T}}_d + \left(\mu_B - \frac{2}{3}\mu\right)(\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\bar{\bar{I}}} $$
[Eq. 3.31] Canonical Navier-Poisson equation.
Key Aerodynamic Simplifications

1. Incompressible Flow ($\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$): The volumetric dilatation term vanishes identically: $\bar{\bar{\tau}}' = 2\mu\bar{\bar{T}}_d$.

2. Stokes Hypothesis ($\mu_B \approx 0$): For dilute monatomic gases, internal molecular relaxation is negligible ($\mu_B = 0 \implies \lambda = -\frac{2}{3}\mu$).

3. Wall Shear Stress $\tau_w$ & Skin-Friction Drag

Consider a viscous boundary layer flowing in the $x$-direction over a solid flat wall located at $y = 0$ with outward unit normal into the fluid $\vec{n} = \hat{j}$.

By the no-slip condition, fluid adheres to the wall: $v_x(x, 0) = 0$ and $v_y(x, 0) = 0$. Along the solid wall, $\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x} \equiv 0$. The tangential viscous shear stress exerted on the wall simplifies to:

$$ \mathbf{\tau_w = \mu \left.\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\right|_{y=0}} $$

The total viscous skin-friction drag $D_f$ acting on a wetted surface of length $L$ and span $W$ is obtained by integrating the local wall shear stress:

$$ \mathbf{D_f = \int_\Sigma \tau_w \, d\sigma = W \int_0^L \tau_w(x) \, dx} $$

In aerospace aerodynamics, this is parameterized by the skin friction drag coefficient $C_f$:

$$ C_f = \frac{D_f}{\frac{1}{2}\rho v_\infty^2 (W L)} $$
Canonical Problem Walkthrough

Braking Torque in a Rotating Couette Viscometer

Physical Setup: A precision Couette (or Searle) rotational viscometer consists of two coaxial concentric cylinders of length $L$. The inner cylinder has radius $R_1$ and rotates at steady angular velocity $\Omega$. The outer cylinder has radius $R_2$ and is fixed. The annular gap between cylinders $h = R_2 - R_1$ is very narrow ($h \ll R_1$), filled with a liquid of unknown viscosity $\mu$.

Objective: Derive the viscous braking torque $\mathcal{M}$ required to maintain steady rotation, and express dynamic viscosity $\mu$ in terms of measurable laboratory quantities.

1Velocity Gradient across the Narrow Annular Gap

Because the gap is narrow ($h/R_1 \ll 1$), curvature effects can be neglected locally. The flow in the gap is an unrolled plane Couette flow with azimuthal velocity $v_\theta(r)$:

  • At the inner rotating cylinder ($r = R_1$): $v_\theta = \Omega R_1$.
  • At the outer stationary cylinder ($r = R_2 = R_1 + h$): $v_\theta = 0$.

The velocity varies linearly across the gap $y = r - R_1$:

$$ v_\theta(r) = \Omega R_1 \left(1 - \frac{r - R_1}{h}\right) $$

The transverse shear rate is uniform:

$$ \left|\frac{dv_\theta}{dr}\right| = \frac{\Omega R_1}{h} $$
2Wall Shear Stress on the Inner Cylinder

Applying Newton's law of viscosity at the inner cylinder surface $r = R_1$:

$$ \tau_w = \mu \left|\frac{dv_\theta}{dr}\right| = \mu \frac{\Omega R_1}{h} $$

This shear stress acts uniformly over the entire cylindrical wetted surface area $A = 2\pi R_1 L$, opposing rotation.

3Integrate Viscous Braking Torque $\mathcal{M}$

Each surface element $dA = R_1 d\theta dz$ experiences a friction force $dF = \tau_w dA$ with a lever arm $r = R_1$ from the rotation axis. Integrating over the surface:

$$ \mathcal{M} = \int_{A} R_1 \tau_w \, dA = R_1 \tau_w (2\pi R_1 L) = 2\pi R_1^2 L \tau_w $$

Substituting $\tau_w = \mu \frac{\Omega R_1}{h}$ yields the analytical Couette Viscometer Torque Formula:

$$ \mathbf{\mathcal{M} = \frac{2\pi \mu \Omega R_1^3 L}{h}} $$
4Inversion for Laboratory Viscosity Measurement

In the laboratory, measuring the applied torque $\mathcal{M}$ via a torsion wire or load cell and the angular velocity $\Omega$ directly determines the dynamic viscosity:

$$ \mathbf{\mu = \frac{\mathcal{M} h}{2\pi \Omega R_1^3 L}} $$
Aerospace Exam Insight: Mechanical Dissipation Power
The mechanical power consumed by the motor to overcome viscous friction is: $$ \dot{W}_{\text{diss}} = \Omega \mathcal{M} = \frac{2\pi \mu \Omega^2 R_1^3 L}{h} $$ By the First Law of Thermodynamics (Subchapter 3.5), this mechanical power is completely converted into heat by viscous dissipation inside the fluid gap, causing the lubricant's temperature to rise if cooling is not provided!
