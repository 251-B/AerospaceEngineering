Subchapter 3.1 · Fundamental Kinematics of Integral Systems

The Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) & Control Volume Selection Strategy

Converting Lagrangian material derivatives into Eulerian flux balances across fixed, moving, and deformable control boundaries.

1. The Reynolds Transport Theorem Master Formula

In continuum mechanics, physical conservation laws (conservation of mass, Newton's second law, and the first law of thermodynamics) are fundamentally formulated for a closed material system: a fixed collection of fluid particles enclosed within a time-dependent, deforming material volume $V_f(t)$ bounded by surface $\Sigma_f(t)$ that moves along with the local fluid velocity $\vec{v}$.

Let $B_{\text{sys}}$ denote any extensive property of this closed system (such as mass $m$, linear momentum $m\vec{v}$, angular momentum $\vec{H}_{\vec{x}_0}$, or total energy $E$), and let $b = dB/dm$ be the corresponding intensive property per unit mass. The total extensive property stored in the fluid volume is:

$$ B_{\text{sys}}(t) = \int_{V_f(t)} \rho b \, dV $$
[Eq. 3.1] Total extensive property $B_{\text{sys}}$ as the volume integral of volumetric density $\phi = \rho b$.

To differentiate this integral with respect to time when both the integrand $\rho b$ and the spatial domain $V_f(t)$ vary simultaneously, we apply the formal analytical limit definition:

$$ \frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \left[ \int_{V_f(t+\Delta t)} \rho b(\vec{x}, t+\Delta t) \, dV - \int_{V_f(t)} \rho b(\vec{x}, t) \, dV \right] $$
[Eq. 3.2] Analytical limit definition of the material time derivative.

Over the infinitesimal time interval $\Delta t$, the material boundary moves with fluid velocity $\vec{v}$. An elementary surface patch $d\sigma$ sweeps out a cylindrical volume of base $d\sigma$ and height $(\vec{v}\Delta t)\cdot\vec{n}$, yielding $dV = (\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n})\Delta t \, d\sigma$. Splitting the limit into local unsteadiness and boundary convective sweeping yields the classical RTT for a material volume:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_f(t)} \rho b \, dV\right] = \int_{V_f(t)} \frac{\partial(\rho b)}{\partial t} \, dV + \int_{\Sigma_f(t)} \rho b (\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}) \, d\sigma $$
[Eq. 3.6] Reynolds Transport Theorem for a material fluid volume $V_f(t)$.

Universal Extension to an Arbitrary Moving Control Volume $V_c(t)$

In aerospace propulsion and aerodynamics, tracking material fluid packages downstream of a jet engine is impossible. Instead, we define a geometric control volume $V_c(t)$ whose control surface $\Sigma_c(t)$ moves with an arbitrary, specified velocity field $\vec{v}_c(\vec{x}, t)$. Matching $V_c(t)$ with $V_f(t)$ instantaneously at time $t$ yields the Universal RTT Master Formula:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left[\int_{V_c(t)} \rho b \, dV\right] + \int_{\Sigma_c(t)} \rho b [(\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] \, d\sigma} $$
[Eq. 3.8] Universal Reynolds Transport Theorem with relative velocity $(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)$.
Physical Interpretation of the Two Terms

1. Rate of Storage / Unsteadiness: $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_c} \rho b \, dV$ measures how much of property $B$ accumulates or depletes inside the instantaneous geometric boundaries of $V_c(t)$.

2. Net Convective Flux: $\int_{\Sigma_c} \rho b [(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n}] \, d\sigma$ accounts for property $B$ carried across the boundary by the relative velocity $\vec{w} = \vec{v} - \vec{v}_c$ with which fluid crosses the control surface.

2. The Control Volume Toolbox: Selection Strategy

The entire art of integral fluid mechanics lies in choosing the optimal control volume $V_c$. The choice of $V_c$ does not change the physical outcome, but a clever choice reduces pages of vector calculus to a 2-line algebraic equation.

Fixed Control Volume $V_0, \vec{v}_c = \vec{0}$

Boundaries are fixed in the laboratory frame. Convective velocity is simply $\vec{v}$. Ideal for wind tunnels, stationary pipe bends, jet nozzles on testbenches, and steady heat exchangers.

Moving Control Volume $V_c(t), \vec{v}_c \neq \vec{0}$

Translates at constant speed $\vec{v}_c$ or accelerates with a vehicle (rockets, cruising aircraft, traveling shock waves, hydraulic bores). Fluxes are carried by relative velocity $\vec{w} = \vec{v} - \vec{v}_c$.

Deformable Control Volume $V_c(t), \vec{v}_c(\vec{x},t)$

Boundary velocity varies along the control surface. Essential for reciprocating internal combustion engine pistons, inflating airbags, expanding rocket propellant bladders, and flexible vessels.

Boundary Decomposition and Sign Conventions

A control surface $\Sigma_c$ is always partitioned into functional segments:

$$ \Sigma_c = \Sigma_{\text{in}} \cup \Sigma_{\text{out}} \cup \Sigma_{\text{wall}} \cup \Sigma_{\text{free}} $$
  • Outward unit normal $\vec{n}$: By universal convention, $\vec{n}$ is strictly outward-pointing from $V_c$ at every boundary element $d\sigma$.
  • Inlets ($\Sigma_{\text{in}}$): Fluid enters the volume against the normal vector: $(\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n} < 0$. This negative sign automatically ensures that inflow increases internal storage!
  • Outlets ($\Sigma_{\text{out}}$): Fluid leaves the volume along the normal vector: $(\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n} > 0$.
  • Impermeable Walls ($\Sigma_{\text{wall}}$): Fluid cannot cross a solid wall. Kinematically, $(\vec{v} - \vec{v}_c)\cdot\vec{n} = 0$, so convective flux through solid walls is identically zero.

3. The Moving Reference Frame Trick

One of the most powerful analytical techniques in aerospace engineering is converting an unsteady flow problem into a steady one by attaching the reference frame and control volume to a traveling disturbance.

Consider a solitary wave, a hydraulic bore, or a normal shock wave propagating at velocity $\vec{c} = c\hat{i}$. In the fixed laboratory frame, the fluid height, pressure, and velocity at any point $x$ change rapidly as the front sweeps past ($\partial/\partial t \neq 0$), requiring complex partial differential equations.

The Trick: Attach a control volume $V_c$ directly to the wave front, moving with constant velocity $\vec{v}_c = c\hat{i}$. Observed from this moving frame:

  1. The wave front is completely stationary inside $V_c$.
  2. The flow is entirely steady: $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V_c} \rho b \, dV \equiv 0$.
  3. Fluid enters from the right with relative speed $w_1 = v_1 - c$ and leaves to the left with relative speed $w_2 = v_2 - c$.

All time derivatives vanish, transforming the problem into a simple 1D steady flux balance!

Canonical Problem Walkthrough · CL2 Setup

The Hydraulic Bore / Moving Shock Wave

Problem Statement: A tidal surge creates a hydraulic bore of height $h_2$ moving with constant wave velocity $c$ into a calm, shallow channel of depth $h_1$ ($h_2 > h_1$) where water is initially at rest ($v_1 = 0$). Behind the bore, fluid moves forward with induced speed $v_b$. Determine the bore propagation speed $c$ and induced velocity $v_b$ in terms of $h_1, h_2,$ and $g$.

Stage 1 Select Moving Control Volume $V_c$

Attach a 2D control volume of width $W$ to the moving wave front, translating at velocity $\vec{v}_c = c\hat{i}$. In this frame, the flow is steady ($\partial/\partial t = 0$).

Calculate relative horizontal velocities:

  • Inflow (upstream ahead of bore): Undisturbed water at rest ($v_1 = 0$) approaches the front with relative speed: $$w_1 = v_1 - c = -c$$
  • Outflow (downstream behind bore): Water moving at $v_b\hat{i}$ leaves the front with relative speed: $$w_2 = v_b - c = -(c - v_b)$$
Stage 2 Apply Mass Conservation (Continuity)

Using the steady 1D continuity equation ($\sum \dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \sum \dot{m}_{\text{out}}$):

$$ \rho W h_1 |w_1| = \rho W h_2 |w_2| \implies h_1 c = h_2 (c - v_b) $$

Solving for the relative exit speed and induced speed $v_b$:

$$ c - v_b = c \frac{h_1}{h_2} \implies v_b = c \left(1 - \frac{h_1}{h_2}\right) = c \frac{h_2 - h_1}{h_2} $$
Stage 3 Apply Linear Momentum Conservation

For a steady moving control volume with negligible bed friction over the short bore length:

$$ \sum F_{x,\text{ext}} = \dot{m}(w_{2,x} - w_{1,x}) $$

External horizontal forces are purely hydrostatic pressure distributions:

  • Right face (height $h_1$): $F_{p1} = \int_0^{h_1} \rho g (h_1 - y) W dy = \frac{1}{2}\rho g W h_1^2$ (pushes left, $-\hat{i}$).
  • Left face (height $h_2$): $F_{p2} = \int_0^{h_2} \rho g (h_2 - y) W dy = \frac{1}{2}\rho g W h_2^2$ (pushes right, $+\hat{i}$).

Net horizontal force (taking right as $+x$):

$$ \sum F_x = \frac{1}{2}\rho g W h_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}\rho g W h_1^2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho g W (h_2^2 - h_1^2) $$

Net convective momentum flux leaving the volume (note $w_{2,x} = -(c-v_b)$ and $w_{1,x} = -c$):

$$ \dot{m}(w_{2,x} - w_{1,x}) = (\rho W h_1 c)[-(c - v_b) - (-c)] = \rho W h_1 c v_b $$
Stage 4 Solve for Wave Speed $c$ and Induced Velocity $v_b$

Equating net force to momentum flux:

$$ \frac{1}{2}\rho g W (h_2^2 - h_1^2) = \rho W h_1 c v_b $$

Substitute $v_b = c \frac{h_2 - h_1}{h_2}$ into the right-hand side:

$$ \frac{1}{2} g (h_2 - h_1)(h_2 + h_1) = h_1 c^2 \frac{h_2 - h_1}{h_2} $$

Dividing by $(h_2 - h_1) \neq 0$ yields the celebrated Hydraulic Bore Propagation Speed Formula:

$$ \mathbf{c = \sqrt{g h_2 \frac{h_1 + h_2}{2 h_1}}} $$

And substituting back into $v_b$:

$$ \mathbf{v_b = (h_2 - h_1)\sqrt{\frac{g (h_1 + h_2)}{2 h_1 h_2}}} $$
Aerospace Exam Insight: Acoustic Limit
In the limit of an infinitesimal wave ($h_2 \to h_1$), $c \to \sqrt{g h_1}$, which is the shallow water gravity wave propagation speed (exact hydraulic analog of the acoustic speed of sound $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$). For finite jumps ($h_2 > h_1$), the bore is supersonic ($Fr = c/\sqrt{gh_1} > 1$), exactly analogous to a normal shock wave in compressible gas dynamics!
