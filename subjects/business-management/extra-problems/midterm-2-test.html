B01 — NPV, IRR, payback and the cost of capital

Ten questions on investment selection (part ii), written for this portal. Each question has four options and only one is right. Answer all ten before opening any explanation.

Question 1 · NPV of a two-year project

A project requires an initial outlay of 120,000 € and generates net cash flows of 70,000 € in year 1 and 80,000 € in year 2. With a yearly discount rate of 5%, its NPV is:

  • a) 30,000.00 €
  • b) 16,054.42 €
  • c) 19,229.02 €
  • d) 139,229.02 €
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

Each flow is discounted to year 0 with its own exponent: $NPV = -120{,}000 + 70{,}000/1.05 + 80{,}000/1.05^2 = -120{,}000 + 66{,}666.67 + 72{,}562.36 = 19{,}229.02$ €.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) This is the undiscounted sum $70{,}000 + 80{,}000 - 120{,}000$, the NPV at 0%; the flows were not discounted.
  • b) Both flows were divided by $1.05^2$: the year 1 flow was discounted twice instead of once.
  • d) The present value of the inflows is right, but the initial outlay of 120,000 € was not subtracted.
Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 5 (a); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (a).

Question 2 · Largest outlay compatible with a given IRR

A project is expected to generate 25,000 € at the end of each of the next three years, and its estimated IRR is 6%. The maximum amount that can be invested in it at year 0 is:

  • a) 66,825.30 €
  • b) 75,000.00 €
  • c) 70,754.72 €
  • d) 62,971.45 €
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

At the IRR the NPV is zero, so the outlay equals the present value of the flows at 6%: $I_0 = 25{,}000/1.06 + 25{,}000/1.06^2 + 25{,}000/1.06^3 = 23{,}584.91 + 22{,}249.91 + 20{,}990.48 = 66{,}825.30$ €.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) This is $3 \times 25{,}000$: the flows were added without discounting them at the IRR.
  • c) The whole 75,000 € was discounted one year, as if every flow arrived at the end of year 1.
  • d) The whole 75,000 € was discounted three years, as if every flow arrived at the end of year 3.
Source.

3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 4 and its key (same method with four flows of 30,000 € at 8%).

Question 3 · Weighted average cost of capital

A company is financed 35% with equity, whose cost is estimated at 12%, and 65% with liabilities, whose average cost is 5%. Its capital cost $K_{WACC}$ is:

  • a) 8.50%
  • b) 9.55%
  • c) 17.00%
  • d) 7.45%
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

$K_{WACC} = K_{EQ}\,E/(E+L) + K_L\,L/(E+L) = 0.35 \times 12\% + 0.65 \times 5\% = 4.20\% + 3.25\% = 7.45\%$.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) This is the simple average $(12\% + 5\%)/2$; the costs were not weighted by the financial structure.
  • b) The weights were swapped: $0.65 \times 12\% + 0.35 \times 5\%$ gives the equity cost the weight of the liabilities.
  • c) The two costs were added, $12\% + 5\%$, which is not an average of any kind.
Source.

Topic 3, Slide 37; 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 8.

Question 4 · Payback with uneven flows

A project requires 90,000 € and generates 30,000 €, 40,000 € and 50,000 € in years 1, 2 and 3. Assuming that each flow arrives evenly during its year, its payback period is:

  • a) 2.25 years
  • b) 2.40 years
  • c) 2.67 years
  • d) 3.00 years
Answer and explanation

Answer: b)

After two years $30{,}000 + 40{,}000 = 70{,}000$ € are recovered; the remaining $90{,}000 - 70{,}000 = 20{,}000$ € come from the 50,000 € of year 3 after $20{,}000/50{,}000 = 0.40$ years, so the payback is 2.40 years.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) The outlay was divided by the average flow, $90{,}000/40{,}000$; the payback follows the cumulative flows year by year, not their average.
  • c) The missing 20,000 € were divided by the year 1 flow (30,000 €) instead of the flow of year 3, the year in which the recovery happens.
  • d) The recovery was rounded up to the end of year 3, ignoring that the flows arrive evenly during the year.
Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 5 (b) and Exercise 9 (a) (fraction of the year in which the outlay is recovered).

Question 5 · What Fisher's rate is

Fisher's rate of two investment projects is:

  • a) The discount rate at which the NPV of each project is zero
  • b) The weighted average of the costs of equity and liabilities of the company
  • c) The discount rate at which both projects have the same NPV
  • d) The discount rate at which each project recovers its initial outlay
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

The key of Exercise 9 obtains Fisher's rate from $NPV_A = NPV_B$; it is the rate at which the NPV profiles of the two projects cross, so the preferred project changes there.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) The rate that makes the NPV of one project zero is its IRR, a property of a single project, not of a pair.
  • b) This is the capital cost $K_{WACC}$ of Slide 37, a property of the company's financing, not of two projects.
  • d) The time in which a project recovers its outlay is the payback period, which is measured in years, not a discount rate.
Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (b); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c).

Question 6 · Both IRRs below the cost of capital

Two mutually exclusive projects have IRRs of 9.2% (project A) and 8.4% (project B). The company's cost of capital is 9.5%. Under the IRR criterion, the company should:

  • a) Undertake A, because it has the higher IRR
  • b) Undertake both, because both IRRs are positive
  • c) Undertake B, because its outlay is recovered with a lower rate
  • d) Reject both, because both IRRs are below the cost of capital
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

A project is accepted only when its IRR exceeds the cost of capital; here $9.2\% < 9.5\%$ and $8.4\% < 9.5\%$, so both NPVs at 9.5% are negative and neither project is undertaken, whatever their ranking.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) Ranking A above B is correct, but ranking alone does not make A acceptable: its IRR is still below the 9.5% cut-off rate.
  • b) A positive IRR is not the acceptance condition; the IRR must exceed the cost of capital, and the projects are mutually exclusive anyway.
  • c) A lower IRR makes a project less attractive, not more: this inverts the meaning of the IRR.
Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 6 (b); 3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 8.

Question 7 · Effect of a higher discount rate

A project made of an initial outlay followed by positive flows has an IRR of 12% and has been valued at 6%. If the discount rate is raised to 9%, its NPV:

  • a) Decreases, but remains positive
  • b) Increases, because the project earns more than 9%
  • c) Becomes negative, because the rate has risen
  • d) Does not change, because the IRR does not depend on the rate
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

The NPV of such a project falls as the rate rises, so it is lower at 9% than at 6%; since 9% is still below the 12% IRR, at which the NPV is zero, it remains positive. The key of Exercise 6 reasons the same way for rates of 7% and 9% with an IRR of 10%.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) A higher rate divides every future flow by a larger factor $(1+k)^t$, so the NPV cannot increase.
  • c) The NPV becomes negative only when the rate exceeds the IRR (12%), not as soon as it rises.
  • d) The IRR itself does not change, but the NPV is evaluated at the discount rate and does change with it.
Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 6 (b).

Question 8 · A false statement about payback

Which one of the following statements about the payback period is FALSE:

  • a) It is the time it takes to recover the initial investment from the project's cash flows
  • b) Of two projects, the one with the shorter payback always has the higher NPV
  • c) It does not take into account the cash flows received after the outlay is recovered
  • d) A project accepted under a maximum payback of three years may have a negative NPV
Answer and explanation

Answer: b)

Payback and NPV can rank projects differently. In Exercise 9 of the sheet project B has the shorter payback (about 11 months against 1 year and 9 months), yet above Fisher's rate of about 5% project A has the higher NPV.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) True: this is the definition used in the key of Exercise 5 (b), so it is not the false statement asked for.
  • c) True: payback stops counting when the outlay is recovered, which is why it ignores later flows; not the false one.
  • d) True: in Exercise 5 project A has a payback of one year and an NPV of −90.91 € at 10%, so this statement is not false.
Source.

Question 9 · IRR ranking against NPV at the cost of capital

Two mutually exclusive projects: A has an IRR of 14% and B an IRR of 11%; B has the larger NPV at 0%, and their Fisher's rate is 7%. The company uses the NPV criterion with a cost of capital of 5%. It should choose:

  • a) A, because it has the higher IRR
  • b) Neither, because the IRRs disagree with the NPV at 0%
  • c) Both, because both IRRs exceed 5%
  • d) B, because below the Fisher's rate B has the higher NPV
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

The two NPV profiles cross only at Fisher's rate (7%). At 0% B has the larger NPV, so B stays above A for every rate below 7%; at 5% the NPV criterion chooses B, even though A has the higher IRR.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) The IRR ranking holds only above Fisher's rate; at 5% it disagrees with the NPV ranking, and the company decides by NPV.
  • b) A disagreement between rankings does not make the projects unacceptable: both IRRs exceed 5%, so both NPVs are positive.
  • c) The projects are mutually exclusive, so only one of them can be undertaken even if both are acceptable.
Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (b); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c).

Question 10 · The cost of each financial resource

In the capital cost of the company, equity has ... cost and liabilities have ... cost:

  • a) an implicit (opportunity) ... an explicit
  • b) no ... an explicit
  • c) an explicit ... an implicit (opportunity)
  • d) an explicit ... no
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

Slide 37 states that every financial resource has a cost: equity has an implicit (opportunity) cost and liabilities an explicit cost (the interest), and both enter the weighted average $K_{WACC}$.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) Equity is not free: the owners give up other uses of their funds, which is its opportunity cost.
  • c) The two kinds of cost were swapped: interest on liabilities is the explicit one.
  • d) Liabilities do carry a cost (their interest), and equity's cost is not explicit but implicit.
Source.

B02 — Break-even point, operating leverage and production

Ten questions on the production function, written for this portal. Each question has four options and only one is right. Answer all ten before opening any explanation.

Question 1 · Which cost is variable

A machining shop makes titanium brackets for aircraft seats. Which of the following costs is a variable cost:

  • a) The annual lease of the workshop
  • b) The salary of the plant manager
  • c) The titanium bar consumed by each bracket
  • d) The depreciation of the CNC machines
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

A variable cost grows with the volume produced, so total cost is $TC = FC + VC\,Q$ with $VC$ per unit (Topic 4, Slide 37). The titanium consumed by each bracket is spent once per unit, so it is a variable cost; the other three are paid whatever the volume, as the rent and the fixed staff costs of Exercise 1 of the examples PDF.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) The lease is paid in full whether the shop makes one bracket or ten thousand, so it is a fixed cost.
  • b) A manager's salary does not depend on the number of brackets produced; it is a fixed cost of the period.
  • d) The yearly depreciation of the machines is the same at any volume of output, so it belongs to the fixed costs.
Source.

Topic 4, Slide 37; Examples PDF, Exercise 1.

Question 2 · Break-even volume

A maker of avionics cooling fans has fixed costs of 900,000 € per year, sells each fan at 250 € and has a variable cost of 175 € per fan. Its break-even point is:

  • a) 12,000 units
  • b) 3,600 units
  • c) 5,143 units
  • d) 2,118 units
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

The unit margin is $m = P - VC = 250 - 175 = 75$ € per fan, and the break-even point is $Q_0 = FC/m = 900{,}000/75 = 12{,}000$ units (Topic 4, Slide 37).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) 3,600 units divides the fixed costs by the price, 900,000/250, forgetting that each unit must also pay its own variable cost.
  • c) 5,143 units divides the fixed costs by the variable cost per unit, 900,000/175, which has no economic meaning.
  • d) 2,118 units adds price and variable cost, 900,000/(250 + 175), instead of subtracting them to get the margin.
Source.

Topic 4, Slide 37.

Question 3 · Break-even point with a commercial discount

A supplier of cabin LED strips has a list price of 80 € per unit and gives a 10% commercial discount on the list price to all its customers. Its variable cost is 54 € per unit and its fixed costs are 360,000 € per year. Its break-even point is:

  • a) 13,846 units
  • b) 15,385 units
  • c) 5,000 units
  • d) 20,000 units
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

Revenue is earned at the net price, $P = 0.90 \times 80 = 72$ € per unit, so $m = 72 - 54 = 18$ € and $Q_0 = 360{,}000/18 = 20{,}000$ units (Topic 4, Slide 37; the discount is handled as in Examples PDF, Exercise 1).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) 13,846 units uses the list price, 360,000/(80 − 54), as if no customer received the discount.
  • b) 15,385 units applies the 10% discount to the margin, 360,000/(0.9 × 26), instead of to the price; the variable cost is not discounted.
  • c) 5,000 units divides the fixed costs by the net price, 360,000/72, and forgets to subtract the variable cost.
Source.

Topic 4, Slide 37; Examples PDF, Exercise 1.

Question 4 · Effect of a fall in sales

A drone maker has an operating leverage of 4 at its current volume. If its sales fall by 5%, what happens to its operating profit:

  • a) It falls by 1.25%
  • b) It falls by 20%
  • c) It falls by 5%, the same as the sales
  • d) It falls by 4%
Answer and explanation

Answer: b)

$OL = (\Delta\text{Profit}/\text{Profit})/(\Delta Q/Q)$, so the relative change of the profit is $OL \times \Delta Q/Q = 4 \times (-5\%) = -20\%$ (Topic 4, Slides 39 and 40: with OL = 3 a 10% fall in sales lowers profit by 30%).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) 1.25% divides the change in sales by the leverage, inverting the ratio that defines the OL.
  • c) A change equal to that of sales would mean OL = 1, which ignores the effect of the fixed costs.
  • d) 4% reads the leverage itself as the change in profit, forgetting to multiply it by the 5% change in sales.
Source.

Topic 4, Slides 39 and 40.

Question 5 · Leverage from the break-even point

A firm that makes oxygen masks for airliners has a break-even point of 30,000 units and sells 40,000 units. Its operating leverage is:

  • a) 0.25
  • b) 1.33
  • c) 4.00
  • d) 3.00
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

Since $FC = Q^*\,m$, the leverage depends only on the distance to the break-even point: $OL = Q/(Q - Q^*) = 40{,}000/(40{,}000 - 30{,}000) = 4$ (Topic 4, Slide 42). A 1% change in sales changes the operating profit by 4%.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) 0.25 is $(Q - Q^*)/Q$, the inverse of the right ratio.
  • b) 1.33 is $Q/Q^*$, which divides by the break-even volume instead of by the distance to it.
  • d) 3.00 is $Q^*/(Q - Q^*)$, with the break-even volume in the numerator instead of the actual volume.
Source.

Topic 4, Slide 42.

Question 6 · Leverage of a firm in losses

A firm sells fewer units than its break-even point ($Q < Q^*$). Which one of the following statements about its operating leverage is TRUE:

  • a) It is greater than 1, so profit reacts more than proportionally to sales
  • b) It lies between 0 and 1, because the firm is in losses
  • c) It equals 1, because the fixed costs are not covered
  • d) It is zero or negative, and below −1 it amplifies the effect of a change in sales
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

With $Q < Q^*$ the denominator of $OL = Q/(Q - Q^*)$ is negative, so $OL \le 0$; for $OL < -1$ the effect is more than proportional (amplifier) and for $-1 < OL < 0$ it is less than proportional (Topic 4, Slide 42).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) OL greater than 1 is the case of a firm with profits ($Q > Q^*$), not of a firm in losses.
  • b) No volume gives an OL between 0 and 1: above the break-even point OL > 1 and below it OL ≤ 0.
  • c) OL = 1 would need zero fixed costs; at the break-even point itself the leverage is not even defined.
Source.

Topic 4, Slide 42.

Question 7 · Type of production system

A company builds satellite ground stations. Each station is designed for one client, and machinery, materials and workers are brought to the site where it is built. Its type of production and plant layout are:

  • a) Project production with a fixed layout
  • b) Batch production with a functional layout
  • c) Mass production with a product layout
  • d) Continuous production with a product layout
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

Individualized products with qualified workers and difficult planning are the project type, and a product that stays in one place while resources are brought to it is the fixed layout (Topic 4, Slides 7, 23 and 26).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) Batch production makes a variety of products in batches in a functional layout; it does not build a single one-off unit on site.
  • c) Mass production makes standard products for mass markets along a line, the opposite of a design for one client.
  • d) Continuous production (petrochemistry) is the high-volume end of the product-process matrix, with no individualized product.
Source.

Topic 4, Slides 7, 23 and 26.

Question 9 · Total productivity index

In one month a workshop that repairs aircraft wheels produces services worth 540,000 €. It uses labour worth 120,000 €, capital worth 90,000 €, raw materials worth 150,000 € and energy worth 40,000 €. Its total productivity index is:

  • a) 4.50
  • b) 0.74
  • c) 2.57
  • d) 1.35
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

Total productivity divides goods and services by all the factors together: $540{,}000/(120{,}000 + 90{,}000 + 150{,}000 + 40{,}000) = 540{,}000/400{,}000 = 1.35$ (Topic 4, Slide 34).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) 4.50 is 540,000/120,000, the labour productivity: a partial index, not the total one.
  • b) 0.74 is 400,000/540,000, inputs over outputs, the inverse of the index.
  • c) 2.57 divides only by labour and capital, 540,000/210,000, leaving out raw materials and energy.
Source.

Question 10 · Fixed costs and economic risk

Two firms of the same sector sell the same number of units and earn the same operating profit, but firm A has higher fixed costs than firm B. Which one of the following is TRUE:

  • a) Both have the same operating leverage, because they earn the same profit
  • b) Firm A has the higher operating leverage and therefore the higher economic risk
  • c) Firm B has the higher operating leverage, because its variable costs are higher
  • d) Firm A has the lower economic risk, because its fixed costs do not change with sales
Answer and explanation

Answer: b)

$OL = Q\,m/\text{Profit}$. With the same profit, $Q\,m = \text{Profit} + FC$ is larger for the firm with higher fixed costs, so firm A has the higher OL; the OL is an index of economic (operating) risk and, ceteris paribus, higher fixed costs give a higher OL (Topic 4, Slides 39 and 41).

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) Equal profits do not give equal leverage: the numerator $Q\,m$ also contains the fixed costs, which differ.
  • c) Higher variable costs mean a smaller margin and smaller $Q\,m$ for the same profit, so firm B has the lower OL.
  • d) Fixed costs that do not change with sales are exactly what makes profit more sensitive to sales, so A carries more risk, not less.
Source.

Topic 4, Slides 39 and 41.

Question 1 · A phase of the analysis system

Which one of the following is a phase of strategic marketing, and not of operative marketing:

  • a) Establishing the marketing budget
  • b) Diagnosing the situation with a SWOT analysis
  • c) Controlling and assessing the results of the marketing mix
  • d) Executing the marketing-mix strategies

Question 2 · Elasticity from two observations

A maker of drone batteries raises the price of its main model from 400 m.u. to 440 m.u., and its monthly sales fall from 2,000 to 1,700 units. Taking the percentage changes with respect to the initial values, as on Slide 17, the coefficient of price elasticity of demand (with its sign) is:

  • a) −0.67
  • b) −1.70
  • c) −1.50
  • d) −1.94
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

With the initial values as the base, $\%\Delta Q = (1{,}700 - 2{,}000)/2{,}000 = -15\%$ and $\%\Delta P = (440 - 400)/400 = +10\%$, so $E_d = -15\%/10\% = -1.50$. Its absolute value, 1.5, is above 1: the demand is elastic.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) −0.67 is the price change divided by the quantity change, the ratio turned upside down.
  • b) −1.70 uses the midpoints of the interval (1,850 units and 420 m.u.) as the base, which the question does not ask for.
  • d) −1.94 takes the final values (1,700 units and 440 m.u.) as the base instead of the initial ones.
Source.

Topic 5, Slide 17; topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2.

Question 3 · Sales after a price rise

A supplier of cabin headsets sells 12,000 units a year. The price elasticity of demand is 0.8 in absolute value, and the firm raises its price by 15%. With the percentage changes taken with respect to the initial values, the expected yearly sales are:

  • a) $10{,}560$ units
  • b) $13{,}440$ units
  • c) $9{,}750$ units
  • d) $10{,}200$ units
Answer and explanation

Answer: a)

From $E_d = \%\Delta Q/\%\Delta P$ with the sign of the law of demand, $\%\Delta Q = (-0.8) \times 15\% = -12\%$. The new sales are $12{,}000 \times (1 - 0.12) = 10{,}560$ units: the demand is inelastic, so the quantity falls by less, in percentage, than the price rises.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • b) 13,440 units adds the 12% instead of subtracting it: the negative sign of the elasticity (law of demand) has been ignored.
  • c) 9,750 units divides the price change by the elasticity ($15\%/0.8 = 18.75\%$) instead of multiplying them.
  • d) 10,200 units applies the 15% price change to the quantity, as if the elasticity were 1 (unit elastic demand).
Source.

topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2; PED Exercises SOL.pdf, Exercises 2 and 4.

Question 4 · Midpoint elasticity of an interval

On the demand table of a satellite-imagery service, a price of 50 m.u. per image goes with 300 images a week and a price of 40 m.u. with 420 images. The price elasticity of demand at the midpoint of this interval, computed as in Exercise 1 of the course's elasticity sheet, is:

  • a) −2.00
  • b) −1.14
  • c) −0.67
  • d) −1.50
Answer and explanation

Answer: d)

The midpoint method divides each change by the average of its two values: $\bar Q = (300 + 420)/2 = 360$ and $\bar P = (50 + 40)/2 = 45$. Then $\Delta Q/\bar Q = 120/360 = 0.3333$ and $\Delta P/\bar P = -10/45 = -0.2222$, so $E_d = 0.3333/(-0.2222) = -1.50$.

Why the other options are wrong:

  • a) −2.00 takes the initial values (300 images, 50 m.u.) as the base, the simple formula of Slide 17, not the midpoint asked for.
  • b) −1.14 takes the final values (420 images, 40 m.u.) as the base.
  • c) −0.67 is the midpoint ratio turned upside down: price change over quantity change.
Source.

PED Exercises SOL.pdf, Exercise 1; topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2.

Question 5 · Cutting the price with inelastic demand

A training-simulator firm faces a price elasticity of demand of 0.6 in absolute value and cuts its price by 10%. What happens to its income from sales:

  • a) It rises, because the quantity sold rises by more, in percentage, than the price falls
  • b) It falls, because the quantity sold rises by about 6%, less than the 10% fall of the price
  • c) It does not change, because the rise in quantity exactly offsets the fall in price
  • d) It falls, because the quantity sold falls by about 6% together with the price
Answer and explanation

Answer: b)

  • d) The law of demand makes the quantity rise when the price falls; this option has the wrong sign of the elasticity.

Question 6 · A new line in a cabin-interiors range

A cabin-interiors firm offers three product lines: seats (4 models), galleys (3 models) and lavatories (2 models). It now launches a fourth line, cabin lighting, with 2 models. What happens to its range of products:

  • a) Only the depth increases, because the firm now offers more individual products
  • b) Only the length increases; the extension stays at three because lighting is also a cabin product
  • c) The extension (width) rises from 3 to 4 lines and the length from 9 to 11 products; the depth of the existing lines does not change
  • d) The consistency of the range is lost, because every new line lowers the consistency with the final use
Answer and explanation

Answer: c)

The extension, or width (breadth), of the range is the number of product lines (3 to 4); the length is the total number of individual products ($4 + 3 + 2 = 9$ to $9 + 2 = 11$).
