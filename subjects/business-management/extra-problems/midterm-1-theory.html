Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C01 — SkyCourier: real and financial flows of a drone-delivery start-up

SkyCourier S.L. delivers medicines by drone to villages in the Pyrenees. It rents a hangar from a local farmer, employs twelve pilots and technicians, and was financed by investors who bought its shares. Each month it charges the regional health service and several pharmacies for the deliveries. In 2025 the families of the valley spent part of their wages on SkyCourier's new home-delivery service, and the company paid its first dividend to its shareholders.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Identify SkyCourier's inputs and outputs, and classify the inputs as factors of production

SkyCourier's output is a service, the delivery of medicines and parcels. Its inputs are the factors of production [Slides 3-4]:

FactorIn SkyCourier
Land (natural resources)The rented hangar site
LabourThe twelve pilots and technicians
CapitalDrones, charging stations, software; the money provided by investors
EntrepreneurshipThe founders who bring the other factors together and take the risk

The business activity is precisely the transformation of these inputs into the delivery service.

Key points
  • Output: a service (drone delivery), not a good.
  • Land: the hangar site; labour: pilots and technicians; capital: drones, equipment and the investors' money; entrepreneurship: the founders who coordinate and bear the risk.

Part (b) · 1.25 points

Describe the real flows and the financial flows between SkyCourier and the households, naming the two markets in which they take place

The circular flow of income links firms and households through two markets [Slides 5-11]:

MarketReal flowFinancial flow (opposite direction)
Factor marketHouseholds → SkyCourier: the hangar (land), work (labour), savings invested in shares (capital)SkyCourier → households: rent to the farmer, wages to the staff, dividends to the shareholders
Market of goods and servicesSkyCourier → households: the delivery serviceHouseholds → SkyCourier: the price of the deliveries, paid out of their wages

The flows close a circle: the wages paid by SkyCourier return to it, in part, when the families buy its home-delivery service. The real flows are the factors and the service; the financial flows are their monetary compensation, running the other way [Slide 11]. What SkyCourier keeps after paying all the factors is its profit, from which the dividend was paid.

Key points
  • Factor market: households deliver land, labour and capital; SkyCourier pays rent, wages and dividends.
  • Goods and services market: SkyCourier delivers the service; households pay for it.
  • Real and financial flows run in opposite directions; what the firm keeps is its profit.
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C02 — Orbital Fasteners: sorting a day of activity into the functional areas

Orbital Fasteners S.A. makes titanium bolts certified for satellites. On a single Monday: (1) the purchasing team ordered 2 tonnes of titanium bar; (2) the CNC lines produced 40,000 bolts; (3) the sales engineer presented the company at a space-industry fair; (4) the finance department negotiated a bank loan to buy a new thread-rolling machine; (5) it collected an invoice from a satellite integrator and paid the titanium supplier; (6) HR interviewed two quality inspectors; (7) the plant manager set the production targets for the next quarter and checked last month's scrap rate against the plan; (8) the IT team upgraded the traceability database.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Classify the eight activities into real, financial and management functions

ActivityFunctionGroup [Slide 13]
1. Ordering titaniumSupply (acquisition of inputs)Real
2. Producing boltsProductionReal
3. Trade fairMarketingReal
4. Bank loan for a machineFinancing and investingFinancial
5. Collecting and payingReceiving revenues and paying for inputsFinancial
6. Hiring inspectorsSupport (Human Resources)Real
7. Targets and scrap checkPlanning and controlManagement
8. Traceability databaseSupport (Information Systems)Real
Key points
  • Real: 1 (supply), 2 (production), 3 (marketing), 6 and 8 (support: HR, information systems).
  • Financial: 4 (financing and investing), 5 (collecting revenues and paying for inputs).
  • Management: 7 (planning and control).

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Explain how the real and the financial cycles relate, and what engineers contribute to the management of a firm like this one

The financial cycle runs alongside the real cycle [Slide 13]: the company raises funds (the loan of activity 4), uses them to pay for inputs (the titanium of activity 1, paid in activity 5), turns the inputs into bolts and sells them, collects the revenue (activity 5) and finally returns capital and interest to those who lent the money. Without the financial functions the real cycle stops; without the real cycle there is no revenue to repay the funds.

Engineers bring project planning, time management and problem solving, attention to detail, experience in data processing, computer skills and technical knowledge [Slide 15]: exactly what activity 7 (targets and scrap control) and activity 8 (traceability data) require.

Key points
  • The financial cycle mirrors the real one: funds raised → inputs paid → revenues collected → capital and interest returned.
  • Engineers: project planning, time management, problem solving, attention to detail, data processing, technical knowledge.
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C03 — Atlas Line Maintenance: the four stages of the management process in a ramp-up

Atlas Line Maintenance S.L. has won a contract to perform the overnight checks of a low-cost airline at a new base. The station manager, Marta, has six months to be ready. She sets the goal of "zero technical delays in the first summer" and a plan to hire and certify 25 mechanics. She draws the shift structure and decides who is responsible for each aircraft type and which tools each shift needs. During the first weeks she holds daily briefings, encourages the team and mediates a conflict between the night and day shifts over handovers. Every Friday she compares the delays and the hours per check with the targets and adjusts the shifts.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Identify the four stages of the management process in Marta's actions

Stage [Slide 14]ContentAt Atlas
PlanningDefining goals, establishing strategy, developing subplans"Zero technical delays"; plan to hire and certify 25 mechanics
OrganizingWhat must be done, how, and by whomShift structure, responsibilities per aircraft type, tools per shift
LeadingDirecting and motivating, resolving conflictsDaily briefings, encouragement, mediation between shifts
ControllingMonitoring that activities go as plannedFriday comparison of delays and hours per check with the targets
Key points
  • Planning: the zero-delay goal and the hiring and certification plan.
  • Organizing: shift structure, responsibilities, tools.
  • Leading: daily briefings, motivation, resolving the shift conflict.
  • Controlling: weekly comparison of delays and hours with the targets.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Explain why the four stages form a cycle and what Marta does when she finds a deviation

The management process is a cycle around the organization's purpose [Slide 14]. Control is not the end: when the Friday review shows a deviation (for example, more hours per check than planned), Marta corrects it by reorganizing the shifts or by revising the plan (more mechanics, new targets), and she must then lead the team through the change. The new results are controlled the following week, so the four stages keep repeating while the base operates.

Key points
  • Control results feed the next round of planning: the process is cyclical.
  • A deviation triggers corrective action: reorganizing the shifts or revising the plan, then leading the change.
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C04 — An eVTOL founder: the four views of the entrepreneur and her rights

Lucía, an aerospace engineer, noticed in 2023 that city hospitals needed faster transfers of organs than road ambulances could offer, and that nobody was serving that need. She designed an electric vertical-take-off aircraft with a new battery-cooling system, mortgaged her flat to fund the first prototype, and brought together a team of engineers, a supplier of carbon structures and a hangar owner. Today she signs every contract of the company, decides who works on each project, keeps whatever profit is left once everyone has been paid, and has been offered a large sum by an airline for her stake.

Part (a) · 1 point

Relate Lucía's behaviour to the four views of the entrepreneur

View [Slide 17]Evidence in the case
"On alert" (Kirzner, 1973)She detected a new business opportunity, a need nobody was serving
Innovator (Schumpeter, 1950)She introduced a new product with a new battery-cooling technology
Risk taker (Knight, 1921)She risked her own flat to finance the prototype
CoordinatorShe brought land (hangar), labour (engineers) and capital (supplier, funds) together

Together these are the functions of the entrepreneur: coordinate and control production, ensure payment of the factors, take risky decisions, innovate, and detect opportunities [Slide 17].

Key points
  • On alert (Kirzner): she detected an unserved need.
  • Innovator (Schumpeter): a new aircraft and battery-cooling system.
  • Risk taker (Knight): she mortgaged her flat.
  • Coordinator: she brought together engineers, supplier and hangar owner.

Part (b) · 1 point

Explain which rights make Lucía "the entrepreneur" of her company

A firm is teamwork, and team production needs someone who controls it; the incentive to take that role comes from four rights [Slide 18]. Lucía holds all of them: she receives the residual income (the profit left once every factor is paid); she controls and reassigns the other factors (she decides who works on each project); she is the central party in all the contracts with the inputs (she signs them); and she can sell these rights (the airline's offer for her stake). The person who holds all these rights is, by definition, the entrepreneur.

Key points
  • Right to the residual income (the profit left after paying the factors).
  • Right to control and reassign the other factors (who works on each project).
  • Being the central party in all contracts.
  • Right to sell these rights (the airline's offer).
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C05 — Three engineers choosing a legal form for a composite-repair shop

Ana, Bruno and Carla want to open a shop that repairs composite parts of light aircraft. Ana would invest 60,000 m.u. of savings; Bruno and Carla only their work. A single faulty repair could lead to claims far larger than everything they own, so they worry about losing their homes. They want to start quickly and cheaply, but in five years they hope to open three more shops with outside investors, and they want the business to continue if one of them leaves.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Compare the sole proprietorship, the partnership and the corporation on the features that matter to the three engineers

FeatureSole proprietorshipPartnershipCorporation
OwnersOne personTwo or more co-ownersShareholders
LiabilityUnlimitedUnlimited and jointLimited
StartingSimpleFew restrictionsComplex and costly
PermanenceImpermanentRelatively impermanentPermanent
FundsHard to raisePooled funds and talentsGreater financial capability
TaxationNo separate taxNo separate taxSpecial and double taxation

A sole proprietorship belongs to one person [Slide 21], so it does not fit three owners. A partnership is easy to set up through a partnership agreement and pools Ana's money with Bruno's and Carla's skills [Slide 22], but each partner answers for the firm's debts with all their personal wealth, jointly [Slide 23]. A corporation is a separate legal entity whose owners have limited liability [Slide 24].

Key points
  • Sole proprietorship: one owner only, unlimited liability: ruled out.
  • Partnership: easy to start, pooled talents; unlimited and joint liability, relative impermanence.
  • Corporation: limited liability, permanence, easy transfer of shares, greater financial capability; complex and costly, double taxation.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Recommend a legal form and justify it with the engineers' goals

The corporation fits best. Limited liability addresses their main fear: a large claim would affect the company's assets, not their homes. It is permanent, so the business continues if one partner leaves, and ownership is easily transferred through shares, which is how the outside investors of the expansion would enter; it also has greater financial capability [Slide 24]. The price is a more complex and costly formation, government regulation and disclosure requirements, and double taxation; the engineers should accept these costs in exchange for protection and growth. The course also lists forms of partnership that limit liability: the limited liability partnership (LLP) [Slide 20] and the limited partnership, where some partners answer only up to what they invested [Slide 29]. An LLP would also meet the fear of claims, but the corporation fits better with the plans for outside investors and continuity.

Key points
  • Recommend a corporation: limited liability protects their homes; permanence; easy entry of investors through shares.
  • Accept its drawbacks: cost of formation, regulation and disclosure, double taxation.
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C06 — Meseta Aerostructures: a CEO, dispersed shareholders and the agency problem

Meseta Aerostructures S.A. is listed on the stock exchange; no shareholder owns more than 2% of its shares. Its CEO, a professional manager, is paid a fixed salary that grows with the number of employees under her. In three years she has bought two loss-making companies, moved headquarters to an expensive tower and ordered a corporate jet, while the share price has fallen by 30%. The annual report gives little detail on these decisions, and most small shareholders do not attend the general meeting.

Part (a) · 1 point

Explain why this situation is an agency problem

In large corporations the company is run by managers, professional employees, while it is owned by the shareholders: ownership is separated from control [Slide 25]. At Meseta, the CEO's pay rises with the number of employees, so her objective is size and prestige (acquisitions, tower, jet), not the value of the shares. Shareholders and managers have different objectives and different access to information (the report gives little detail), and with stakes below 2% no shareholder has enough information or incentive to assess her performance. The result is a conflict of interest between ownership and control: the agency problem.

Key points
  • Ownership (shareholders) is separated from control (the professional CEO).
  • The CEO pursues her own benefit (size, prestige) instead of the shareholders' (value).
  • Different access to information and different objectives; dispersed shareholders lack information and incentives to monitor.

Part (b) · 1 point

Propose mechanisms that would align the CEO's decisions with the shareholders' interests

Shareholders must be able to control how the manager acts [Topic 1, Slide 26]:

  1. Select the Board of Directors so that it actually supervises the CEO and approves large acquisitions.
  2. Contracts linked to goals: replace pay tied to headcount with incentive compensation tied to profit or share value [Topic 2, Slide 4].
  3. Request audits of the acquisitions and expenses, and publish indicators that let shareholders judge performance.
  4. Outside the firm, the market of enterprises (a falling share price invites a takeover that would replace the CEO) and the labour market for managers (her reputation) also discipline her [Topic 2, Slide 4].

Increasingly, shareholders with large blocks of shares take the decisions [Slide 26]; a group of institutional investors could build such a block at Meseta.

Key points
  • Select a Board of Directors that supervises the CEO.
  • Contracts linked to goals (pay tied to profit or share value instead of headcount); incentive compensation.
  • Audits and indicators.
  • External discipline: the market of enterprises (takeover risk) and the labour market for managers.
Theory case · Topic 1 · 2 points · 15 min

C07 — A drone-spraying cooperative and a launcher joint venture

Two situations in the same region:

Case 1. Forty vineyard owners cannot each afford a crop-spraying drone. They create an organization that buys three drones and hires two pilots; any vineyard owner of the area may join, each member has one vote in the assembly regardless of size, and at the end of the year what is left after paying the expenses is returned to each member in proportion to the hectares sprayed.

Case 2. A European launcher manufacturer and a Japanese engine company set up a company owned 50/50 for the sole purpose of developing a reusable upper stage over eight years. When the stage is certified, the company will be dissolved.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Identify the form of business ownership in each case and justify it with its defining features

Case 1 is a cooperative association [Slides 27-28]: an incorporated organization whose user-members are also its owners and get back the revenue left after expenses. It follows the cooperative principles: open membership (any vineyard owner may join), democratic control (one member, one vote), and benefits distributed in proportion to each member's participation (hectares sprayed). Of the course's common types, the closest is a buying co-op, which helps members buy at lower prices (here, a spraying service); it is not an agricultural co-op in the course's sense, which helps member farmers market their products [Slide 28].

Case 2 is a joint venture [Slide 29]: a special type of partnership set up by firms to accomplish a specific task or project, the reusable upper stage, and dissolved once the task is done. It is very common in international business, as here.

Key points
  • Case 1: cooperative association (open membership, democratic control, benefits in proportion to participation, user-members are owners); closest type: a buying co-op.
  • Case 2: joint venture: a special partnership for a specific task or project, common in international business.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Explain one advantage of each form for its members

The cooperative gives small vineyard owners access to equipment that none could buy alone, at cost, while keeping control democratic and sharing the surplus fairly. The joint venture lets two firms from different countries share the cost and risk of a long, uncertain project and combine complementary know-how (launcher and engines) without merging the whole companies; each parent keeps its independence in the rest of its business. These advantages are reasoning applied to the case; the deck defines the forms but does not list their advantages.

Key points
  • Co-op: access to expensive equipment and lower costs, with democratic control and fair sharing.
  • Joint venture: sharing a large, risky project and combining complementary know-how across countries without merging the parent companies.

Consider three enterprises:

  • TitanMine, a private company with 35 employees that extracts titanium ore in one region and sells it only in its own country;
  • AeroNational, a state-owned manufacturer of military transport aircraft with 9,000 employees, plants in three countries and customers in twenty;
  • Wingtip Consulting, a one-person business that advises flying schools on safety management in several markets of one country.

A classmate claims: "Firms are unnecessary; each engineer could simply sell their work to whoever needs it on the market."

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Classify the three enterprises using the economic and legal criteria of the course

Criterion [Slide 19]TitanMineAeroNationalWingtip Consulting
SectorPrimary (extraction)Secondary (manufacturing)Tertiary (services)

  • AeroNational: secondary, big, multi-market and multinational, state-owned; corporation.
  • Wingtip: tertiary, small, multi-market and national, private, sole proprietorship.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Answer the classmate using the course's explanation of why enterprises exist

Economics studies how scarce resources are used to satisfy unlimited wants; those wants are met through specialization, the division of labour, and specialization requires coordination [Slide 12]. Coordination can be carried out either by the market, as the classmate suggests, or by the enterprise. Building a transport aircraft requires thousands of specialists working together on one product: coordinating them through separate market contracts would be impractical, so an enterprise appears to coordinate them (reasoning applied to the case, beyond the slides). In the course's words, an enterprise appears because there is a dissatisfied need [Slide 12]. Wingtip shows that, for simple services, one person selling directly to the market can also work.

Key points
  • Wants are met through specialization (division of labour), which requires coordination.
  • Coordination can be done by the market or by the enterprise; the enterprise appears where there is a dissatisfied need that it coordinates better.
Theory case · Topic 2 · 2 points · 15 min

C09 — Costa Airport: profit maximization or value creation?

A private company operates Costa Airport. A new board member argues: "Our only objective is to maximize this year's profit: let's double parking fees, cut the night shift of air-traffic support staff and postpone the runway resurfacing." The finance director replies that the board should instead aim to "maximize the value of the company", and the operations director warns that the airline customers, the unions, the city council and the regional government will all react to such measures.

Part (a) · 1 point

Explain the limitations of profit maximization as the objective of the firm, using the board member's proposal

The proposal follows the classical objective, profit maximization, which has four limitations [Slide 3]:

  1. Profit is difficult to define and to quantify: cutting maintenance raises this year's profit but lowers future profits (a degraded runway, a closure for emergency works).
  2. It ignores risk: the cuts raise the risk of incidents and of losing airlines, which a single profit figure does not show.
  3. The concept of maximization itself is questionable [Slide 3]; in practice managers aim at a minimum level of profit that satisfies the shareholders [Slide 4].
  4. It gives an excessive role to the entrepreneur: in a company like this, ownership and control are separated, and the board, managers and shareholders have different goals.
Key points
  • Profit is hard to define and quantify (this year vs future years).
  • It ignores the risk of achieving the profits (postponed resurfacing, safety, reaction of airlines).
  • The very idea of "maximizing" is questionable.
  • It gives an excessive role to the entrepreneur, whereas ownership and control are separated.

Part (b) · 1 point

Explain why the finance director's objective is the one the course proposes, and how stakeholders shape it

According to organizational theory, the objectives of the firm are the outcome of a negotiation among its stakeholders: here the shareholders, the airlines (customers), the employees and unions, the city council and regional government (the State), suppliers, banks and society [Slides 5-6]. The negotiation is limited because the firm must survive and because not all groups have the same power. The objective that satisfies the most powerful group while respecting the others and the firm's survival is to maximize the value of the enterprise, to create value [Slide 6]. In the unified vision, value comes from a cycle in which sales generate profits, retained profits finance investments (such as the resurfacing) and these renew the products and markets [Slide 7]; sacrificing the investment for one year's profit breaks that cycle.

Key points
  • Objectives result from negotiation among stakeholders (shareholders, customers, employees, State, society...).
  • Constraints: the firm must survive; groups have different bargaining power.
  • The resulting objective: maximize the value of the enterprise (create value), linking survival, profit and growth.

C10 — Closing an engine-parts plant: mapping the stakeholders and their power

TurboCast S.A., a maker of turbine blades, plans to close its oldest plant, in a town of 8,000 inhabitants where it employs 600 people, and move production to a new automated plant. The shareholders support the plan; the main bank, which financed the new plant, wants it to happen fast. The unions call a strike. The regional government offers a subsidy if the plant stays open two more years. The engine manufacturer that buys 70% of the blades demands that deliveries are not interrupted. Local suppliers and environmental groups also make their voices heard.

Part (a) · 1 point

Identify the stakeholders in the case and what each one wants

Stakeholder [Slide 5]In the caseWhat it wants
ShareholdersOwners of TurboCastLower costs and higher value through automation
Banks and financial agentsLender of the new plantRepayment; a fast transition that makes the plant profitable
Employees and unions600 workersTo keep their jobs and conditions
The StateRegional governmentEmployment and activity in the region
CustomersEngine manufacturerUninterrupted, on-time deliveries
SuppliersLocal suppliersTo keep their contracts
SocietyTown, environmental groupsLocal activity, responsible behaviour (CSR)
Key points
  • Shareholders: higher profit and value through automation.
  • Bank: repayment of the loan for the new plant.
  • Employees and unions: keep their jobs.
  • State (regional government): employment in the region.
  • Customer (engine maker): uninterrupted deliveries.
  • Suppliers and society: local business, environment.

Part (b) · 1 point

Explain, using organizational theory, how the final decision is likely to be reached

Organizational theory sees the objective as the outcome of a negotiation among stakeholders, limited by two facts: the firm must survive, and not all groups have the same power [Slide 6]. Here the engine manufacturer has very high bargaining power (70% of sales), as do the shareholders and the bank; the unions and the government have less power but can impose costs (strikes, subsidies withdrawn). The likely outcome is a compromise: the move to the automated plant goes ahead, because survival requires competitive costs, but it is phased over two years with the regional subsidy, deliveries are protected with stock built in advance, and part of the staff is relocated or retrained. That decision satisfies the most powerful groups while taking the others into account, which is what maximizing the value of the enterprise means in practice.

Key points
  • The decision is a negotiated outcome, not one group's wish.
  • It satisfies the group with the most bargaining power (here the engine maker, which buys 70%, together with shareholders and bank) while taking the others into account.
  • Constraint of survival: TurboCast must stay competitive.
  • A likely compromise: a phased closure (subsidy, two years), guaranteed deliveries, relocation or retraining.

C11 — A small-launcher start-up reads its generic environment

Altair Launch S.L. is developing a rocket for satellites under 200 kg. In its business plan it lists: interest rates have doubled in two years and venture capital is scarcer; the European Union has created a fund to secure independent access to space, and a new national space law sets licensing rules; additive manufacturing has made engine parts much cheaper, while reusable rockets are becoming a key technology; the country trains many aerospace engineers, but public opinion is increasingly worried about space debris and the noise of the launch site.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Classify each element into the four groups of factors of the generic environment

Group [Slide 11]Element of the caseEffect on Altair
EconomicHigher interest rates, less venture capitalFinancing becomes harder and costlier
Political-legalEU fund for independent access to space; national space law and licencesOpportunity (public demand and funding) and a regulatory requirement
Socio-culturalMany trained engineers (level of education); concern about debris and noise (value system)Easier hiring; pressure on the launch site and on debris mitigation
TechnologicalAdditive manufacturing; reusable rocketsCheaper engines; a key technology that may become a must

The course's four groups have no separate environmental factor: the table places the public's concern under the socio-cultural value system (public opinion), while the outside-in view of [Slide 13] also lists the natural environment among the forces around the industry. The PESTEL framework of other books would treat debris and noise as a separate environmental factor (extension).

Key points
  • Economic: interest rates, scarce venture capital.
  • Political-legal: EU fund, national space law and licensing.
  • Socio-cultural: supply of trained engineers (education), public concern about debris and noise (value system).
  • Technological: additive manufacturing (key), reusability (incipient/key).

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Explain the difference between the generic and the specific environment and why Altair needs both

The generic environment affects all firms, whatever their industry: the interest rates or the space law hit every company in the country [Slide 11]. The specific environment is the environment of the firm's own industry: the rivalry among launch providers, entry and exit barriers, substitutes (for example rideshare launches on large rockets), and the relationships with customers and suppliers [Slide 12]. The analysis moves from the outside in, from the economy, technology and politics to the industry at the centre [Slide 13]. Altair needs both: the generic factors explain the conditions it cannot change, and the specific ones explain whether it can earn money against its direct competitors.

Key points
  • Generic environment: factors that affect every firm in the country/economy.
  • Specific environment: the firm's own industry (rivalry, entry and exit barriers, substitutes, customers, suppliers), analysed with Porter's five forces.
  • The analysis moves from the outside in.

C12 — Island regional airlines under Porter's five forces

Three airlines fly between the islands of an archipelago with similar turboprops and similar fares; none is clearly larger. Two manufacturers in the world build turboprops of that size, and a single company supplies the jet fuel at the airports. Most passengers are price-sensitive residents who compare fares online, plus the regional government, which buys tickets for public employees. A fast-ferry company has just started services between the two main islands. To start a new airline one needs aircraft, an operating certificate and slots at airports that are almost full.

Part (a) · 1.5 points

Analyse the five competitive forces of this industry, rating each one as high, medium or low with a reason

Force [Slides 14-15]RatingDeterminants in the case
Rivalry among existing firmsHighThree firms of similar size (low concentration, no leader), similar product and fares (little differentiation)
Bargaining power of suppliersHighOnly two turboprop manufacturers and a single fuel supplier: high relative bargaining power
Bargaining power of buyersHighResidents are price-sensitive and compare online; the government buys in volume
Threat of new entrantsLow to mediumCapital requirements (aircraft), legal and regulatory barriers (operating certificate), access to "distribution" (airport slots almost full)
Threat of substitutesMedium to highThe fast ferry: buyers' propensity to substitute depends on its relative price and travel time
Key points
  • Rivalry: high (three similar firms, little differentiation, no leader).
  • Suppliers: high (two aircraft makers, one fuel supplier).
  • Buyers: high (price-sensitive residents; the government as a large buyer).
  • New entrants: low-medium (capital, certificate, full slots: legal and access barriers).
  • Substitutes: medium-high (fast ferry on the main route).

Part (b) · 0.5 points

Conclude on the attractiveness of the industry and suggest one strategic response for an airline

With strong rivalry, strong suppliers, strong buyers and a growing substitute, the industry is unattractive: the profit available is squeezed from every side, and only the entry barriers protect it. An airline can respond by building a cost advantage (a single aircraft type [Slide 21], high capacity utilization [Slide 22]) to survive the price war, or a differentiation advantage (frequent schedules, connections with long-haul flights, loyalty schemes for residents) that reduces buyers' price sensitivity [Slides 23-24].

Key points
  • Low attractiveness: four of five forces are strong.
  • A response: cost advantage (fleet standardization, high capacity utilization) or differentiation (schedule, connections, service) that weakens buyer power; possibly cooperation or consolidation to reduce rivalry.
Theory case · Topic 2 · 2 points · 15 min

C13 — Can a car-seat maker enter the aircraft-seat market? Barriers to entry

ComfortAuto S.A., a large maker of car seats, wants to enter the market of passenger seats for commercial aircraft. Three established firms supply almost all airlines. Each new seat model must pass crash and flammability certification that takes two years. Airlines buy seats through long contracts linked to aircraft orders, and the established firms have decades of reputation for reliability. They produce in large volumes in plants built for this purpose, and they own patents on lightweight seat frames. ComfortAuto, however, has strong engineering, low-cost purchasing of foam and fabrics, and spare plant capacity.

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Part (a) · 1.25 points

Identify the type of vertical integration in each proposal and the advantages Helios seeks with the purchase of the engine supplier

The deck names forward and backward vertical integration [Slide 27]; in the standard reading (extension), backward integration moves toward the inputs and forward integration toward the customer. Buying the engine supplier is therefore backward vertical integration, and buying the satellite-integration company would be forward vertical integration. Both change the vertical scope of the firm, replacing a market interface with an administrative one [Slide 19].

The advantages Helios seeks with the engine supplier [Slide 27]:

  • control of inputs: no more unexpected price rises or delays;
  • reduction of transaction costs: the disputes over design changes become internal decisions;
  • independence from a single supplier and a better competitive position;
  • reduction of information asymmetries: Helios will know the real costs and status of the engines.
Key points
  • Buying the engine supplier: backward vertical integration; buying payload integration: forward vertical integration.

Use the disadvantages of vertical integration to evaluate the director's objection

  • Decreased flexibility: tied to its own engine technology when it changes fast.
