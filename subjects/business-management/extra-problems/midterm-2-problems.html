Problem · Topic 3 · NPV, IRR and Fisher's rate · 2.5 points · 25 min · Practice item, not an official exercise

M01 — Aldebaran Space Systems: IRR ranking against NPV at the cost of capital

Aldebaran Space Systems, a manufacturer of small satellites, is evaluating two investment projects:

  • Investment A: an automated integration line for satellite buses, requiring an initial outlay of 500,000 € and expected to generate net cash flows of 300,000 € in year 1 and 300,000 € in year 2.
  • Investment B: a test stand for reusable rocket engines with an unknown initial outlay, expected to generate net cash flows of 200,000 € in year 1 and 700,000 € in year 2.

The company is financed 40% with equity, whose cost is estimated at 9%, and 60% with liabilities, whose average cost is 4%. It uses the NPV criterion with its weighted average cost of capital as the yearly discount rate. The projects are mutually exclusive (only one can be accepted).

a) Determine the discount rate of the company and the maximum initial outlay of project B for it to be interesting (NPV ≥ 0). (0.5 points)

b) The initial outlay of project B is finally fixed at 750,000 €. Which investment is preferable under the IRR criterion? Does the NPV at the company's discount rate give the same ranking, and which project does the company undertake? (0.75 points)

c) A third project C is presented, a ground-station network for a satellite data-download service (a new line of business), requiring an initial investment of 200,000 € and generating 100,000 € in year 1 and 150,000 € in year 2. Draw a schematic graph of the NPV of the three projects as a function of the discount rate and compute Fisher's rates of the pairs A–B, A–C and B–C. (0.75 points)

d) Reason which project would be chosen depending on the discount rate, state from which discount rate no project should be selected, and say which project the company chooses at its own discount rate. (0.5 points)

Model

  • Cost of capital (Topic 3, Slide 37): the capital cost is the weighted average of the costs of the financial resources, $K_{WACC} = K_{EQ}\,\frac{E}{E+L} + K_L\,\frac{L}{E+L}$. The key of 3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 8 uses it as the cut-off rate of the IRR.
  • Net present value (Examples PDF, Exercise 5): with outlay $A$, flows $Q_t$ and discount rate $k$,
$$NPV = -A + \frac{Q_1}{1+k} + \frac{Q_2}{(1+k)^2}$$

A project is interesting when $NPV \geq 0$; of mutually exclusive projects, the one with the largest positive NPV is chosen.

  • Internal rate of return: the rate $r$ at which $NPV = 0$ (3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 4). For a two-year project, with $x = 1 + r$ and multiplying by $x^2 > 0$, $NPV = 0$ becomes the quadratic $A\,x^2 - Q_1\,x - Q_2 = 0$, solved with $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. Only the root with $x > 0$ has economic meaning.
  • Fisher's rate of a pair: the rate at which both NPVs are equal (3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9). Multiplying $NPV_P = NPV_Q$ by $x^2$ gives $-A_P x^2 + Q_{1P} x + Q_{2P} = -A_Q x^2 + Q_{1Q} x + Q_{2Q}$, again a quadratic in $x$ (Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c)).
  • Hypotheses: flows at the end of each year, a constant yearly discount rate, no taxes and no residual value.

Part (a) · 0.5 points

Discount rate and maximum outlay of project B

Discount rate from the financial structure

The weights are the shares of equity and liabilities in the financial structure, $E/(E+L) = 0.40$ and $L/(E+L) = 0.60$ (Topic 3, Slide 37):

$$K_{WACC} = 0.40 \times 9\% + 0.60 \times 4\% = 3.60\% + 2.40\% = 6.00\%$$

So $k = 0.06$ and the discount factors are $1 + k = 1.06$ and $(1 + k)^2 = 1.06 \times 1.06 = 1.1236$.

NPV of B with an unknown outlay

Writing every term of the NPV of B with outlay $X$:

$$NPV_B = -X + \frac{200{,}000}{1.06} + \frac{700{,}000}{1.1236} = -X + 188{,}679.245 + 622{,}997.508 = -X + 811{,}676.75$$

Condition NPV ≥ 0

$$-X + 811{,}676.75 \geq 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X \leq 811{,}676.75\ \text{€}$$

Project B is interesting as long as its initial outlay does not exceed 811,676.75 €, the present value of its flows at 6%. Units: each term is a sum of money in € divided by a dimensionless factor $(1.06)^t$, so the NPV is in €.

Key results
  • $K_{WACC}$ = 6.00%.
  • Present value of the flows of B at 6%: 811,676.75 €.
  • B is interesting for an outlay $X \leq$ 811,676.75 €.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

IRR ranking of A and B, and the NPV at the cost of capital

IRR of A

$$-500{,}000 + \frac{300{,}000}{x} + \frac{300{,}000}{x^2} = 0 \quad\xrightarrow{\ \times x^2\ }\quad 500{,}000\,x^2 - 300{,}000\,x - 300{,}000 = 0$$

Dividing by 100,000: $5x^2 - 3x - 3 = 0$, with $a = 5$, $b = -3$, $c = -3$:

$$x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{(-3)^2 - 4 \cdot 5 \cdot (-3)}}{2 \cdot 5} = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 60}}{10} = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{69}}{10} = \frac{3 \pm 8.30662}{10}$$

The roots are $x = 1.130662$ and $x = -0.530662$; the negative one has no economic meaning. Hence $r_A = 1.130662 - 1 = 0.1307$, IRR(A) = 13.07%.

IRR of B

$$-750{,}000 + \frac{200{,}000}{x} + \frac{700{,}000}{x^2} = 0 \quad\xrightarrow{\ \times x^2\ }\quad 750{,}000\,x^2 - 200{,}000\,x - 700{,}000 = 0$$

Dividing by 50,000: $15x^2 - 4x - 14 = 0$, with $a = 15$, $b = -4$, $c = -14$:

$$x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4 \cdot 15 \cdot (-14)}}{2 \cdot 15} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 840}}{30} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{856}}{30} = \frac{4 \pm 29.25748}{30}$$

The positive root is $x = 1.108583$ (the other, $-0.841916$, is rejected), so IRR(B) = 10.86%.

Ranking by IRR

Both IRRs exceed the 6% cost of capital, so each project is acceptable on its own (IRR > k, 3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 8). Under the IRR criterion A is preferable, because $13.07\% > 10.86\%$.

NPVs at the company's discount rate

$$NPV_A(6\%) = -500{,}000 + \frac{300{,}000}{1.06} + \frac{300{,}000}{1.1236} = -500{,}000 + 283{,}018.868 + 266{,}998.932 = 50{,}017.80\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_B(6\%) = -750{,}000 + 188{,}679.245 + 622{,}997.508 = 61{,}676.75\ \text{€}$$

(the two discounted terms of B are those of part (a); equivalently $811{,}676.75 - 750{,}000$).

The two rankings disagree

At 6% the NPV ranks B first ($61{,}676.75 > 50{,}017.80$), the opposite of the IRR ranking. The company has adopted the NPV criterion and the projects are mutually exclusive, so it undertakes B. The IRR compares the projects at their own break-even rates, not at the company's 6%; B receives most of its money in year 2, so its NPV is high when the rate is low and falls fast when the rate rises. Part (c) shows that the two NPV profiles cross at Fisher's rate A–B of 8.06%, above the 6% of the company.

Key results
  • IRR(A) = 13.07%; IRR(B) = 10.86%; both above 6%, and A ranks first by IRR.
  • $NPV_A(6\%)$ = 50,017.80 €; $NPV_B(6\%)$ = 61,676.75 €; B ranks first by NPV.
  • The company uses NPV, so it undertakes B (the conflict is explained by Fisher's rate A–B in (c)).

Part (c) · 0.75 points

NPV profiles and Fisher's rates of the three pairs

IRR of C

$$-200{,}000 + \frac{100{,}000}{x} + \frac{150{,}000}{x^2} = 0 \quad\xrightarrow{\ \times x^2\ }\quad 200{,}000\,x^2 - 100{,}000\,x - 150{,}000 = 0$$

Dividing by 50,000: $4x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$, so

$$x = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2 - 4 \cdot 4 \cdot (-3)}}{2 \cdot 4} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 48}}{8} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{52}}{8} = \frac{2 \pm 7.21110}{8}$$

The positive root is $x = 1.151388$ (the other, $-0.651388$, is rejected): IRR(C) = 15.14%.

Points of the profiles

At $r = 0$ every discount factor is 1, so the NPV is the sum of the flows minus the outlay:

$$NPV_A(0) = -500{,}000 + 300{,}000 + 300{,}000 = 100{,}000\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_B(0) = -750{,}000 + 200{,}000 + 700{,}000 = 150{,}000\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_C(0) = -200{,}000 + 100{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 50{,}000\ \text{€}$$

At 6%, $NPV_C = -200{,}000 + \frac{100{,}000}{1.06} + \frac{150{,}000}{1.1236} = -200{,}000 + 94{,}339.623 + 133{,}499.466 = 27{,}839.09$ €.

Fisher's rate A–B

$$-500{,}000\,x^2 + 300{,}000\,x + 300{,}000 = -750{,}000\,x^2 + 200{,}000\,x + 700{,}000$$

Moving every term to the left: $250{,}000\,x^2 + 100{,}000\,x - 400{,}000 = 0$; dividing by 50,000, $5x^2 + 2x - 8 = 0$:

$$x = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4 \cdot 5 \cdot (-8)}}{2 \cdot 5} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 160}}{10} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{164}}{10} = \frac{-2 \pm 12.80625}{10}$$

The positive root is $x = 1.080625$ (the other, $-1.480625$, is rejected): FR(A–B) = 8.06%.

Fisher's rate A–C

$$-500{,}000\,x^2 + 300{,}000\,x + 300{,}000 = -200{,}000\,x^2 + 100{,}000\,x + 150{,}000$$

Moving every term to the right: $300{,}000\,x^2 - 200{,}000\,x - 150{,}000 = 0$; dividing by 50,000, $6x^2 - 4x - 3 = 0$:

$$x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4 \cdot 6 \cdot (-3)}}{2 \cdot 6} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 72}}{12} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{88}}{12} = \frac{4 \pm 9.38083}{12}$$

The positive root is $x = 1.115069$ (the other, $-0.448403$, is rejected): FR(A–C) = 11.51%.

Fisher's rate B–C

$$-750{,}000\,x^2 + 200{,}000\,x + 700{,}000 = -200{,}000\,x^2 + 100{,}000\,x + 150{,}000$$

Moving every term to the right: $550{,}000\,x^2 - 100{,}000\,x - 550{,}000 = 0$; dividing by 50,000, $11x^2 - 2x - 11 = 0$:

$$x = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2 - 4 \cdot 11 \cdot (-11)}}{2 \cdot 11} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 484}}{22} = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{488}}{22} = \frac{2 \pm 22.09072}{22}$$

The positive root is $x = 1.095033$ (the other, $-0.913215$, is rejected): FR(B–C) = 9.50%.

Who is above whom on each side of each crossing

Each pair crosses only once for $r > 0$, so the project with the larger NPV at 0% stays ahead until the crossing:

  • A–B: B is ahead below 8.06% (150,000 > 100,000 at 0%), A above it.
  • A–C: A is ahead below 11.51% (100,000 > 50,000 at 0%), C above it.
  • B–C: B is ahead below 9.50% (150,000 > 50,000 at 0%), C above it.
Key results
  • IRR(C) = 15.14%; $NPV$ at 0%: A 100,000 €, B 150,000 €, C 50,000 €.
  • Fisher's rates: A–B 8.06%; A–C 11.51%; B–C 9.50%.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

Decision intervals and the rate above which no project is chosen

The upper envelope of the three profiles

  • From 0% to 8.06%, B is above A (A–B) and above C (B–C holds up to 9.50%): B.
  • At 8.06% A overtakes B. Between 8.06% and 11.51%, A is above B (A–B) and above C (A–C): A. The B–C crossing at 9.50% lies inside this interval, below A, so it does not change the decision.
  • At 11.51% C overtakes A, and C is already above B (since 9.50%). Between 11.51% and 15.14%, C has the largest NPV and it is positive: C.
  • Above 15.14%, the largest IRR, every NPV is negative: no project should be selected.

Check at one rate inside each interval: at 4%, $NPV_A$ = 65,828.40 €, $NPV_B$ = 89,497.04 €, $NPV_C$ = 34,837.28 € (B); at 10%, 20,661.16 €, 10,330.58 € and 14,876.03 € (A); at 14%, −6,001.85 €, −35,934.13 € and 3,139.43 € (C); at 16%, −18,430.44 €, −57,372.18 € and −2,318.67 € (none).

Decision of the company

$$0\% \leq r < 8.06\% \Rightarrow B, \qquad 8.06\% < r < 11.51\% \Rightarrow A, \qquad 11.51\% < r < 15.14\% \Rightarrow C, \qquad r > 15.14\% \Rightarrow \text{no project}$$

At exactly a Fisher's rate the two leading projects are indifferent. At its own discount rate of 6%, Aldebaran chooses project B. The trap is twofold: A beats B by IRR, and C has the highest IRR of the three, yet at 6% C has the lowest NPV (27,839.09 €). The IRR ranking only tells which project survives longest as the rate rises; at a given cost of capital the decision follows the NPV.

Key results
  • $0 \leq r < 8.06\%$: B; $8.06\% < r < 11.51\%$: A; $11.51\% < r < 15.14\%$: C; $r > 15.14\%$: no project.
  • At the company's 6%: project B.
  • The B–C crossing at 9.50% happens while A is ahead of both, so it changes nothing.

Interpretation and checks

The three profiles differ in how their money is spread over time. B concentrates 700,000 € in year 2, so a rising rate hits it hardest: it leads at low rates and is the first to drop out. C is small and returns most of its outlay in year 1, so its profile is flat and it is the last to become negative. Limiting cases: at $r = 0$ the NPV is the plain sum of flows minus the outlay, and as $r \to \infty$ every discounted flow tends to 0, so the NPV tends to minus the outlay. Dimensions: every NPV is a sum of amounts in € divided by dimensionless factors, so it is in €; the IRR and Fisher's rates are dimensionless rates per year. A common exam mistake is to stop at "A has the higher IRR, so A is chosen" without comparing the NPVs at the company's own rate, which here lies below Fisher's rate A–B.

Problem · Topic 3 · NPV, IRR and Fisher's rate · 2.5 points · 25 min · Practice item, not an official exercise

M06 — Corvus Flight Simulators: payback against NPV under a budget limit

Corvus Flight Simulators builds full-flight simulators for airline training centres. For next year its board has received four proposals, which are independent of each other, cannot be split and cannot be repeated. Each one lasts two years:

  • Project A, a motion-platform retrofit kit, costs 400,000 € today and brings in 380,000 € at the end of the first year and 100,000 € at the end of the second.
  • Project B, a new simulator for a wide-body aircraft type, costs 600,000 €; it returns only 90,000 € in year 1, while the certification is completed, and 680,000 € in year 2.
  • Project C, a visual-system upgrade for the existing fleet, costs 300,000 € and returns 60,000 € in year 1 and 300,000 € in year 2.
  • Project D, a refurbished-simulator resale deal, costs 200,000 € and returns 180,000 € in year 1 and 30,000 € in year 2.

The shareholders require a yearly return of 8% on every project. Until now the board has accepted any project that recovers its outlay in at most 1 year and 9 months, and it has 900,000 € available to invest.

a) Compute the payback period of the four projects (in years, months and days, with 30-day months and the flows spread evenly over each year), rank them, and say which ones the board's payback rule accepts. (0.5 points)

b) Compute the NPV of the four projects at 8% and rank them. Which projects create value? Comment on the projects for which the payback rule and the NPV give opposite answers. (0.5 points)

c) The board proposes to use the 900,000 € by taking the projects in order of shortest payback while they still fit in the budget. Which projects would it undertake, and what total NPV would it obtain? Which set of projects maximises the total NPV within the budget? (0.5 points)

d) Compute the IRR of the four projects and compare the IRR ranking of A, B and C with their NPV ranking at 8%. Draw a schematic graph of the NPV of A, B and C as a function of the discount rate. (0.5 points)

e) The budget is finally cut to 600,000 €, so only one of A, B and C can be undertaken. Compute Fisher's rates of the pairs A–B, A–C and B–C, state which project would be chosen depending on the discount rate and from which rate none would be chosen, and give the choice at 8%. (0.5 points)

Model

When the outlay is recovered during year 2, the payback is $1 + (A - Q_1)/Q_2$ years; the fraction of a year times 12 gives the months, and the fraction of a month times 30 gives the days. The shorter, the better; it ignores the time value of money and every flow after the recovery.
  • Net present value (Examples PDF, Exercise 5): with outlay $A$, flows $Q_t$ and discount rate $k$,
$$NPV = -A + \frac{Q_1}{1+k} + \frac{Q_2}{(1+k)^2}$$

A project creates value when $NPV > 0$. With a budget and independent, indivisible projects, the set chosen is the one with the largest total NPV that fits in the budget (3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 7).

  • Internal rate of return: the rate $r$ at which $NPV = 0$ (3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 4). With $x = 1 + r$ and multiplying by $x^2 > 0$, $NPV = 0$ becomes $A\,x^2 - Q_1\,x - Q_2 = 0$, solved with $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$; only the root with $x > 0$ has economic meaning.
  • Fisher's rate of a pair: the rate at which both NPVs are equal (3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9). Multiplying $NPV_P = NPV_Q$ by $x^2$ gives a quadratic in $x$ (Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c)).
  • Hypotheses: flows at the end of each year for the NPV and spread evenly over the year for the payback, a constant yearly rate, no taxes and no residual value.

Part (b) · 0.5 points

NPV at 8% and the conflict with payback

NPV of each project

$$NPV_A = -400{,}000 + \frac{380{,}000}{1.08} + \frac{100{,}000}{1.1664} = -400{,}000 + 351{,}851.852 + 85{,}733.882 = 37{,}585.73\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_B = -600{,}000 + \frac{90{,}000}{1.08} + \frac{680{,}000}{1.1664} = -600{,}000 + 83{,}333.333 + 582{,}990.398 = 66{,}323.73\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_C = -300{,}000 + \frac{60{,}000}{1.08} + \frac{300{,}000}{1.1664} = -300{,}000 + 55{,}555.556 + 257{,}201.646 = 12{,}757.20\ \text{€}$$
$$NPV_D = -200{,}000 + \frac{180{,}000}{1.08} + \frac{30{,}000}{1.1664} = -200{,}000 + 166{,}666.667 + 25{,}720.165 = -7{,}613.17\ \text{€}$$

Units: each term is an amount in € divided by a dimensionless factor $(1.08)^t$, so every NPV is in €.

Ranking and the two conflicts

By NPV the order is B, A, C, D: A, B and C create value, D does not.

  • D is second by payback and passes the rule, yet its NPV is negative. Its flows add up to 210,000 €, only 10,000 € more than the outlay, and that margin does not cover the 8% the shareholders require over two years. Payback only checks that the money comes back quickly, not that it comes back with its cost.
  • C is rejected by the rule because it recovers its outlay 18 days too late, yet its NPV is positive: payback ignores that the 300,000 € of year 2 more than pay for the wait.
  • B, last but one by payback, has the largest NPV: its large flow arrives late, which payback penalises and the NPV only discounts.
Key results
  • $NPV_A$ = 37,585.73 €; $NPV_B$ = 66,323.73 €; $NPV_C$ = 12,757.20 €; $NPV_D$ = −7,613.17 €.
  • Ranking by NPV: B, A, C, D. A, B and C create value; D destroys value although it passes the payback rule; C creates value although the rule rejects it.

Interpretation and checks

Payback measures liquidity, not value: it ranked first A and D, the projects that return their money in year 1, and it would have spent more than a fifth of the budget on D, whose small margin does not cover the cost of the funds. The NPV ranks by the value created at the required return, and with a budget the right choice is the combination with the largest total NPV, not the first projects of a list. Limiting cases: at $r = 0$ each NPV is the plain sum of flows minus the outlay, and as $r \to \infty$ it tends to minus the outlay; a project with a later flow profile, like B, moves the most between these two limits. A common mistake is to report Fisher's rate B–C as a switching point: it lies where neither project is worth undertaking.

Part (a) · 0.5 points

Classify the costs and find the break-even list price

Classify the costs

The lease, the salaries of the operators (fixed annual contracts, as the direct labour of Examples PDF, Exercise 1), the salaries of the engineering and quality staff, the depreciation and the insurance and administration are the same whatever the number of blades: they are fixed costs.

$$FC = 380{,}000 + 460{,}000 + 200{,}000 + 100{,}000 + 60{,}000 = 1{,}200{,}000\ \text{€}$$

The prepreg and resin, the energy of the autoclave and the packaging and transport are spent once per blade: they are variable costs per unit.

$$VC = 36 + 7 + 5 = 48\ \text{€/blade}$$

The interest on the bank loan is a financial expense, a non-operating item of the income statement (Topic 3, Slide 11): it lies below the operating profit (EBIT) and does not enter a break-even point defined on the operating profit. It is left out of $FC$.

Average net price

Of every 100 blades, 40 are sold at $0.90\,P_L$ (list price minus the 10% discount) and 60 at $P_L$. The average net price per blade is the weighted mean:

$$P = 0.40 \times 0.90\,P_L + 0.60 \times P_L = (0.36 + 0.60)\,P_L = 0.96\,P_L$$

Break-even condition at 25,000 blades

At the break-even point revenue equals total cost, $P\,Q_0 = FC + VC\,Q_0$ (Topic 4, Slide 37). Solving for the net price:

$$P = \frac{FC + VC\,Q_0}{Q_0} = \frac{1{,}200{,}000 + 48 \times 25{,}000}{25{,}000} = \frac{2{,}400{,}000}{25{,}000} = 96\ \text{€/blade}$$

From the net price to the list price

$$P_L = \frac{P}{0.96} = \frac{96}{0.96} = 100\ \text{€/blade}$$

Check: $m = 96 - 48 = 48$ € and $Q_0 = FC/m = 1{,}200{,}000/48 = 25{,}000$ blades, the forecast volume. Units: € divided by € per blade gives blades. Had the interest been counted as a fixed cost, the net price would be $(1{,}260{,}000 + 1{,}200{,}000)/25{,}000 = 98.4$ € and the list price 102.5 €; had the discount been applied to all customers, as in Pegasus, the list price would be $96/0.90 = 106.67$ €.

Key results
  • $FC$ = 1,200,000 €; $VC$ = 48 € per blade; the interest (60,000 €) is not an operating cost
  • Average net price = 0.96 × list price
  • Net price = 96 € per blade; list price = 100 € per blade

Part (b) · 0.5 points

Volume for an operating profit of 10% of net sales

With the list price of 100 €, the net price is $P = 96$ € and $m = P - VC = 96 - 48 = 48$ € per blade. The target is

$$Q\,m - FC = 0.10 \times P\,Q$$

Solve for the volume

Move the terms in $Q$ to the left and factor $Q$:

$$Q\,(m - 0.10\,P) = FC \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q = \frac{FC}{m - 0.10\,P} = \frac{1{,}200{,}000}{48 - 0.10 \times 96} = \frac{1{,}200{,}000}{38.40} = 31{,}250\ \text{blades}$$

Each blade leaves 48 € of margin, of which 9.60 € must be kept as profit, so only 38.40 € per blade is left to cover the fixed costs.

Check

Revenue $= 96 \times 31{,}250 = 3{,}000{,}000$ €. The volume is above the break-even point (25,000 blades), as a positive profit requires.

Key results
  • Unit margin $m$ = 48 € per blade, 10% of the net price = 9.60 € per blade
  • $Q$ = 31,250 blades
  • Revenue 3,000,000 €, operating profit 300,000 €

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Operating leverage at 31,250 and at 40,000 blades

Check with the break-even point, $Q^* = 25{,}000$ (Topic 4, Slide 42): $OL = 31{,}250/(31{,}250 - 25{,}000) = 31{,}250/6{,}250 = 5.00$.

Leverage at 40,000 blades

$$OL = \frac{40{,}000 \times 48}{40{,}000 \times 48 - 1{,}200{,}000} = \frac{1{,}920{,}000}{720{,}000} = 2.67$$

Meaning

The OL measures how a change in sales translates into a change in operating profit (Topic 4, Slide 40). At 31,250 blades a 1% change in sales changes the operating profit by 5% in the same direction (a 10% fall in sales would cut profit by 50%); at 40,000 blades the same 1% changes the profit by only 2.67%. Both values are greater than 1 because the firm has profits ($Q > Q^*$), and the leverage falls as the volume moves away from the break-even point (Topic 4, Slide 42). The OL is an index of economic risk (Topic 4, Slide 41): Helix is riskier at 31,250 blades, close to its break-even point, than at 40,000. The leverage is a pure number: € over €, or blades over blades.

Key results

  • At 40,000 blades: $Q\,m$ = 1,920,000 €, profit 720,000 €, OL = 2.67

Part (d) · 0.75 points

A 4% cut in the list price with an elasticity of 1.5

$$\frac{\Delta Q}{Q} = -E_d\,\frac{\Delta P}{P} = -1.5 \times (-0.04) = +0.06$$
$$Q_{new} = 40{,}000 \times (1 + 0.06) = 42{,}400\ \text{blades}$$

New net price and margin

The average net price is $0.96\,P_L$, so it changes in the same proportion as the list price:

$$P_{new} = 96 \times (1 - 0.04) = 92.16\ \text{€/blade} \qquad m_{new} = 92.16 - 48 = 44.16\ \text{€/blade}$$

(New list price $100 \times 0.96 = 96$ €; $0.96 \times 96 = 92.16$ €, the same result.)

New operating profit

$$\text{Profit}_{new} = Q_{new}\,m_{new} - FC = 42{,}400 \times 44.16 - 1{,}200{,}000 = 1{,}872{,}384 - 1{,}200{,}000 = 672{,}384\ \text{€}$$

Before the price cut: $\text{Profit} = 40{,}000 \times 48 - 1{,}200{,}000 = 720{,}000$ €. The profit falls by $720{,}000 - 672{,}384 = 47{,}616$ €.

Revenue and explanation

Revenue before: $40{,}000 \times 96 = 3{,}840{,}000$ €. Revenue after: $42{,}400 \times 92.16 = 3{,}907{,}584$ €, a rise of 67,584 €. The demand is elastic ($E_d > 1$), so a price cut raises the volume more than proportionally and the revenue rises (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 3). The operating profit, however, falls: the unit margin drops from 48 € to 44.16 €, a fall of $3.84/48 = 8\%$, twice the 4% price cut because the variable cost does not fall, and the 6% gain in volume does not make up for it. Higher revenue does not mean higher profit; the decision must be judged on the margin.

Key results
  • $\Delta Q/Q$ = +6%, new volume 42,400 blades
  • New net price 92.16 €, new margin 44.16 €
  • New operating profit 672,384 € (was 720,000 €: −47,616 €)
  • Revenue rises from 3,840,000 € to 3,907,584 € (+67,584 €)

Interpretation and checks

Helix's break-even list price (100 €) already includes the fact that only 40% of the blades carry the discount, which lowers the average price by 4% instead of 10%. The leverage falls from 5.00 to 2.67 as the firm moves from 31,250 to 40,000 blades, so further away from its break-even point its profit is less sensitive to sales. The price cut of part (d) is the opposite of the price rise of Pegasus: with elastic demand it gains revenue but loses operating profit, because each blade sold earns 3.84 € less while the fixed costs stay at 1,200,000 €. Limiting cases: with no OEM customers the factor 0.96 becomes 1 and the list price would be 96 €; at $Q = Q^* = 25{,}000$ the OL is not defined (the profit is zero). Dimensions: prices and margins in € per blade, volumes in blades, profits and revenues in €, OL and elasticity dimensionless.

At the forecast the robotic cell gives 120,000 € more profit, but it carries more economic risk: a 1% change in sales changes its profit by 3%, against 2% for the manual cell (Topic 4, Slide 40). The reason is its cost structure. The robotic cell has more fixed costs (960,000 € against 360,000 €), and, ceteris paribus, the higher the fixed costs, the higher the OL (Topic 4, Slide 41). In terms of the break-even point, 24,000 brackets is only 1.5 times $Q^*_R$ but 2 times $Q^*_M$, so R is relatively closer to its break-even point, and the closer to it, the larger the leverage (Topic 4, Slide 42). The robotic cell is the rigid system of Topic 4, Slide 17: a high total cost to start with that grows slowly with the volume. Choosing it means a higher profit if the forecast holds and a larger exposure if it does not.

Key results
  • R: $Q\,m$ = 1,440,000 €, profit 480,000 €, OL = 3.00
  • M: $Q\,m$ = 720,000 €, profit 360,000 €, OL = 2.00
  • R has more economic risk: higher fixed costs, a break-even point closer to the forecast in relative terms, higher OL

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Sales 25% below the forecast

Prediction with the leverage

$\Delta Q/Q = -0.25$, so the volume is $24{,}000 \times (1 - 0.25) = 18{,}000$ brackets. By the definition of the OL (Topic 4, Slide 39):

$$\frac{\Delta\text{Profit}_R}{\text{Profit}_R} = 3.00 \times (-0.25) = -0.75 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{Profit}_R = 480{,}000 \times (1 - 0.75) = 120{,}000\ \text{€}$$
$$\frac{\Delta\text{Profit}_M}{\text{Profit}_M} = 2.00 \times (-0.25) = -0.50 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{Profit}_M = 360{,}000 \times (1 - 0.50) = 180{,}000\ \text{€}$$

Direct check

$$\text{Profit}_R = 18{,}000 \times 60 - 960{,}000 = 1{,}080{,}000 - 960{,}000 = 120{,}000\ \text{€}$$
$$\text{Profit}_M = 18{,}000 \times 30 - 360{,}000 = 540{,}000 - 360{,}000 = 180{,}000\ \text{€}$$

The predictions are exact because the profit is linear in the volume and the OL was measured at the starting volume of 24,000.

Which process would have been better

At 18,000 brackets, below the crossing volume of 20,000, the manual cell gives 60,000 € more profit than the robotic cell. The higher leverage of R, which multiplied its gains above the forecast, has multiplied its losses of profit here: this is the economic risk measured in part (b).

Key results
  • New volume 18,000 brackets
  • R: profit falls 75%, to 120,000 €; M: profit falls 50%, to 180,000 €
  • At 18,000 (below 20,000) the manual cell would have been better

Part (d) · 0.75 points

A 10% price rise with an elasticity of 2.5

New volume

$\Delta P/P = +0.10$ and $E_d = 2.5$ in absolute value (Topic 5, Slide 17; topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2):

$$\frac{\Delta Q}{Q} = -E_d\,\frac{\Delta P}{P} = -2.5 \times 0.10 = -0.25 \qquad Q_{new} = 24{,}000 \times (1 - 0.25) = 18{,}000\ \text{brackets}$$

New margins and profits

$P_{new} = 100 \times 1.10 = 110$ €, so $m_R = 110 - 40 = 70$ € and $m_M = 110 - 70 = 40$ € per bracket.

$$\text{Profit}_R = 18{,}000 \times 70 - 960{,}000 = 1{,}260{,}000 - 960{,}000 = 300{,}000\ \text{€}$$
$$\text{Profit}_M = 18{,}000 \times 40 - 360{,}000 = 720{,}000 - 360{,}000 = 360{,}000\ \text{€}$$

Change in revenue

Revenue before: $24{,}000 \times 100 = 2{,}400{,}000$ €. Revenue after: $18{,}000 \times 110 = 1{,}980{,}000$ €, a fall of 420,000 €. The demand is elastic ($E_d > 1$), so a price rise lowers the revenue (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 3). The revenue does not depend on the process chosen.

Which process, and is the rise advisable

The crossing volume is still $600{,}000/30 = 20{,}000$ brackets, because the price cancels when the two profits are equated (part (a)). The price rise moves the volume to 18,000, below the crossing point, so with the rise the manual cell gives more profit (360,000 € against 300,000 €). The decision on the process depends on the expected volume, and a price change with elastic demand can move it across the crossing point.

The rise is not advisable for either process in its best form. With the robotic cell the profit falls from 480,000 € to 300,000 €; with the manual cell it stays at 360,000 € (its extra margin of 10 € on 18,000 brackets, 180,000 €, exactly makes up for the margin of 30 € lost on 6,000 brackets, 180,000 €). The highest profit of all four combinations is the robotic cell at the present price, 480,000 €. The trap is to read "with the price rise the manual cell is better" as "the manual cell is better": it is better only inside a plan that is itself worse than keeping the price.

Key results
  • New volume 18,000 brackets, new price 110 €
  • R: margin 70 €, profit 300,000 €; M: margin 40 €, profit 360,000 €
  • Revenue falls from 2,400,000 € to 1,980,000 € (−420,000 €)
  • With the rise M is better; the best plan overall is R without the rise (480,000 €)

Interpretation and checks

The robotic cell is the fixed-heavy, rigid process: it breaks even later (16,000 against 12,000 brackets) but earns twice the margin per bracket, so it wins above 20,000 brackets and loses below. At the forecast it gives more profit with more economic risk (OL 3.00 against 2.00), and part (c) shows that risk at work: a 25% fall in sales cuts its profit by 75% and makes the manual cell the better choice. A price rise with elastic demand ($E_d = 2.5$) lowers the volume by 25% and the revenue by 420,000 €; it never helps the robotic cell, whose high margin makes every lost bracket costly. Limiting cases: if both processes had the same fixed costs, the one with the lower variable cost would win at every volume and there would be no crossing point; at $Q = Q^*$ the OL is not defined. Dimensions: prices, variable costs and margins in € per bracket, volumes in brackets a year, profits and revenues in € a year, OL and elasticity dimensionless.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

The two distribution channels

Channel for new aircraft

"Sold directly to the two aircraft makers": the producer sells directly to the final buyers, a direct channel [Topic 5, Slide 35], with no intermediary, the zero-level channel of the TechNova case [Examples PDF, Exercise 2 (c)]. The buyers are firms, so it is business-to-business (B2B).

Channel for spares and refurbishment

"An independent firm that buys the products from Altamira, stores them and resells them to airlines and maintenance companies": an independent intermediary takes the title, so the channel is indirect [Topic 5, Slide 35]. It sells to organizational buyers and not to final consumers, which is what the course calls a wholesaler [Topic 5, Slide 35]; the storage is one of the functions of the intermediaries [Topic 5, Slide 36]. Extension: the level of a channel is the number of intermediaries between the producer and the final buyer; this one has one level (Altamira, distributor, airline). The buyers are again firms: B2B.

"One authorised distributor per continent ... the sole right to sell Altamira products in its continent" is exclusive distribution [Topic 5, Slide 37]. Extension (definition not given on the slide): exclusive distribution grants a single outlet per area, against a limited number of chosen outlets (selective) or as many as possible (intensive).

The key-account engineers

A distribution channel is a series of interdependent firms that transfer the title of the product from the producer to the final buyer [Topic 5, Slide 34]. The engineers are Altamira's own employees, so they add no level to the channel: they are the firm's sales force, and their monthly visits are personal selling, a tool of the promotion mix [Topic 5, Slide 33]. The channel of the new aircraft stays direct and zero-level.

Key points
  • New aircraft: direct channel, zero levels (Altamira to aircraft maker), B2B.
  • Spares and refurbishment: indirect channel, one level (Altamira, distributor, airline or maintenance company), the distributor acts as a wholesaler, B2B.
  • Intensity of the distributors: exclusive, one distributor per continent with the sole right to sell.
  • The key-account engineers are not a channel: they are Altamira's own sales force (personal selling, a promotion tool) inside the direct channel.

M04 — Altair MRO introduces a digital maintenance platform

Altair MRO is a maintenance, repair and overhaul company with 600 employees and three hangars that service regional aircraft. Today every inspection and repair is recorded on paper task cards; the cards travel by hand to the planning office, aircraft often leave the hangar late because a card or a spare part is missing, and the airlines that are its customers complain that they only learn about delays at the last minute.

The Chief Executive Officer wants to replace the paper cards with a digital maintenance platform: technicians would record each task on a tablet at the aircraft, the stock of spare parts would update automatically, and the airline would follow the progress of its aircraft online. The firm has never used such a system and no competitor of its size has one. The firm is organized by functions (inspection, repair, planning, stores), each with its own head; every procedure is written in detailed manuals, there are six hierarchical levels between the technician and the CEO, and any change to a procedure must be approved by the technical director.

Tasks:

a) Describe at least one task that should be performed in each of the four managerial functions to introduce the platform. (0.5 points)

b) List the steps of the decision-making process the CEO should follow, applying each one to the case. (0.5 points)

c) Classify the decision to adopt the platform by type of decision and by management level, and say which basic managerial skill weighs most for the manager who takes it. (0.5 points)

d) Define four KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) to control the introduction of the platform. (0.5 points)

e) Using the basic organizational configurations of Mintzberg, state in which one the platform would be hardest to introduce and in which one easiest, justifying each answer with the features of the configuration. (0.5 points)

Part (a) · 0.5 points

One task per managerial function

Planning

Planning is setting goals and deciding on the strategy to achieve them, and a goal is a specific achievement to be attained at a future date (Topic 6, Slide 10). Task: set the objective "all three hangars working without paper task cards within twelve months", forecast the cost and the training time, compare a gradual roll-out (one hangar first) with a simultaneous one, and fix the calendar of the chosen option.

Organizing

Organizing arranges the human resources so that their activities contribute to the firm's goals, through differentiation (dividing the work) and integration (coordinating it) (Topic 6, Slide 11). Task: create a project team with members of inspection, repair, planning and stores, give each member a distinct task (data migration, tablet set-up, rewriting the procedures), name one person per hangar to coordinate, and update the organization chart with the new roles.

Leading

Leading is human resources management (Topic 6, Slides 2 and 23): directing (motivation, leadership, communication) and staffing (recruiting, selecting, training, appraisal and compensation). Task: explain to the technicians why the paper cards are being replaced, train every technician on the tablets, and recruit and then select a data specialist if nobody in the firm can run the platform. Recruitment (attracting candidates) comes before selection (choosing among them).

Controlling

Controlling ensures that activities provide the planned results: establishing standards, measuring actual performance, identifying deviations and taking corrective action (Topic 6, Slide 24). Task: compare every month the progress of the roll-out with the calendar set when planning, measure the KPIs of part (d), and correct the deviations (more training in a hangar that lags behind, for example). Buying the tablets is an operation of the project, not a managerial function in itself.

Key points
  • Planning: objectives and calendar of the roll-out (goal with a date, forecasts, alternatives, chosen course of action).
  • Organizing: who does what, a project team, new tasks and their coordination, the organization chart updated.
  • Leading: motivation, communication and training of technicians; recruiting and selecting new profiles if needed.
  • Controlling: standards, measurement, deviations, corrective action.

Part (b) · 0.5 points

The decision-making process applied to the case

The eight steps in the case

The decision-making process has eight steps (Topic 6, Slide 6):

  1. Recognizing the problem or opportunity: aircraft leave late because paper cards and spare parts go missing, and customers learn about delays at the last minute.
  2. Gathering information, identifying the critical factors: how many delays come from lost cards or missing parts, how many technicians and aircraft are involved, the budget, and what the airlines expect.
  3. Considering alternative courses of action: keep paper but add a courier between hangar and office; scan the cards at the end of each shift; adopt the full digital platform.
  4. Analysing the alternatives: cost, time to introduce, training needed and expected reduction of delays of each one.
  5. Choosing an alternative: the digital platform, the only one that updates stock and informs the customer in real time.
  6. Implementing the decision: the roll-out planned and organized in part (a).
  7. Evaluating the results: compare delays and customer complaints before and after, with the KPIs of part (d).
  8. Implementing changes: adjust the platform, the procedures or the training where the evaluation shows deviations.
Key points
  • The eight steps in the order of the course, each applied to Altair MRO.
  • Analysis (step 4) before choice (step 5); evaluation (step 7) before changes (step 8).

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Type of decision, management level and skill

Type of decision

The firm "has never used such a system and no competitor of its size has one": the problem is unusual and the alternatives are ambiguous and not planned, so this is an innovative (not planned) decision, not a routine one, which would face a known, well-defined problem with known alternatives (Topic 6, Slide 5).

Management level

The platform changes the way the three hangars, the stores and the relation with the customers work: it affects the firm as a whole, so it is a strategic, general decision of top management (the CEO), not a tactical decision of middle management or an operational one of first-line supervisors (Topic 6, Slide 7; Examples PDF, Exercise 4, statement 5). Choosing which hangar team is trained first on Monday would, by contrast, be an operational decision.

Skill

The skill that weighs most in top management is the conceptual skill, the ability to see the firm as a whole: the CEO must see how the platform links maintenance, stores, planning and customers. Technical skill weighs most in low management (Topic 6, Slide 8).

Key points
  • Innovative (not planned) decision: new and ambiguous problem, unplanned alternatives; quote "never used such a system", "no competitor of its size".
  • Top management, strategic and general decision.
  • Conceptual skill, the ability to see the firm as a whole.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

Four KPIs to control the introduction

Definition and link with controlling

A KPI is a quantified measure of performance against an objective (Examples PDF, Exercise 3; extension). It serves the second activity of controlling, measuring actual performance, so that deviations from the standard can be identified and corrected (Topic 6, Slide 24).

Four KPIs for Altair MRO

  1. On-time release rate: percentage of aircraft returned to the airline on or before the promised date (the problem that started the decision).
  2. Digital adoption rate: percentage of maintenance tasks recorded on the tablet instead of on paper, per hangar and per month.
  3. Spare-part waits: number of tasks stopped per month because a spare part was missing, which the automatic stock update should reduce.
  4. Customer satisfaction score: the airlines' rating of the information they receive on the progress of their aircraft, after each maintenance visit.

Other valid KPIs: hours of training completed per technician, number of task cards lost (should fall to zero), average turnaround time per check.

Key points
  • Four measurable indicators, each with its unit or ratio, linked to the objectives of the case: punctuality, data on time, spare parts, customer, adoption.
  • The link with controlling: standard, measurement, deviation, corrective action.

Part (e) · 0.5 points

Hardest and easiest configuration

Hardest: machine bureaucracy

The machine bureaucracy has a proliferation of rules and very formalized procedures, highly specialized routine tasks, functional departmentalization, centralized decision-making and many layers with a low span of control (Topic 6, Slide 16). Altair MRO already shows these features: procedures "written in detailed manuals", a firm "organized by functions" with a head each, "six hierarchical levels" and every change "approved by the technical director". Every manual must be rewritten, the functional departments must start sharing data they keep separate today, and each change climbs six levels before it is approved, so the platform is hardest to introduce there.

Easiest: adhocracy

The adhocracy (innovative organization) has low standardization and formalization, decentralization as the key to flexibility, matrix departmentalization and a mix of middle line, operating core and staff, and it is used as a complementary structure for specific projects (Topic 6, Slide 22). A cross-functional project team can test the platform, change it and decide on the spot, so the introduction is easiest there; Altair MRO can use such a team for the pilot in one hangar, as part (a) proposes.

The other configurations and alternative answers

The simple structure decides quickly by direct supervision but is meant for start-ups and very small firms, not for 600 employees (Topic 6, Slide 15); in a professional bureaucracy qualified professionals decide on their own work (Topic 6, Slide 18), and in a division organization each division is a bureaucracy that can try the platform on its own (Topic 6, Slide 20). An answer that names a functional structure (departmental silos) as the hardest and a matrix structure as the easiest is also valid if it is reasoned (Examples PDF, Exercise 3): functional departmentalization is a feature of the machine bureaucracy and matrix departmentalization of the adhocracy (Topic 6, Slides 16 and 22).

Key points
  • Hardest: machine bureaucracy, with its features (rules and formal procedures, routine specialized tasks, functional departmentalization, centralized decisions, many layers, low span of control) matched to the facts of the statement.
  • Easiest: adhocracy (low standardization and formalization, decentralization, matrix departmentalization, used for specific projects).
  • Functional, divisional or matrix arguments are also accepted when reasoned.

Interpretation and checks

The case uses the whole of Topic 6: the decision is innovative and strategic (Slides 5 and 7), it is taken through the eight-step process (Slide 6), carried out through the four managerial functions (Slides 3-4), checked with indicators that serve controlling (Slide 24), and its difficulty depends on the configuration of the firm (Slides 15-22). The present structure of Altair MRO is close to a machine bureaucracy, so the advice is to pilot the platform in an adhocracy-like project team before extending it to the three hangars.

M12 — Kestrel Drones outgrows its simple structure

Kestrel Drones was founded four years ago by Lucía Ferrer, an aeronautical engineer, with eight colleagues. Until last year the firm had 25 people: Lucía decided everything, from the price of each contract to who flew which test, and every engineer, pilot and technician reported to her directly. Nobody had a fixed job: the same engineer designed a wing in the morning and repaired a customer's drone in the afternoon, and there was no written procedure, because "we just ask Lucía".

After a large investment round the firm has grown to 180 employees and now sells two very different product lines: light crop-survey drones for farm cooperatives in Spain and Portugal, and heavy inspection drones for electricity grid operators across Europe, which must pass a certification by the aviation authority. Each line has its own customers, its own sales cycle and its own engineering team. Lucía no longer has time to approve every decision: offers wait weeks for her signature, two customers received drones with different software versions, and the certification audit found that test flights were not recorded in a standard way.

The founders who joined in the first year still meet Lucía every Friday afternoon and settle many questions there, outside any meeting on the calendar; newcomers say that "the real decisions are taken at the Friday coffee". In a normal week Lucía also opens the firm's stand at a drone trade fair, negotiates the terms of the investment round, reassigns two engineers when a prototype crashes during a test, and reads the reports on a competitor's new drone before telling the engineering teams what she has learnt.

Part (a) · 0.5 points

Factors that determine the structure, before and after

Specialization

Specialization is horizontal (the number of tasks a person does) and vertical (the autonomy to organize one's own job) (Topic 6, Slide 13). Before, horizontal specialization was low: "the same engineer designed a wing in the morning and repaired a customer's drone in the afternoon". Now each product line has "its own engineering team", so horizontal specialization rises: people concentrate on fewer tasks of one line.

Standardization and formalization

Standardization is how far work follows set rules and procedures, and formalization how far those rules are written down (Topic 6, Slide 13). Before there was "no written procedure", so both were very low. Now the two customers with "different software versions" and the test flights "not recorded in a standard way" show that standardized procedures are needed, and the certification by the aviation authority requires them to be written: both factors must increase.

Centralization

Before, decision-making was fully centralized: "Lucía decided everything" (Topic 6, Slide 13). Now "offers wait weeks for her signature", which shows that one person can no longer take every decision for 180 employees: decisions on offers, tests and production must be decentralized to the heads of each line.

Structural configuration

Before there was a single layer between Lucía and everybody, with a span of control of about 25 people and no administrative component. Now the firm needs departments, at least one more hierarchical layer (heads of line), a smaller span of control for each manager and an administrative component that handles certification, quality and finance (Topic 6, Slide 13).

  • Standardization and formalization: almost none before ("no written procedure"), needed now (software versions, recorded test flights for certification).
  • Centralization: total before ("Lucía decided everything"), decentralization needed now (offers waiting weeks).
  • Structural configuration: one layer and a very wide span of control before; more layers, a smaller span and an administrative component now.

Part (b) · 0.5 points

The parts at the start

A simple structure has only a strategic apex and an operating core (Topic 6, Slide 15). At Kestrel Drones the strategic apex (top management) was Lucía, and the operating core (the employees who do the basic work) were the engineers, pilots and technicians who "reported to her directly" (Topic 6, Slide 14).

  • Middle line (middle management): a head for the crop-survey line and a head for the grid-inspection line, who approve offers and assign people inside their line; this removes the bottleneck of "offers wait weeks for her signature".
  • Technostructure (analysts who design systems and processes): a quality and certification office that designs the procedure for releasing a software version and the standard record of every test flight, the two failures found in the statement.
  • Support staff (outside the operating workflow): units such as legal, finance and human resources, which handle the investment round, the contracts and the hiring of the 155 newcomers without doing the engineering work itself.

  • At the start: only the strategic apex (Lucía) and the operating core (engineers, pilots, technicians).

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Configuration at the start and the one to move to

The configuration at the start

With 25 people, Lucía as the key part deciding by direct supervision and only strategic apex and operating core, Kestrel Drones was a simple structure (entrepreneurial start-up), the configuration of start-ups and very small businesses (Topic 6, Slide 15).

The proposed configuration

The firm now sells "two very different product lines", each with "its own customers, its own sales cycle and its own engineering team", in a changing market just funded by an investment round. This matches the division organization: autonomous divisions, the middle line as the key part, top management coordinating the performance and outputs of the divisions, used by diversified businesses in more dynamic environments (Topic 6, Slide 20). The departmentalization proposed is therefore by product and market: a crop-survey division and a grid-inspection division (extension: grouping by product, by customer or by geographic area are the usual criteria besides functional and matrix; the deck only says "departmentalization (types)", Topic 6, Slide 13). Inside each division the work can be grouped by function, because each division is itself a bureaucracy (Topic 6, Slide 20); the quality and certification office can serve both.

Why not the other configurations

A machine bureaucracy (functional departmentalization, centralized decisions, many layers, for mature firms in a stable and simple environment, Topic 6, Slide 16) would keep one engineering department for two very different lines and keep decisions at the top, the present problem. An adhocracy (matrix departmentalization, low standardization and formalization, Topic 6, Slide 22) suits specific projects, but certification demands written, standard procedures; it can still be used as a complementary structure for the design of a new drone. A reasoned answer that proposes a functional structure for a single-line firm is also valid, but here the two lines with separate customers make the division organization the better fit.

Key points
  • At the start: simple structure (entrepreneurial start-up), strategic apex as key part, direct supervision, only apex and operating core.
  • Proposal: division organization, with one division per product line (departmentalization by product and market); middle line as key part; top management coordinates performance and outputs; diversified businesses, dynamic environment.
  • Alternatives (machine bureaucracy, adhocracy) discussed and rejected or accepted with reasons.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

The informal structure and the real structure

The informal structure in the case

The informal structure is the informal contacts, communications and ways of doing things that the employees develop (Topic 6, Slide 12). At Kestrel Drones it is the Friday afternoon meeting of the first-year founders with Lucía, held "outside any meeting on the calendar", where "many questions" are settled, so that newcomers feel that "the real decisions are taken at the Friday coffee".

The formal structure is the one defined by management (Topic 6, Slide 12): the divisions, heads and offices of parts (b) and (c), drawn in the organization chart, its graphic representation (Topic 6, Slide 11). The real structure is the combination of both (Topic 6, Slide 12): at Kestrel Drones, the new chart plus the influence of the founders' group.

How management should treat it

The informal structure cannot be abolished by drawing a new chart. Lucía should keep it as a fast channel of communication, but take the decisions of each line inside the division, with its head present; otherwise the new middle line would be bypassed and decisions would stay as centralized as before.

Key points

  • Formal structure: defined by management (the new chart of divisions). Informal structure: contacts, communications and ways of doing things that employees develop.
  • Real structure: formal plus informal.
  • Management should not ignore it: use it for communication, but keep the decisions of the new heads of division in their formal channel.

Problem · Topic 6 · Management case · 2.5 points · 22 min · Practice item, not an official exercise

M13 — Orbis Aerostructures launches a quality and training programme

Orbis Aerostructures is a plant of 2,400 employees that machines and assembles fuselage panels and wing ribs for two aircraft manufacturers. Its operators do short, repetitive tasks on each workstation, every task follows a written work instruction, decisions are taken at the top and pass down through seven hierarchical levels, and a methods office designs the instructions. Last year one panel in every twenty-five was rejected at the customer's incoming inspection, mostly for drilling and sealing defects, and one manufacturer has warned that it will move part of its orders if the figure does not halve within a year.

The board of directors has approved a quality-control and training programme for the whole plant. Other plants of the same group solved a similar problem with programmes of this kind, but Orbis must adapt them to its own parts and machines. The programme has these actions:

  1. Set the target "rejections below one panel in fifty within twelve months" and the calendar of the programme.
  2. Create a quality cell in each production line, with a quality inspector who reports to the head of quality.
  3. Hire twelve new quality inspectors, since nobody in the plant holds the inspection certificate.
  4. Train the 900 operators of drilling and sealing in the new inspection checklists.
  5. Explain to the operators in meetings on every shift why the programme matters for the plant's orders.
  6. Pay a quarterly bonus to the lines that meet the target, and assess each operator's work every six months.
  7. Measure every week the rejections at the customer and the defects found inside the plant, and correct the lines that fall behind.
  8. Buy two new automatic drilling machines.

In each shift, the shift supervisor decides every morning which operators attend that day's training session, following the rota set by the training office.

Tasks:

a) Classify the actions 1 to 8 into the four managerial functions, and say which action is not a managerial function. (0.5 points)

b) Classify by type of decision and by management level: the board's decision to launch the programme, and the supervisor's daily choice of who attends the session. Say which managerial skill weighs most in each case. (0.5 points)

c) Using the activities of human resources management, explain how Orbis should recruit, select, train, appraise and compensate its people in the programme, and say which actions belong to directing and which to staffing. (0.5 points)

d) Define three KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) to control the programme and classify the control of action 7 by when it takes place and by its frequency. (0.5 points)

e) State in which basic organizational configuration this programme is easiest to introduce and in which it is hardest, justifying each answer with the features of the configuration. (0.5 points)

Part (a) · 0.5 points

The actions and the four managerial functions

Planning: action 1

Planning is setting goals and deciding on the strategy to achieve them, and a goal is a specific achievement to be attained at a future date (Topic 6, Slide 10). Action 1 sets exactly such a goal, "below one panel in fifty within twelve months", and its calendar.

Organizing: action 2

Organizing arranges the human resources so that their activities contribute to the firm's goals, by dividing and coordinating the work (Topic 6, Slide 11). Action 2 creates a new unit, the quality cell, and fixes who reports to whom: it changes the organization chart.

Leading: actions 3, 4, 5 and 6

Leading is human resources management (Topic 6, Slides 2 and 23). Hiring inspectors (3), training operators (4), explaining the programme on every shift (5), and the bonus and the six-monthly assessment (6) all manage people; part (c) sorts them into directing and staffing.

Controlling: action 7

Controlling ensures that activities provide the planned results: standards, measurement of actual performance, deviations and corrective action (Topic 6, Slide 24). Action 7 measures rejections and defects every week against the target of action 1 and corrects the lines that fall behind: planning and controlling are inseparable (Topic 6, Slide 24).

What does not belong: action 8

Buying two drilling machines is an investment decision and part of the production process, not one of the four managerial functions. Like "communication is not a product attribute" in Exercise 2 of the examples PDF, it is the item that does not belong to the list of the course.

Key points
  • Planning: action 1.
  • Organizing: action 2.
  • Leading (human resources management): actions 3, 4, 5 and 6.
  • Controlling: action 7.
  • Action 8 (buying machines) is an investment and an operation of the production function, not a managerial function.

Part (b) · 0.5 points

Type of decision, management level and skill

The board's decision

The problem, rejections of drilled and sealed panels, is known and measured, and "other plants of the same group solved a similar problem", so the alternatives are partly known; but Orbis "must adapt them to its own parts and machines". It is neither a routine decision (known problem, known alternatives) nor an innovative one (unusual problem, ambiguous and not planned alternatives): it is an adaptable decision, between the two (Topic 6, Slide 5). It affects the whole plant and its relation with the customers, so it is a strategic, general decision of top management (Topic 6, Slide 7), where the skill that weighs most is the conceptual skill, seeing the firm as a whole (Topic 6, Slide 8).

The supervisor's daily choice

Choosing which operators attend today's session is a problem that is known and well defined, with known alternatives fixed by the rota of the training office: a routine (planned) decision (Topic 6, Slide 5). It is taken by a first-line supervisor, low management, and concerns one shift on one day, an operational decision of the lower levels of the pyramid, which the course calls tactical and functional by scope (Topic 6, Slide 7). The skill that weighs most there is the technical skill, knowing how the workstations operate and who can leave them (Topic 6, Slide 8). The interpersonal skill weighs at every level, for the board and for the supervisor alike.

Key points
  • Board decision: adaptable decision (known problem of rejections, alternatives known from the group's other plants but to be adapted); top management, strategic and general; conceptual skill.
  • Supervisor's choice: routine (planned) decision (known, well-defined problem, known alternatives, a rota); low management (first-line supervisor), operational; technical skill.
  • Interpersonal skill weighs at every level.

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Human resources management in the programme

The five activities

Human resources management is the process of recruiting, selecting, training, appraising and compensating employees, and its problem is to align individual interests with the objectives of the company (Topic 6, Slide 23). In the programme:

  • Recruiting: attract candidates for the twelve inspector posts, since "nobody in the plant holds the inspection certificate"; for example, a job offer to people who hold it.
  • Selecting: choose the twelve best among the candidates attracted, for example with a test of measurement and inspection of a panel. Recruitment builds the pool, selection chooses from it; confusing them is the usual error.
  • Training: action 4, the 900 operators of drilling and sealing learn the new checklists.
  • Appraising: the six-monthly assessment of each operator's work (action 6), a performance appraisal system.
  • Compensating: the quarterly bonus to the lines that meet the target (action 6), an incentive programme that ties the operators' interest to the objective of action 1.

Directing and staffing

Directing is motivation, leadership and communication: action 5, the shift meetings that explain why the programme matters. Staffing is recruiting, selecting and training, plus appraisal, compensation and incentives: actions 3, 4 and 6 (Topic 6, Slide 23).

Key points
  • Recruiting: attract candidates holding the inspection certificate (action 3).
  • Selecting: choose the twelve inspectors among them.
  • Training: action 4, the 900 operators.
  • Appraising: the six-monthly assessment (action 6).
  • Compensating: the quarterly bonus as an incentive programme (action 6).
  • Directing: action 5 (communication, motivation); staffing: actions 3, 4 and 6.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

KPIs and the type of control

Three KPIs

A KPI is a quantified measure of performance against an objective (Examples PDF, Exercise 3; extension). It is the measure of actual performance that controlling compares with the standard (Topic 6, Slide 24).

  1. Customer rejection rate: panels rejected at the customer's incoming inspection divided by panels delivered, per month; the standard is the target of action 1, below one in fifty (2%), from one in twenty-five (4%) today.
  2. Internal defect rate per line: drilling and sealing defects found by the quality cell per hundred panels, per week and per line, which shows the lines that fall behind.
  3. Training coverage: operators of drilling and sealing who have completed the checklist training divided by the 900 planned, per month.

Other valid KPIs: share of panels passing inspection first time, hours of rework per panel, number of lines receiving the bonus.

Type of control of action 7

By frequency, action 7 is periodic control: it takes place every week, not constantly or only occasionally (Topic 6, Slide 25). By when, measuring rejections at the customer is reactive: it acts after the defect has happened. The weekly in-plant count of defects lets the quality cells correct a line before more panels are shipped, which brings it close to interactive control. Proactive control would act before the defects appear, as the training of action 4 does. By area, it controls each production line, a unit of the plant (Topic 6, Slide 25).

Key points
  • Three measurable KPIs linked to the target: rejection rate at the customer, internal defect rate per line, share of operators trained (or inspections passed first time).
  • Action 7 is periodic (weekly) control.
  • By when: it reacts to defects already produced (reactive), with the in-plant measurement allowing correction before the panels ship (interactive part); reasoned answers accepted.

Part (e) · 0.5 points

Easiest and hardest configuration

Easiest: machine bureaucracy

The machine bureaucracy has many rules and very formal procedures in the operating core, highly specialized routine tasks, functional departmentalization, centralized decision-making and an elaborate administrative structure with many layers (Topic 6, Slide 16). Orbis already shows these features: "short, repetitive tasks", "a written work instruction" for every task, decisions "taken at the top" through "seven hierarchical levels", and a methods office that "designs the instructions", which is its technostructure, the part that designs systems and processes (Topic 6, Slide 14). A programme of new written checklists, decided by the board and applied to every line, uses the configuration's own way of coordinating: standardization. It is therefore easiest there.

Hardest: adhocracy

The adhocracy has low standardization and formalization, decentralization as the key to flexibility, and in it the technostructure disappears (Topic 6, Slide 22). Nobody would write the checklists and nobody would impose them across teams that organize their own work, so a plant-wide programme of standard procedures is hardest there.

Alternative answers

The professional bureaucracy is also a reasoned answer for "hardest": highly qualified professionals with standardization of their skills and knowledge, and decentralized decisions (Topic 6, Slide 18), resist a checklist written by analysts for their work. The simple structure has only a strategic apex and an operating core, so it has no technostructure to design the programme (Topic 6, Slide 15). The trap is to answer as in Exercise 3 of the examples PDF, where an agile methodology was hardest in the machine bureaucracy and easiest in the adhocracy: what is easy or hard depends on what is introduced, flexibility or standardization.

Key points
  • Easiest: machine bureaucracy (standardization and very formal procedures in the operating core, routine specialized tasks, centralized decisions, elaborate administrative structure); Orbis already is one, and its methods office is the technostructure that writes the checklists.
  • Hardest: adhocracy (low standardization and formalization, technostructure disappears, decentralization); also accepted: professional bureaucracy, where qualified professionals standardize their skills and decide on their own work, or the simple structure, which has no technostructure.
  • The trap: the opposite of the answer for an agile or flexible methodology (Examples PDF, Exercise 3).

Interpretation and checks

The case covers the four functions as one cycle (Slides 3-4): the target of planning (action 1) is the standard of controlling (action 7), and organizing and human resources management (Slides 11 and 23) carry it out. The two decisions of part (b) show that the type of decision and the level of the manager change together (Slides 5, 7 and 8). The configuration of part (e) is the opposite of the one that suits a flexible methodology, because a quality programme of written procedures relies on the standardization that defines the machine bureaucracy (Slide 16).

M05 — Celeris reusable launcher against an expendable incumbent

Celeris Launch is a Spanish start-up founded in 2021 by four engineers who had worked for six years on the guidance of vertically landing vehicles in a university research group. The university's technology transfer office helped them license the guidance software and set up the company, and a private non-profit foundation, which does no research of its own, funded the last two years of the group's landing experiments. Celeris has its plant in an aerospace park where some twenty firms (structures, avionics, test services) share suppliers and customers; several of its engineers came from those neighbouring firms, bringing with them what they had learnt there. Celeris's small launcher has a reusable first stage: after separation it restarts its engines, deploys grid fins and landing legs, and lands vertically on a barge, so the stage, its engines and its guidance work together in a way no expendable launcher needs. The incumbent, Vektor Space, has built expendable launchers in series for thirty years; its strength is producing single-use stages cheaply, and it has no experience in landing guidance or in refurbishing a stage between flights. Its last three upgrades each cost more than the previous one and raised the payload delivered to orbit per million euros by less than 2%. Celeris's first flights delivered less payload per million euros than Vektor's launcher, but the figure has improved quickly with each landing and reuse. Celeris's chief executive says that "our design will become the standard of the industry as soon as the space agency declares it the official one".

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Vektor's position on its S-curve

Each of Vektor's last three upgrades "cost more than the previous one and raised the payload delivered to orbit per million euros by less than 2%". More effort brings smaller and smaller gains in performance: the expendable technology is in the flat upper part of its S-curve, approaching the limit of the technology [Topic 7, Slide 14].

Celeris's technology as a discontinuous technology

Reusable launch fulfils the same market need (putting payloads in orbit) by building on an entirely new knowledge base (landing guidance, engine restart, refurbishment between flights), which makes it a discontinuous technology [Topic 7, Slide 15]. Its curve behaves like the second technology of Slide 15 (b): "Celeris's first flights delivered less payload per million euros than Vektor's launcher", so it starts below, "but the figure has improved quickly with each landing and reuse", so it is in the steep part of its own S-curve.

What it means for the incumbent

If the trend continues, the second curve crosses Vektor's curve, and further effort on the expendable technology yields little because it is near its limit. Since the new technology is competence-destroying for Vektor (part a), its thirty years of skill in series production of single-use stages do not carry over: to compete it must acquire a new knowledge base rather than keep investing in the old curve.

Key points
  • Vektor: flat upper part of its S-curve, near the limit of the technology (rising cost of each upgrade, gains below 2%).
  • Celeris: a discontinuous technology (same market need, entirely new knowledge base) on a second S-curve that starts below and improves quickly.
  • Risk for Vektor: the second curve can cross the first; its competences do not carry over.

Part (d) · 0.5 points

Which factors of the course could make a reusable design dominant, and what is wrong with the chief executive's statement?

Learning effects

The figure of Celeris "has improved quickly with each landing and reuse": the more the technology is used, the more it is developed and the more efficient it becomes; the sales of each flight generate revenue to reinvest in refining it, and the firm's experience lets it use the technology more productively. Its absorptive capacity, the ability to recognize, assimilate and use new knowledge (here the university guidance research and the knowledge of engineers from the park), speeds this up [Topic 7, Slide 18].

Network externalities

The course defines network externalities as an increase in the value of a good to a user with the number of users, through the installed base and complementary goods, with the example of a video game console [Topic 7, Slide 19]. Applied to launchers (an application of the case, extension of the course example): the installed base would be the number of customers and satellites already flying on the reusable design, and the complementary goods the payload adapters, integration services and ground support built for it; the more of them there are, the more valuable the design is to the next customer.

Dominant design and the chief executive's statement

If these forces work, the reusable architecture could reach usually 50% or more of the market and become the dominant design, pushing the market towards winner-take-all, with alternative designs (expendable launchers) surviving in niches [Topic 7, Slides 17 and 19]. The chief executive's claim does not belong to the course's explanation: a dominant design is a de facto standard, a standard of the industry even if it is not officially enforced or acknowledged; it is selected by the market through learning effects and network externalities, not by an official declaration of the space agency [Topic 7, Slide 17].

Key points
  • Dominant design: a single architecture with usually 50% or more of the market, a de facto standard.
  • Learning effects: each landing and reuse improves the design; revenue reinvested; absorptive capacity.
  • Network externalities: installed base and complementary goods (extension of the console example to launchers, stated as such).
  • Winner-take-all tendency, with alternatives surviving in niches.
  • The chief executive confuses a de facto standard with an officially declared one.

Interpretation and checks

The reusable stage is at once product, radical, architectural and competence-destroying for the incumbent, which is why it threatens Vektor far more than the lighter interstage or the printed injector, both incremental component changes.

  • Component, not architectural: the line keeps its configuration and only gains one station before packing [Topic 7, Slide 13].
Key points
  • Distributed hybrid-electric propulsion: product, radical, architectural.
  • Silicon-anode cell: product (of Litheon), incremental, component.
  • X-ray inspection station: process (fewer defects), incremental, the line keeps its configuration (a component change of the production system).

Part (b) · 0.5 points

For Brenner Turbines, Litheon Cells and Voltaris Aero, is the hybrid-electric propulsion competence-enhancing or competence-destroying?

Brenner Turbines: competence-destroying

Brenner's forty years of knowledge are in "turboprop engines, with their reduction gearboxes and variable-pitch propellers". In the VX-19 the propellers are driven by electric motors and the only turbine, the turbo-generator, is "bought from Kestrel Power". The innovation renders Brenner's existing skills obsolete for this aircraft [Topic 7, Slide 12].

Litheon Cells and Voltaris Aero: competence-enhancing

Litheon makes "cells for electric cars", and the VX-19 needs a battery pack: the innovation builds on its existing knowledge base and opens a new market to it. Voltaris's founders "had designed electric motors for sports cars", the same knowledge the eight motors use [Topic 7, Slide 12].

Why the answers differ

The same innovation enhances the competences of two firms and destroys those of a third: whether an innovation is competence-enhancing or competence-destroying depends on whose perspective is taken, so the firm must always be named [Topic 7, Slide 12].

Key points
  • Brenner: competence-destroying (turboprops, reduction gearboxes and variable-pitch propellers are no longer needed; the turbo-generator comes from Kestrel Power, not from Brenner).
  • Litheon: competence-enhancing (cells for electric cars).
  • Voltaris: competence-enhancing (electric motors for sports cars).
  • One innovation, opposite answers: the course says it depends on the perspective.

Part (c) · 0.5 points

Where are Brenner's turboprop and Litheon's cell technology on their S-curves, and why is hybrid-electric propulsion a discontinuous technology for regional flights?

Brenner's turboprop

The improvement obtained per 100 million euros of development effort falls from $6/1.8 = 3.33\%$ to $3/2.6 = 1.15\%$ and then to $1.2/3.9 = 0.31\%$. Each generation costs more and yields less: on the S-curve of performance against effort, the turboprop is in the flat upper part, approaching the limit of the technology [Topic 7, Slide 14].

Litheon's cells

The cells "gained little for years (from 160 to 190 Wh/kg between 2010 and 2016) and then quickly (300 Wh/kg in 2024)": the slow first part of the S-curve is over and the technology is in its steep middle part [Topic 7, Slide 14]. The path of the specific energy through time is the technology trajectory of the cells, measured as its rate of performance improvement [Topic 7, Slide 10].

A discontinuous technology

Hybrid-electric propulsion fulfils the same market need, carrying 19 passengers on regional routes, by building on an entirely new knowledge base (electric motors, batteries, distributed propulsion), so it is a discontinuous technology [Topic 7, Slide 15]. As the second technology of Slide 15, it starts below the first one ("400 km, against 1,500 km") but improves fast, while the turboprop is near its limit; if the trend continues, the second curve may overtake the first, which is the threat Brenner faces given part (b).

Key points
  • Improvement per 100 million euros: 3.33%, 1.15% and 0.31%: the turboprop is in the flat upper part, near the limit of the technology.
  • Cells: slow start (160 to 190 Wh/kg in six years), then fast growth (300 Wh/kg in 2024): the steep middle part.
  • Discontinuous: same market need (regional passenger transport), entirely new knowledge base (electric propulsion, batteries); starts below (400 km against 1,500 km) and may overtake.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Place Fjordair, the airlines that ordered by the end of year 3, Mercator Regional and Alcudia Air in the adopter categories of the course, and say what Voltaris needs to reach the next category

Cumulative shares

The spread of the VX-19 among the 200 airlines is a technology diffusion, which follows the S-curve of cumulative adopters [Topic 7, Slides 15-16]:

  • after year 1: $4/200 = 2.0\%$, below the 2.5% boundary of the innovators;
  • after year 3: $30/200 = 15.0\%$, between 2.5% and 16% (2.5% + 13.5%), so the buyers of years 2 and 3 are early adopters and the early adopters are not yet complete;
  • the boundary of 16% is $0.16 \times 200 = 32$ airlines: the 33rd airline to order would be the first of the early majority. The categories hold $0.025 \times 200 = 5$, $0.135 \times 200 = 27$, $0.34 \times 200 = 68$, $68$ and $0.16 \times 200 = 32$ airlines.

The airlines of the case

  • Fjordair: innovator. It ordered in the first year, its managers "follow every new propulsion technology closely" (risk-oriented, leading-edge, extremely interested in technological developments), and its short routes make the 400 km range enough [Topic 7, Slide 16].
  • Mercator Regional: early majority. It will order "once ten airlines have flown it for two years without trouble": open to new ideas, but it waits to see how they are received before investing [Topic 7, Slide 16].
  • Alcudia Air: laggard. A small, conservative firm with old aircraft that will "only buy when nobody sells turboprops any more": it invests only once an idea is extremely well established [Topic 7, Slide 16].

What Voltaris needs

The next large group is the early majority (34%, 68 airlines), which waits for evidence: Voltaris must make the experience of the first operators visible (reliability, costs, range in service). Improving range along its S-curve (part c) also widens the set of routes, and so of airlines, for which the aircraft is a real alternative.

Key points
  • Year 1: 4/200 = 2.0% < 2.5%: the first buyers, Fjordair included, are innovators.
  • Year 3: 30/200 = 15.0%, between 2.5% and 16%: diffusion is still in the early adopters; the early majority starts after 32 airlines (16% of 200).
  • Number of airlines per category: 5, 27, 68, 68, 32.
  • Mercator: early majority (waits to see how it is received). Alcudia: laggard.
  • To reach the early majority Voltaris must show the results of the first operators.

Interpretation and checks

The case links the three patterns of Part II of Topic 7: the four pairs classify each innovation (and the competence pair needs a named firm), the S-curves of performance explain why a radical, discontinuous technology threatens an incumbent near the limit of its technology, and the diffusion curve tells how far adoption has gone. Thirty orders sound like many, but at 15% of the market the VX-19 has not yet reached the early majority.

Problem · Topic 7 · Innovation case · 2.5 points · 22 min · Practice item, not an official exercise

M15 — Aurelia standard small-satellite bus

Until a decade ago every small satellite was designed from scratch around its payload. Aurelia Smallsat sells instead the AB-12, a standard bus: structure, power, attitude control, radio and on-board computer are always the same, and the customer's payload is fixed to a standard mechanical and electrical interface. Aurelia grew out of a student satellite programme of its region's polytechnic university, which the company still sponsors and from which it recruits most of its graduates. It started in a science park and incubator run by the regional government, and a national space agency grant paid for the in-orbit demonstration of the first bus. The standard payload interface was not invented by Aurelia: Terravista, an Earth-observation customer, had designed it for its own cameras because it was tired of re-wiring each one to a different satellite, and gave it to Aurelia.

The firm is located in a valley where thirty space firms (payload makers, ground-station operators, test laboratories) buy from and sell to each other and run joint research projects. Two engineers who left Nordsat, a neighbouring rival, joined Aurelia and brought with them Nordsat's know-how on testing radios under vacuum.

During the last year Aurelia made three changes to the AB-12: (1) a new star tracker, more precise, which fits in the same slot with the same connector and data format; (2) deployable solar panels in place of body-mounted ones, a modest gain in power that nevertheless forced Aurelia to move the antennas and rewrite the attitude-control software, because the open panels change how the satellite turns; (3) a cheaper on-board computer that replaces the old one board for board.

Forty AB-12 buses now fly for twenty-two operators. Each flight has raised the reliability of the bus, and Aurelia reinvests the income in it. More and more payload makers sell cameras and radios "ready for the AB-12 interface", ground-station operators offer software for it, and two launch providers sell dispensers sized for it. A Nordsat manager answers that "a design can only become the industry standard if an authority imposes it"; Aurelia's chief executive adds that, once the AB-12 dominates, the firms of the sector "will keep developing completely new buses as much as now".

Part (b) · 0.5 points

Classify the standard bus itself and the three changes of the last year as architectural or component innovations

The standard bus

Compared with satellites "designed from scratch around its payload", the AB-12 separates bus and payload through a standard interface: the overall design of the satellite and the way its parts interact change, an architectural innovation [Topic 7, Slide 13].

The three changes

  • Star tracker: component. It "fits in the same slot with the same connector and data format": one component changes without affecting the overall configuration [Topic 7, Slide 13].
  • Deployable solar panels: architectural. The gain in power is "modest", but the change "forced Aurelia to move the antennas and rewrite the attitude-control software, because the open panels change how the satellite turns": the way the components interact changes, which is what defines an architectural innovation [Topic 7, Slide 13]. The trap is to call it component because it is small: the pair is about the configuration of the system, not about the size of the improvement.
  • On-board computer: component. It "replaces the old one board for board" [Topic 7, Slide 13].
Key points
  • Standard bus with a standard payload interface: architectural (new way bus and payload interact).
  • Star tracker: component. On-board computer: component.
  • Deployable panels: architectural, although the gain is modest (antennas moved, attitude software rewritten).

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Which forces of the course could make the AB-12 the dominant design, and what is wrong with the Nordsat manager's statement?

Learning effects

"Each flight has raised the reliability of the bus, and Aurelia reinvests the income in it": the more the technology is used, the more it is developed and the more effective it becomes, and adoption generates revenue that is reinvested in refining it [Topic 7, Slide 18]. Aurelia's absorptive capacity, its ability to recognize, assimilate and use new knowledge (the university's graduates, the testing know-how from Nordsat), speeds the process [Topic 7, Slide 18].

Network externalities

The value of the AB-12 to an operator grows with the number of other operators that use it [Topic 7, Slide 19]:

  • installed base: "forty AB-12 buses now fly for twenty-two operators";
  • complementary goods: cameras and radios "ready for the AB-12 interface", ground-station software and launch dispensers "sized for it", goods and services that enhance the value of the bus. The more of them there are, the more attractive the bus is to the next operator, which in turn attracts more complements.

Dominant design and the manager's statement

These two forces push the market towards winner-take-all: the AB-12 could reach usually 50% or more of the small-satellite market and become its dominant design, while alternative platforms survive in niche markets [Topic 7, Slides 17 and 19]. The Nordsat manager is wrong for the course: a dominant design is a de facto standard, a standard of the industry even if it is not officially enforced, selected by learning effects and network externalities [Topic 7, Slide 17]. An imposed standard is a different case: a standard set by regulation, that is, by a rule of a public authority that firms must obey, is an extension beyond the course, which gives no role to regulation in the selection of a dominant design.

Key points
  • Learning effects: reliability rises with each flight, income reinvested; absorptive capacity (graduates, Nordsat know-how).
  • Network externalities: installed base (40 buses, 22 operators); complementary goods (payloads, ground software, dispensers).
  • Result: winner-take-all tendency; usually 50% or more of the market.
  • The manager confuses a de facto standard with an imposed one; regulation is not a course factor (extension).

Part (d) · 0.5 points

What does the course say will happen in the sector once a dominant design is selected, and is the chief executive right?

What follows the selection

Once the dominant design is selected, producers and customers focus their efforts on improving their efficiency in manufacturing, delivering, marketing or deploying it, rather than continuing to develop alternative designs [Topic 7, Slide 17]. In this case, effort would go into building AB-12 buses faster and cheaper, integrating payloads on them, and component improvements such as the star tracker and the on-board computer of part (b).

The chief executive's claim

The claim that the firms "will keep developing completely new buses as much as now" contradicts the course: the development of alternative architectures falls after the selection, and the platforms that remain outside the dominant design survive in niche markets [Topic 7, Slides 17 and 19], for example satellites whose payload is too large or too unusual for a standard bus.

Key points
  • After the selection, producers and customers focus on efficiency in manufacturing, delivering, marketing or deploying the dominant design, rather than developing alternatives.
  • The chief executive is wrong: completely new buses will be developed less, not "as much as now".
  • Alternatives survive only in niche markets.

Interpretation and checks

The AB-12 itself is an architectural innovation born from a network (a user, a university, government programmes and a cluster), and its chance of dominating rests on the two forces of Part III of Topic 7, not on an official decision. A small change can be architectural, and after a dominant design emerges the effort of the sector moves from developing alternative designs to making and deploying the dominant one more efficiently.
