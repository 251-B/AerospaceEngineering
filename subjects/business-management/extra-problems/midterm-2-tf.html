F01 — Investment selection criteria and the cost of capital

Ten statements on investment selection (part ii), written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · What the NPV adds up

The NPV of a project is the sum of its net cash flows minus the initial outlay, with every flow counted at its nominal value whatever the year in which it arrives.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

That sum is only the NPV at a discount rate of 0%. The NPV discounts each flow to year 0, $NPV = -A + \sum_t Q_t/(1+k)^t$, so a flow received later is worth less today.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 5 (a); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (a).

Statement 2 · The rate that cancels the NPV

The internal rate of return of a project is the discount rate at which its NPV is equal to zero.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

This is the definition used in the key: the IRR is the rate that makes the NPV equal to 0, which is why a known IRR lets one solve for the initial outlay.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercises 4 and 6.

Statement 3 · Ranking two projects below the cost of capital

Two mutually exclusive projects have IRRs of 9% and 7%, and the cost of capital is 9.5%. Under the IRR criterion the company should undertake the project with the 9% IRR, because it ranks first.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Ranking is not enough: a project is accepted only if its IRR exceeds the cost of capital. Both IRRs are below 9.5%, so both NPVs at 9.5% are negative and neither project is accepted.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 6 (b); 3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 8.

Statement 4 · IRR above the discount rate

A project with conventional cash flows (an outlay followed by positive flows) has an IRR of 10%. If it is valued at a discount rate of 7%, its NPV is positive.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The NPV of such a project falls as the rate rises and is zero at the IRR. At 7%, below the 10% IRR, it is therefore positive (IRR > k).

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 6.

Statement 5 · Flows after the recovery

The payback period takes into account every cash flow of the project, including those received after the initial outlay has been recovered.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The payback is the time it takes to recover the initial investment, so it stops counting at that moment and ignores every later flow. Two projects with the same payback can differ greatly in what they return afterwards.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercises 3 and 5 (b).

Statement 6 · Fisher's rate of one project

Fisher's rate of a project is the discount rate at which the NPV of that project becomes zero.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

That is the IRR. Fisher's rate belongs to a pair of projects: it is the discount rate at which the two NPVs are equal, and it marks where the preferred project changes.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (b); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c).

Statement 7 · The weighted average cost of capital

The capital cost of a company is the average of the cost of its equity and the cost of its liabilities, each weighted by its share in the financial structure.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Topic 3 defines $K_{WACC} = K_{EQ}\,E/(E+L) + K_L\,L/(E+L)$; the key of Exercise 8 uses this weighted cost as the cut-off rate for the IRR.

Source.

Topic 3, Slide 37; 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 8.

Statement 8 · Is equity free

Equity has no cost for the company, because the company does not pay interest on the funds contributed by its owners.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Every financial resource has a cost: liabilities have an explicit cost and equity an implicit (opportunity) cost, which enters the weighted average cost of capital.

Source.

Topic 3, Slide 37; 3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 8 (cost of equity 15%).

Statement 9 · Raising the discount rate

For a project made of an initial outlay followed by positive cash flows, a higher discount rate gives a lower NPV.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Each positive flow is divided by $(1+k)^t$, which grows with $k$, while the outlay at year 0 is not discounted; the NPV therefore decreases as the rate rises.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 6 (b); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c), table of NPVs at 0% and 8%.

Statement 10 · Equal NPVs at a zero rate

If two projects have the same NPV at a discount rate of 0% (the same sum of net cash flows minus the outlay), then 0% is a Fisher's rate of that pair.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Fisher's rate is the rate at which both NPVs are equal; at 0% both NPVs equal their undiscounted sums, which are the same, so $r = 0\%$ solves the equation.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c), pair A–B ($x = 1$, FR = 0%), and Exercise 6 (c).

F06 — Cash-flow profiles, timing of flows and payback

Ten statements on investment selection (part ii), written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · A negative flow after the outlay

The payback criterion cannot be applied to a project whose net cash flow in year 1 is negative.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The payback still follows the cumulative flows: the negative flow only delays the recovery. Project X of Exercise 3 (outlay 1,200, flows −200, 300, 500, 200, 400, 400) accumulates −200, 100, 600, 800 and 1,200, so its payback is 5 years, and the key even prefers it.

Source.

3_II_NPV IRR.pdf, Exercise 3, and its key (Payback X = 5 years).

Statement 2 · Same total, different timing

Two projects require the same outlay and their flows add up to the same total, but one of them receives most of its money in year 1 and the other in year 3. At any positive discount rate, the project that receives its money earlier has the higher NPV.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Each flow is divided by $(1+k)^t$, and this factor grows with $t$ when $k > 0$; moving money to an earlier year reduces the discount applied to it, so the present value of the same total is larger.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 5 (a) (each flow discounted with its own exponent); Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (a).

Statement 3 · Where the profile meets the axes

On a graph of the NPV against the discount rate, the profile of a project meets the vertical axis at its undiscounted net sum and the horizontal axis at its IRR.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

At $r = 0$ every discount factor is 1, so the NPV is the sum of the flows minus the outlay; the profile reaches zero at the rate that cancels the NPV, which is the IRR. These are the two columns, NPV at 0% and IRR, that the key tabulates before drawing the graph.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c) and Exercise 6 (c), tables of NPV at 0% and IRR.

Statement 4 · Very large discount rates

As the discount rate grows without limit, the NPV of a project made of an outlay followed by positive flows tends to zero.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The discounted flows $Q_t/(1+k)^t$ tend to zero, but the outlay at year 0 is not discounted, so the NPV tends to minus the initial outlay, $-A$, not to zero.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c) (schematic graph of the NPV profiles); 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 5 (a).

Statement 5 · The steeper profile and the IRR

Two projects have the same NPV at a discount rate of 0%. The one whose NPV profile falls faster as the rate rises has the lower IRR.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Both profiles start from the same point on the vertical axis, so the one that falls faster cuts the horizontal axis first, at a lower rate. In Exercise 5 of the examples, A and B both have an NPV of 100,000 at 0%; B falls faster (6,447.19 against 28,120.71 at 8%) and its IRR is lower (8.62% against 11.65%).

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (b) and (c).

Statement 6 · Payback with uneven flows

A project requires 100,000 € and generates 20,000 €, 50,000 € and 60,000 € in years 1, 2 and 3. If each flow arrives evenly during its year, its payback period is 2.5 years.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

After two years $20{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 70{,}000$ € are recovered; the remaining 30,000 € come from the 60,000 € of year 3 in $30{,}000/60{,}000 = 0.5$ years, so the payback is 2.5 years.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (a) (fraction of the year in which the outlay is recovered).

Statement 7 · Does payback discount

Because the payback period adds up the flows year by year until the outlay is recovered, it already takes the time value of money into account.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The payback adds the flows at their nominal value, with no discount factor; it only counts how long the recovery takes. The time value of money enters through the NPV, which divides each flow by $(1+k)^t$.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercises 3 and 5 (b) (payback from undiscounted flows) against Exercise 5 (a) (NPV).

Statement 8 · Slightly negative NPV

A project has an NPV of 100,000 at 0% and an NPV of −826.45 at the company's discount rate of 10%. It should be accepted, because its NPV is large at 0% and only slightly negative at 10%.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The decision is taken with the NPV at the company's own rate: a negative NPV at 10%, however small, means the project does not cover the cost of its funds (its IRR, 9.91%, is below 10%), so it is rejected. This is project A of Exercise 6 of the examples.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 6 (b).

Statement 9 · Deferring a debt for two years

A creditor who can invest at 5% a year is asked to defer a debt of 1,000 € for two years. The amount he should require at the end of year 2 is 1,100 €.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Capitalization is compound: $C_n = C_0(1+i)^n$, so $C_2 = 1{,}000 \times 1.05^2 = 1{,}102.50$ €. The figure 1,100 € adds the interest of the first year twice and forgets the interest earned on the interest.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 1.

Statement 10 · New debt with a constant financial structure

A company with capital of 6,000,000, reserves of 4,800,000 and debt of 7,200,000 retains 1,200,000 of profit in year 1. To keep its financial structure unchanged, it can take on 800,000 of new debt.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Equity is $6{,}000{,}000 + 4{,}800{,}000 = 10{,}800{,}000$, 60% of the total of 18,000,000. After retaining profit equity is 12,000,000, so the total must be $12{,}000{,}000/0.6 = 20{,}000{,}000$ and the debt $0.4 \times 20{,}000{,}000 = 8{,}000{,}000$: the new debt is $8{,}000{,}000 - 7{,}200{,}000 = 800{,}000$.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 2.

F07 — Ranking conflicts, Fisher's rate and the cut-off rate

Ten statements on investment selection (part ii), written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · Reading Fisher's rate on the graph

On a graph of the NPV against the discount rate, the Fisher's rate of two projects is read as the horizontal coordinate of the point where their two profiles cross.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Where the profiles cross, both projects have the same NPV, which is the condition $NPV_A = NPV_B$ that defines Fisher's rate; the horizontal axis carries the discount rate, so the crossing point gives the rate.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c); 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (b).

Statement 2 · The rate above which nothing is chosen

When several mutually exclusive projects are compared, the discount rate above which no project is chosen is the lowest of their IRRs.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Above the lowest IRR the other projects can still have a positive NPV. No project is chosen only when every NPV is negative, that is above the highest IRR: 18.02% (project C) in Exercise 5 of the examples and 12.87% (project C) in Exercise 6.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercises 5 (c) and 6 (c), conclusions.

Statement 3 · Which project lies below the crossing

When the NPV profiles of two projects cross once at a positive Fisher's rate, the project with the higher IRR is preferred at every discount rate below that Fisher's rate.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

It is the other way round: the higher-IRR project is preferred above the Fisher's rate (as long as its NPV is positive), and below it the project with the larger NPV at 0% leads. In Exercise 5 of the examples, A is preferred to C below 4.77% even though C has the higher IRR (18.02% against 11.65%).

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c), pair A–C.

Statement 4 · Ranking under a budget

When a budget limits a set of independent projects that cannot be repeated, the key of the problem sheet undertakes them in decreasing order of NPV until the budget is used up.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

In Exercise 7 every project has a positive NPV at 10%, and with 40,000 € the key takes D, C and F (the three largest NPVs, outlays $13{,}000 + 12{,}000 + 15{,}000 = 40{,}000$ €).

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 7.

Statement 5 · What makes the rankings disagree

The NPV and IRR rankings of two projects can only disagree when the projects require different initial outlays.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The timing of the flows alone can make the profiles cross. With the same outlay of 100,000 €, a project paying 120,000 € in year 1 has an IRR of 20% and an NPV of 20,000 € at 0%, while one paying 135,000 € in year 2 has an IRR of about 16.2% and an NPV of 35,000 € at 0%; below their Fisher's rate of 12.5% the NPV ranks the second first and the IRR the first.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (c) (profiles that cross); 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 9 (b). The figures of the reasoning are an example worked for the bank.

Statement 6 · Independent projects

If two projects are independent and both have a positive NPV at the cost of capital, the company should undertake only the one with the higher NPV.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Choosing only one is the rule for mutually exclusive projects ("only one can be accepted"). Independent projects are each accepted when their NPV is positive, as in Exercise 7, where every project with a positive NPV is undertaken while the budget allows.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (statement: mutually exclusive projects); 3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 7.

Statement 7 · Cheaper liabilities in the mix

If a company replaces part of its equity with liabilities whose cost is lower than that of equity, and both costs stay unchanged, its capital cost falls.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

$K_{WACC} = K_{EQ}\,E/(E+L) + K_L\,L/(E+L)$ is a weighted average; moving weight from the dearer resource to the cheaper one, with the two costs fixed, lowers the average.

Source.

Topic 3, Slide 37.

Statement 8 · Comparing the yield with the cost of debt

A company is financed 30% with equity at a cost of 15% and 70% with liabilities at an average cost of 8%. It should accept a project with a yield of 8.5%, because the yield exceeds the cost of the liabilities that finance most of it.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The cut-off rate is the weighted cost of all the funds, $K = 0.30 \times 15\% + 0.70 \times 8\% = 10.1\%$, not the cost of the debt alone. Since $8.5\% < 10.1\%$ the project is rejected; a project yielding 18.5% would be accepted.

Source.

3_II_NPV_NPV IRR_solutions.pdf, Exercise 8; Topic 3, Slide 37.

Statement 9 · A pair whose only crossing is at zero

For two two-year projects whose only Fisher's rate is 0%, one of the two projects is preferred to the other at every positive discount rate.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The two profiles meet only at $r = 0$, so for $r > 0$ one stays above the other. In Exercise 6 of the examples, A and B cross at 0% and B is never preferred for $r > 0$.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 6 (c), pair A–B and conclusion; Exercise 5 (c), pair A–B.

Statement 10 · The quadratic of a two-year IRR

For a project with an outlay $A$ and flows $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ in years 1 and 2, the IRR is found by writing $x = 1 + r$ and taking the positive root of $A x^2 - Q_1 x - Q_2 = 0$.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Setting $-A + Q_1/x + Q_2/x^2 = 0$ and multiplying by $-x^2$ gives $A x^2 - Q_1 x - Q_2 = 0$; the product of its roots is $-Q_2/A < 0$, so exactly one root is positive and the IRR is that root minus 1.

Source.

Examples PDF, Exercise 5 (b) ($500{,}000x^2 - 200{,}000x - 400{,}000 = 0$) and Exercise 6 (b).

F02 — Production systems, costs and operating leverage

Ten statements on the production function, written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · What a Gantt chart shows

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that normally shows the dependency relationships between the activities of a project, which makes it the preferred tool for planning complex projects.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The Gantt chart shows the scheduled start and stop dates of each step, but it normally does not show the dependency relationships between activities and it is used for non-complex projects.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 31.

Statement 2 · Flexibility and cost

Flexibility and cost are two objectives of the production function that can contradict each other.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The four objectives (cost, quality, time of delivery and flexibility) can conflict, and flexibility - cost is one of the pairs named by the course: making many different products in small amounts loses the savings of standard, large-scale production.

Source.

Topic 4, Slides 5 and 6.

Statement 3 · Characteristics of rigid production

Rigid (continuous) production is characterized by flexible volumes of production, highly qualified workers and equipment for general use.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Those are the characteristics of flexible or intermittent production. Rigid production has big volumes, standard products sold at a low price and is capital intensive.

Source.

Topic 4, Slides 10 and 14.

Statement 4 · Flexible versus rigid below the crossing volume

Below the volume at which the total cost lines of the two systems cross, a flexible production system is cheaper than a rigid one, because the rigid system starts with higher fixed costs.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The rigid system's total cost starts higher (more fixed cost) and grows more slowly (less variable cost per unit), so at low volumes (zone A) the flexible system is cheaper and at high volumes (zone B) the rigid one is.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 17.

Statement 5 · Bottlenecks in a product layout

In a product layout the pace of the line is set by the slowest machine, and a problem in one machine affects the whole process.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Both are disadvantages of the product layout listed by the course: time is set by the slowest machine (bottlenecks must be avoided) and a failure of one machine stops the whole flow.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 25.

Statement 6 · Inventories under Just in Time

Just in Time production relies on large safety inventories of finished products so that every customer request can be served at once.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Just in Time is based on requests and eliminates inventories; production is pulled by the orders, not served from stock.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 18.

Statement 7 · Labour productivity

Labour productivity is a total productivity index, because labour is the main factor of production of most firms.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Labour productivity (goods and services divided by labour) relates the output to one factor only, so it is a partial productivity index; the total index divides by labour, capital, raw materials and energy together.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 34.

Statement 8 · A higher price and the break-even point

If the selling price rises while the fixed costs and the variable cost per unit stay the same, the break-even point falls.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

$Q_0 = FC/(P - VC)$: a higher price raises the unit margin $m = P - VC$, and the same fixed costs are covered with fewer units.

Source.

Topic 4, Slide 37.

Statement 9 · Sensitivity just above the break-even point

A firm that sells slightly more than its break-even volume sees its operating profit change by much more than 1% when its sales change by 1%.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

$OL = Q/(Q - Q^*)$: when $Q$ is just above $Q^*$ the denominator is small, so the operating leverage is large and the effect on profit is more than proportional.

Source.

Topic 4, Slides 40 and 42.

Statement 10 · Margin of safety in monetary units

The margin of safety in monetary units is the difference between the actual volume and the break-even volume multiplied by the selling price.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

In monetary units the margin of safety is $(Q - Q^*)\,m$: the units above the break-even point multiplied by the unit margin, not by the price, because each extra unit adds only its margin to the profit.

Source.

Statement 3 · Doubling output and every input

A repair shop that doubles the value of its services while the labour, capital, raw materials and energy it uses also double keeps the same total productivity.

Ten statements on entrepreneurship and innovation, written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · Creativity of an organization

The creativity of an organization depends not only on the creativity of its individuals but also on the social processes and contextual factors that shape the way those individuals interact.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The course defines organizational creativity as a function of the creativity of the individuals and of the social processes and contextual factors that shape their interaction; it is not a simple sum, because structure, routines and incentives can thwart or amplify individual creativity.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 5.

Statement 2 · A minor change called radical

A new coating that slightly extends the life of an existing turbine blade, without changing the blade or the engine, is a radical innovation, because it improves an aerospace product.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

A relatively minor change to existing practices is an incremental innovation; a radical innovation is very new and different from prior solutions. Improving a product does not make an innovation radical: the size of the departure from prior solutions does.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 12.

Statement 3 · Same innovation, two firms

The same innovation can be competence-enhancing for one firm and competence-destroying for another firm of the same industry.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Whether an innovation enhances or destroys competences depends on whose perspective is taken: it enhances the competences of a firm whose existing knowledge base it builds on, and destroys those of a firm whose competences it does not use or renders obsolete.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 12.

Statement 4 · A new display in the same panel

Replacing the analogue altimeter of a light aircraft with a digital one that fits the same slot of the instrument panel, without changing how the other systems of the aircraft interact, is an architectural innovation.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

A change to one component that does not significantly affect the overall configuration of the system is a component (modular) innovation. An architectural innovation changes the overall design of the system or the way its components interact with each other, which this change does not do.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 13.

Statement 5 · Where the S-curve is steepest

On the S-curve of technology performance, performance improves fastest when the first units of effort are invested in a new technology, and then grows more and more slowly.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Plotted against effort, performance improves slowly at first, then quickly, and finally flattens as it approaches the limit of the technology. The steepest part is in the middle of the curve, not at its beginning.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 14.

Statement 6 · Effort near the limit

When a technology reaches the flat upper part of its S-curve, additional effort brings only small improvements in performance, because the technology is approaching its limit.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The upper part of the S-curve flattens towards the dotted line of the limit of the technology drawn on the slide, so each additional unit of effort yields a smaller gain in performance.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 14.

Statement 7 · A new knowledge base

A discontinuous technology fulfils a similar market need to the existing technology by building on an entirely new knowledge base, and at first its performance may be lower than that of the technology it later overtakes.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

This is the course's definition of a discontinuous technology; the second S-curve of the slide may start below the first technology and later cross it and reach a higher performance.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 15.

Statement 8 · What the diffusion curve plots

The S-curve of technology diffusion plots the performance of a technology against the effort invested in developing it.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Performance against effort is the S-curve of technology performance. The diffusion S-curve plots the cumulative share of adopters against time (2.5%, 16%, 50%, 84% and 100% at the boundaries of the adopter categories), because diffusion is the spread of a technology through a population.

Source.

Topic 7, Slides 14-16.

Statement 9 · The two majorities

The early majority and the late majority each account for about 34% of the adopters of a technology, so together they make up about two thirds of the market.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The bell-shaped curve of market share gives 34% to the early majority and 34% to the late majority, 68% in total; innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%) and laggards (16%) make up the rest.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 16.

Statement 10 · How a design becomes dominant

A design becomes dominant only when a regulator or standards body officially enforces it; learning effects and network externalities play no part in its selection.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

A dominant design is a de facto standard: it dominates its product category (usually 50% or more of the market) even if it is not officially enforced. The course gives learning effects and network externalities (installed base and complementary goods) as the reasons why dominant designs are selected.

Source.

F14 — Sources of innovation, creativity and collaborative networks

Ten statements on entrepreneurship and innovation, written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · Pilots who build their own tools

A group of glider pilots who design a better variometer mount for their own cockpits are a source of innovation recognised by the course, even though they are neither a firm nor a research institution.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Slide 3 lists individuals among the sources of innovation, both the lone inventor and users who design solutions for their own needs; the pilots are such users.

Topic 7, Slide 3.

Statement 2 · Who has the incentive to differentiate

According to the course, government-funded entities face stronger incentives than firms to develop differentiating new products and services.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The course says the opposite: firms face strong incentives to develop differentiating new products and services, which may give them an advantage over non-profit or government-funded entities.

Topic 7, Slide 3.

Statement 3 · One origin per innovation

In the course's view of the sources of innovation as a complex system, every innovation must come from exactly one of its components, so the links between the players never give rise to an innovation by themselves.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

An innovation may emerge primarily from one or more components of the system or from the linkages between them, and the course names those linkages as the most important source of innovation.

Topic 7, Slide 4.

Statement 4 · Judging which ideas deserve effort

Being able to analyse which ideas are worth pursuing and which are not is one of the most important intellectual abilities for creative thinking.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Slide 5 lists three key intellectual abilities: looking at problems in unconventional ways, analysing which ideas are worth pursuing and which are not, and articulating ideas so as to convince others that they are worthwhile.

Topic 7, Slide 5.

Statement 5 · What creative ability depends on

An individual's creative ability depends only on intellectual abilities and knowledge; personality, motivation and the environment have no bearing on it.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The course makes creative ability a function of six factors: intellectual abilities, knowledge, style of thinking, personality, motivation and environment. Leaving out the last four contradicts that definition.

Statement 6 · Routines that hold talent back

An engineering firm full of highly creative designers can still show low creativity as an organization if its structure, routines and incentives stifle what those designers propose.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Organizational creativity is not a simple aggregate of individual creativity: structure, routines and incentives can thwart individual creativity or amplify it.

In the rank order of the sources of research work reported by firms, sponsored university research comes before internal R&D within divisions.

For research work, central corporate research is first, internal R&D within divisions second and sponsored university research third, so university research comes after the divisions, not before them.

Statement 9 · Why universities open transfer offices

Many universities have set up technology transfer offices to increase the degree to which their research leads to commercial innovation.

T / F

This is the role the course gives to universities as a source of innovation: technology transfer offices turn more university research into commercial innovation.

Statement 10 · Clusters without joint research

Firms of a region that share a common technology but deal with each other only as buyers and suppliers cannot form a technology cluster, because a cluster requires joint research.

F15 — Spillovers, trajectories, diffusion and learning effects

Ten statements on entrepreneurship and innovation, written for this portal in the style of the True/False exercise of the course's example exam. State whether each one is true (T) or false (F) and justify it briefly.

Statement 1 · Know-how that leaves with the engineers

When avionics engineers move from one firm to other firms of the same region and take their know-how with them, the spread of that knowledge is a technological spillover, a positive externality of the first firm's R&D.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

A technological spillover is a positive externality of R&D resulting from the spread of knowledge across organizational or regional boundaries; here the knowledge crosses the boundary of the firm that paid for it.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 9.

Statement 2 · Two uses of a trajectory

A technology trajectory is most often used to represent either the rate at which a technology's performance improves or its rate of adoption in the marketplace.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The course defines the technology trajectory as the path a technology follows through time and names these two uses; the S-curve of performance and the diffusion curve are the two patterns it then studies.

Source.

Topic 7, Slides 10 and 14-16.

Statement 3 · Faster fuselage assembly

A new riveting technique that halves the hours needed to assemble each fuselage, while the aircraft delivered are unchanged, is a product innovation, because aircraft are what the firm sells.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The novelty lies in the technique of producing, not in the output, so it is a process innovation, aimed at the efficiency of production (more output in a given time). A product innovation would be embodied in the aircraft themselves.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 11.

Statement 4 · Revenue and experience feed the technology

Learning effects arise because a technology that is adopted generates sales revenue that can be reinvested in refining it, and because firms that accumulate experience with it find ways to use it more productively.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

These are the two mechanisms of Slide 18: the more a technology is used, the more it is developed and the more effective and efficient it becomes, which is one reason why dominant designs are selected.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 18.

Statement 5 · Not building on a firm's knowledge

From the perspective of a given firm, an innovation is competence-destroying only when it renders that firm's competencies obsolete; an innovation that simply does not build on them is competence-enhancing.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The course counts both cases as competence-destroying: an innovation is competence-destroying for a firm if it does not build on the firm's existing competencies or renders them obsolete. Competence-enhancing requires building on the firm's existing knowledge base.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 12.

Statement 6 · A lighter seat in the same rails

Replacing the passenger seats of an airliner with lighter seats that fit the same floor rails and the same cabin layout is a component (modular) innovation.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

The change affects one component and does not significantly alter the overall configuration of the cabin or the way its components interact, which is the course's definition of a component innovation.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 13.

Statement 7 · The horizontal axis of the performance curve

On the course's S-curve of technology performance, performance is plotted against calendar time.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

The S-curve of technology performance plots performance against the effort invested in the technology. Time is the horizontal axis of the diffusion curves (cumulative adopters and market share).

Source.

Topic 7, Slides 14 and 16.

Statement 8 · The need served by a discontinuous technology

A discontinuous technology is one that serves a market need different from that of the existing technology.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

A discontinuous technology fulfils a similar market need to the existing technology; what is new is the knowledge base it builds on, which is entirely new.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 15.

Statement 9 · When the early majority has adopted

When the last member of the early majority adopts a technology, the cumulative share of adopters reaches 84% of the population.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: False (F)

Cumulatively, innovators, early adopters and early majority add up to 2.5% + 13.5% + 34% = 50%. The 84% line is reached when the late majority has also adopted; only the laggards (16%) remain.

Source.

Topic 7, Slide 16.

Statement 10 · Software for an electronic flight bag

The charts, performance calculators and other applications available for an electronic flight bag platform raise its value to pilots; the course calls such goods complementary goods.

T / F

Answer and reasoning

Answer: True (T)

Complementary goods are additional goods and services that enable or enhance the value of another good, like the games and online services of a video game console on Slide 19. The number of users of the platform would instead be its installed base.

Source.
