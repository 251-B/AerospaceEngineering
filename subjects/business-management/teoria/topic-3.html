Financial Management (I): Financial Statements and Financial Structure

Economic and financial structure, the income statement, the balance sheet, the sources of financing, the debt-to-equity ratio and the weighted average cost of capital.

📖 Source: Universidad Carlos III de Madrid · Topic 3_Financial Management (I).pdf (Prof. Carmen Paz-Aparicio), Slides 1-37
Official material Topic 3 Financial Management (I).pdf T3_Financial Statements_EXERCISES.pdf

Central idea: every firm has two sides. The economic structure (assets: what is owned) is where the money is invested and it generates profitability; the financial structure (equity + liabilities: what is owed) is where the money comes from and it has a cost. The firm creates value when profitability > cost. Two statements describe the firm: the income statement (performance over a period: revenues − expenses = net income) and the balance sheet (position at a point in time: assets = liabilities + equity). The financial structure is the mix of self-financing, external equity, long-term and short-term debt, measured by the debt-to-equity ratio and priced by the weighted average cost of capital.

Part I — Introduction: economic and financial structure

§ 1 The finance area and its two decisions [Slide 3]

Finance area [Slide 3]

The finance area of a company refers to the collection and management of the funds (capital) used by the company.

The finance manager faces two important types of decisions while managing a company:

QuestionDecision
What to invest in and when?Investing decisions
How to obtain the money to invest?Financing decisions

§ 2 Economic structure and financial structure [Slide 4]

The two structures are two views of the same money: the financial structure says where each euro came from, the economic structure says what it was spent on. That is why, in the balance sheet, both sides always add up to the same total (§8).

§ 3 The objective: maximize value [Slide 5]

The slide draws the balance as two columns: ASSETS on the left and EQUITY + LIABILITIES on the right. Assets generate profitability; equity and liabilities carry a cost (the arrows show that the profitability of the assets has to pay for the cost of the funds).

$$\text{OBJECTIVE: MAXIMIZE VALUE} \iff \text{PROFITABILITY} > \text{COST}$$

§ 4 What the company's decisions focus on [Slide 6]

Financing decisions:

  • they refer to the choice of equity or debt;
  • the financial resources that a company may use can be internal equity (self-financing: retained earnings), external equity or debt;
  • financial decisions involve decisions about the capital structure and decisions about the dividends.

Investing decisions:

  • they consist in the allocation of capital to those investment projects from which we expect future profits;
  • companies will invest in those projects that will create value for them (profits > costs).

Part II — Financial statements

§ 5 The income statement: basic format [Slide 7]

  • The first statement prepared is the income statement.
  • The income statement reports the business performance for a specific period (a quarter, a semester, a year).
  • A simple format is:
$$\text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses} = \text{Net income}$$

§ 6 Revenues and expenses [Slide 8]

Revenues and expenses [Slide 8]

Revenues are earned for the sale of goods or services. Revenues occur when the sale is made; the payment may or may not have been received. Examples: sales, service revenue and interest revenue.

Expenses are incurred when a business receives goods and services. Like revenues, payment may or may not have been made. Examples: salaries expense, utility expense and interest expense.

This is the key rule for the exercises: a revenue is recorded when the sale happens, not when the customer pays; an expense is recorded when the good or service is received (consumed), not when the firm pays. The difference between them and the cash movements is what creates receivables (sold but not collected) and payables (received but not paid) in the balance sheet.

Each subtotal answers a different question: gross profit measures what is earned on the products themselves; income from operations adds the cost of running the business; income before taxes adds everything not related to the primary operations; net income is what is left for the owners after taxes.

7.2 Cost of goods sold and operating expenses [Slide 10]

  • Cost of goods sold represents the expense a business incurred to buy or make a product for resale. Example on the slide: a book store buys a book for 25 USD and then sells it for 32 USD; the cost of goods sold is 25 USD (and the gross profit on that sale is $32 - 25 = 7$ USD).
  • Operating expenses are the usual expenses incurred in operating a business. Accounts such as salaries expense, utility expenses and depreciation expenses are all shown in this section.

7.3 Non-operating items and income taxes [Slide 11]

  • Non-operating items are revenues, expenses, gains and losses that do not relate to the company's primary operations. Accounts include interest expense and gains and losses on the sale of equipment and investments.
  • Income taxes are computed by multiplying income before taxes by the income tax rate.

Example on the slide: income before taxes = 50,000 USD and income tax rate = 30%:

$$\text{Income taxes} = 50{,}000 \times 0.30 = 15{,}000 \text{ USD}, \qquad \text{Net income} = 50{,}000 - 15{,}000 = 35{,}000 \text{ USD}$$

§ 8 The balance sheet: purpose and basic equation [Slide 12]

The purpose of the balance sheet is to report the financial position of an accounting entity at a particular point in time (a photograph on one date, while the income statement is a film of a period).

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$

§ 9 Assets, liabilities and equity [Slide 13]

  • Assets are economic resources owned by a company. Examples: cash, accounts receivable, supplies, buildings and equipment.
  • Liabilities are the company's debt or obligations. Examples: accounts payable, unearned revenues and bonds payable.
  • Equity is the residual balance:
$$\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$$
  • Equity is commonly called stockholders' equity if the business is a corporation, as it represents the financing provided by the stockholders along with the earnings from the business not paid out as dividends (retained earnings).

The residual definition is why the balance sheet always balances: equity is whatever makes the two sides equal. If the firm earns a profit and keeps it, both assets (more cash or receivables) and equity (more retained earnings) grow by the same amount.

§ 10 Current and non-current assets [Slides 14-15]

$$\text{Current assets} + \text{Non-current assets} = \text{Total assets}$$
  • Current assets are assets that will be used or turned into cash within one year. Examples: cash, accounts receivable, inventory, short-term investments, supplies and prepaids [Slide 14].
  • Non-current assets are the remainder of the assets. They include long-term investments, land, building, equipment and patents [Slide 15].

§ 11 Current and long-term liabilities [Slide 16]

$$\text{Current liabilities} + \text{Long-term liabilities} = \text{Total liabilities}$$
  • Current liabilities are obligations that will be paid in cash (or other services) or satisfied within the coming year.
  • Long-term liabilities are obligations that will not be paid or satisfied within the year.

The classification looks at when the obligation is due, measured from the balance-sheet date. A loan that is repaid in instalments is therefore split: the instalments due in the next twelve months are current, the rest are long-term.

§ 12 Stockholders' equity [Slide 17]

$$\text{Contributed capital} + \text{Retained earnings} = \text{Total stockholders' equity}$$

Contributed capital is what the stockholders put into the company; retained earnings are the earnings not paid out as dividends ([Slide 13]).

Part III — The financial structure of the company

§ 15 Financing decisions and the capital structure [Slide 20]

Financing decisions require the analysis of the different financial alternatives.

  • Business financing consists in the collection of the financial resources needed to develop the company's activity.
  • The combination of all the possible financial sources is known as the CAPITAL STRUCTURE or FINANCIAL STRUCTURE.

The two classifications cut the same list in the same place: equity is the company's own resources (its owners' money, whether contributed or retained), and liabilities are external resources (money owed to third parties).

§ 17 Internal equity: self-financing [Slides 23-24]

Self-financing [Slide 23]

The retained earnings (resources generated within the company in its normal activity) constitute the self-financing of the company. These are the resources generated by the company.

Profits can be:

  • retained in the company = self-financing;
  • spread (distributed) = dividends.

Two ratios describe the split:

$$\text{Pay-out} = \frac{\text{Dividends}}{\text{Profits}}, \qquad \text{Self-financing (\%)} = \frac{\text{Retained earnings}}{\text{Profits}}$$

Since every euro of profit is either distributed or retained, $\text{Dividends} + \text{Retained earnings} = \text{Profits}$, and dividing by the profits:

$$\text{Pay-out} + \text{Self-financing (\%)} = 1$$

Example on the slide: profits = 100,000 €, dividends = 75,000 €, retained earnings = 25,000 €:

$$\text{Pay-out} = \frac{75{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.75 = 75\%, \qquad \text{Self-financing} = \frac{25{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.25 = 25\%$$

[Slide 24] repeats the map of the financial structure, now highlighting external equity.

§ 18 External equity [Slides 25-26]

18.1 Equity capital [Slide 25]

When a business is created, owners put some funding into the business to finance assets.

Equity capital [Slide 25]

Equity capital are the funds raised from owners at the start of a business (initial capital) and further rights-issues (issuing new shares), used to finance the investments of the company.

Two features distinguish it from debt:

  • it is not to be paid back to the investor at any expiring date;
  • a share of capital can only be converted into cash by sale or transmission (the investor recovers the money by selling the share to someone else, not by asking the company for it).

18.2 Equity securities: shares [Slide 26]

  • A share of stock is one of a finite number of equal portions in the capital of a company, entitling the owner to a proportion of the distributed, non-reinvested profits, known as dividends, and to a portion of the value of the company in case of liquidation.
  • Shares are equity securities.
  • A share has different values: nominal, accounting (book), intrinsic and market value.
  • New shares are issued in a rights-issue (capital increase).
  • Payment of dividends is not compulsory.

§ 19 Long-term external resources: bonds [Slide 29]

Bond [Slide 29]

A bond is a debt security, in which the authorized issuer owes the holders a debt and is obliged to repay the principal and interest (the coupon) at a later date, termed maturity.

It is a loan in the form of a security:

IssuerBorrower
Bond holderLender

Bonds enable the issuer to finance long-term investments with external funds.

[Slide 30] repeats the map, highlighting long-term loans and bank credits.

§ 20 Long-term loans and bank credits [Slide 31]

  • Long-term loans: the company receives an amount of money, which needs to be paid back at a certain time. This service is provided at a cost: the debt interest.
  • Bank credits: the financial institution makes available a maximum amount of money to the customer. The customer will then borrow any amount of money under the limit, paying interests only for the amount borrowed (interest rates are higher).

The difference: with a loan the whole amount is received and pays interest from the first day; with a credit the firm draws only what it needs and pays interest only on that, at a higher rate.

[Slide 32] repeats the map, highlighting leasing.

§ 21 Leasing [Slide 33]

Leasing [Slide 33]

Leasing is a process by which a firm can obtain the use of a certain fixed asset for which it must pay a series of contractual, periodic payments. The lessee (receiver of the services or the assets under the lease contract) has the right to buy the asset at the end of the agreed period.

Types: finance leasing and operating leasing (the slide names the two types and does not develop them further).

[Slide 34] repeats the map, highlighting the short-term external resources.

§ 22 Short-term external resources [Slide 35]

  • Accounts payable: financing from suppliers (trade credit) arises when payment of goods is done some time after their reception. Suppliers offer the company the possibility to defer the payment.
  • Bank financing:
  • loans and credits, with the same characteristics as the long-term ones;
  • trade discounts: the financial institution makes a note (bill of exchange, promissory note, ...) available to the customer before its expiring date, having previously deducted some expenses (interest rate, commission, ...).
  • Factoring: the company sells to a factoring company a credit from a customer, before it expires. The factoring company is in charge of its reimbursement, assuming the risk of insolvency.

In the exercises, accounts payable are the most frequent short-term resource: every purchase "paid at 60 or 90 days" is trade credit, and while it is unpaid it appears as a current liability.

§ 23 Measuring the financial structure: leverage [Slide 36]

The financial structure is the combination of the different financial resources. With $E$ = equity and $L$ = liabilities:

$$\%\,\text{Equity} = \frac{E}{E+L}, \qquad \%\,\text{Liabilities} = \frac{L}{E+L}, \qquad \text{Debt-to-equity ratio} = \frac{L}{E}$$

The debt-to-equity ratio is used to evaluate a company's financial leverage. It measures the degree to which a company is financing its operations through debt versus wholly-owned funds. It reflects the ability of shareholder equity to cover all outstanding debts in the event of a business downturn. Higher leverage ratios tend to indicate a company or stock with higher risk to shareholders.

Example on the slide: total liabilities + equity = 1,000 M€, equity = 700 M€, liabilities = 300 M€.

$$\%\,\text{Equity} = \frac{700}{1{,}000} = 70\%, \qquad \%\,\text{Liabilities} = \frac{300}{1{,}000} = 30\%, \qquad \frac{L}{E} = \frac{300}{700} = 0.43$$

So the company has 0.43 € of debt for every euro of equity. The first two ratios always add up to one, because $\frac{E}{E+L} + \frac{L}{E+L} = \frac{E+L}{E+L} = 1$.

§ 24 The cost of the financial resources: WACC [Slide 37]

All the financial resources have a cost:

  • implicit cost (opportunity cost): equity. Nothing is paid by contract, but the owners give up what they could earn elsewhere with that money;
  • explicit cost: liabilities. The interest is fixed by contract.
Capital cost [Slide 37]

The capital cost is the weighted average cost associated to all the financial resources obtained by the company ($K$):

$$K_{WACC} = K_{EQ} \cdot \frac{E}{E+L} + K_L \cdot \frac{L}{E+L}$$

where $K_{EQ}$ is the cost of equity, $K_L$ the cost of liabilities, and the weights are the two percentages of [Slide 36]. With the structure of the slide-36 example (70% equity, 30% liabilities), the formula reads

$$K_{WACC} = 0.70\,K_{EQ} + 0.30\,K_L$$

so the cost of capital is always between the cheaper and the dearer of the two sources, closer to the one with the larger weight. This closes the argument of [Slide 5]: the investments (assets) create value only if their profitability is greater than this cost of the funds that finance them.
