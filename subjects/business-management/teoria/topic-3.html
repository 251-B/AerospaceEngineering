Financial Management (I): Financial Statements and Financial Structure

Economic and financial structure, the income statement, the balance sheet, the sources of financing, the debt-to-equity ratio and the weighted average cost of capital.

📖 Source: Universidad Carlos III de Madrid · Topic 3_Financial Management (I).pdf (Prof. Carmen Paz-Aparicio), Slides 1-37
Official material Topic 3 Financial Management (I).pdf T3_Financial Statements_EXERCISES.pdf

Central idea: a firm has two sides. Its assets (the economic structure, what it owns) generate profitability; its equity and liabilities (the financial structure, what it owes) carry a cost. Value is created when profitability exceeds cost. Two statements describe the firm: the income statement, which measures performance over a period (revenues − expenses = net income), and the balance sheet, which shows the position at one moment (assets = liabilities + equity). The financial structure combines self-financing, external equity and long- and short-term debt; it is measured with the debt-to-equity ratio and priced with the weighted average cost of capital.

The topic has three parts [Slide 2]: an introduction to the economic and the financial structure; the financial statements, namely the income statement and the balance sheet; and the financial structure of the company.

Part I — Economic and financial structure

§ 1 The finance area and its two decisions [Slide 3]

The finance area of a company is in charge of the collection and management of the funds (capital) the company uses. The finance manager faces two types of decision: what to invest in and when (investing decisions), and how to obtain the money to invest (financing decisions).

Figure 1. The finance manager's two questions and the decisions they correspond to.

§ 2 Economic structure and financial structure [Slides 4-5]

The economic structure is defined by the investments the company carries out. It represents the use of the financial resources in the form of goods (assets) and rights: the assets, or what is owned.

The financial structure is defined by the sources of financing. It includes all the financial resources used to carry out the investments (debt and liabilities): the liabilities, or what is owed [Slide 4].

The assets produce profitability, while equity and liabilities have a cost. The objective of the company is to maximize value, which requires profitability > cost [Slide 5].

Figure 2. The two sides of the firm: the assets (economic structure) generate profitability, equity and liabilities (financial structure) carry a cost; value is created when profitability exceeds cost.

§ 3 What the company's decisions focus on [Slide 6]

Financing decisions concern the choice between equity and debt. The financial resources available can be internal equity (self-financing, i.e. retained earnings), external equity or debt. Financing decisions involve decisions on the capital structure and on dividends.

Investing decisions consist in allocating capital to the investment projects from which future profits are expected. Companies invest in the projects that create value for them, those in which profits > costs.

Part II — Financial statements

§ 4 The income statement [Slides 7-8]

The income statement is the first statement prepared. It reports the performance of the business over a specific period, and in its simplest form [Slide 7]:

$$\text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses} = \text{Net income}$$

Revenues are earned from the sale of goods or services and are recorded when the sale is made, whether or not the payment has been received; examples are sales, service revenue and interest revenue. Expenses are incurred when the business receives goods and services, whether or not it has paid for them; examples are salaries, utility and interest expense [Slide 8].

§ 5 The income statement: extended format [Slides 9-11]

The lines are defined as follows:

  • The cost of goods sold is the expense the business incurred to buy or make a product for resale. If a bookshop buys a book for 25 USD and sells it for 32 USD, the cost of goods sold is 25 USD [Slide 10].
  • Operating expenses are the usual expenses of running the business, such as salaries, utilities and depreciation [Slide 10].
  • Non-operating items are the revenues, expenses, gains and losses that do not relate to the company's primary operations, such as interest expense and gains and losses on the sale of equipment and investments [Slide 11].
  • Income taxes are the income before taxes multiplied by the tax rate [Slide 11]. With an income before taxes of 50,000 USD and a rate of 30%:
$$\text{Income taxes} = 50{,}000 \times 0.30 = 15{,}000 \text{ USD}$$

§ 6 The balance sheet [Slides 12-13]

The balance sheet reports the financial position of an accounting entity at a particular point in time [Slide 12]:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$$
  • Assets are the economic resources owned by the company: cash, accounts receivable, supplies, buildings, equipment.
  • Liabilities are the company's debts or obligations: accounts payable, unearned revenues, bonds payable.
  • Equity is the residual balance, $\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} = \text{Equity}$. In a corporation it is called stockholders' equity, because it represents the financing provided by the stockholders together with the earnings not paid out as dividends (retained earnings) [Slide 13].

§ 7 Classes of assets, liabilities and equity [Slides 14-17]

Assets are split into current and non-current [Slides 14-15]:

$$\text{Current assets} + \text{Non-current assets} = \text{Total assets}$$
  • Current assets will be used or turned into cash within one year: cash, accounts receivable, inventory, short-term investments, supplies and prepaids.
  • Non-current assets are all the others: long-term investments, land, buildings, equipment and patents.

Liabilities are split into current and long-term [Slide 16]:

$$\text{Current liabilities} + \text{Long-term liabilities} = \text{Total liabilities}$$
  • Current liabilities will be paid in cash (or other services) or satisfied within the coming year.
  • Long-term liabilities will not be paid or satisfied within the year.

Stockholders' equity is split into contributed capital and retained earnings [Slide 17]:

$$\text{Contributed capital} + \text{Retained earnings} = \text{Total stockholders' equity}$$

§ 8 The complete balance sheet [Slide 18]

$$\text{Current assets} + \text{Non-current assets} = \text{Current liabilities} + \text{Long-term liabilities} + \text{Stockholders' equity}$$
Figure 3. Layout of the balance sheet: total assets (non-current + current) equal total liabilities and stockholders' equity. Block sizes are only illustrative.

Part III — The financial structure of the company

§ 10 Financing decisions and the capital structure [Slide 20]

Financing decisions analyse the different financial alternatives. Business financing is the collection of the financial resources needed to carry out the company's activity, and the combination of all the possible financial sources is the capital structure or financial structure.

§ 11 The map of the financial structure [Slides 21-22]

The sources of financing can be classified by their nature, equity or liabilities [Slide 21], or by their origin, the company's own resources or external resources [Slide 22]. Both classifications draw the line in the same place:

Figure 4. The financial structure: equity (the company's own resources, internal or external) and liabilities (external resources, long term or short term), with the instruments of each.

§ 12 Self-financing [Slides 23-24]

The retained earnings, the resources the company generates in its normal activity, constitute its self-financing. Profits can either be retained in the company (self-financing) or distributed (dividends). Two ratios describe the split:

$$\text{Pay-out} = \frac{\text{Dividends}}{\text{Profits}}, \qquad \text{Self-financing (\%)} = \frac{\text{Retained earnings}}{\text{Profits}}$$

With profits of 100,000 €, dividends of 75,000 € and retained earnings of 25,000 €, the pay-out is $75{,}000 / 100{,}000 = 75\%$ and the self-financing ratio is $25{,}000 / 100{,}000 = 25\%$ [Slide 23].

§ 13 External equity [Slides 25-26]

When a business is created, its owners put in funds to finance the assets. Equity capital is the money raised from the owners at the start of the business (initial capital) and through later rights issues (issues of new shares), used to finance the company's investments. It is not repaid to the investor at any maturity date, and a share can be turned into cash only by selling or transferring it [Slide 25].

A share is one of a finite number of equal portions of the capital of a company. It entitles its owner to a proportion of the distributed, non-reinvested profits (dividends) and to a portion of the value of the company in case of liquidation. Shares are equity securities and have several values: nominal, accounting (book), intrinsic and market value. New shares are issued through a rights issue (capital increase), and paying dividends is not compulsory [Slide 26].

§ 14 Bonds [Slide 29]

A bond is a debt security: the issuer owes the holders a debt and must repay the principal and pay interest (the coupon) at a later date, the maturity. It is a loan in the form of a security, in which the issuer is the borrower, the bondholder is the lender and the coupon is the interest. Bonds allow the issuer to finance long-term investments with external funds.

§ 15 Long-term loans and bank credits [Slide 31]

With a long-term loan, the company receives an amount of money that must be paid back at a certain time, and pays for this service through the debt interest. With a bank credit, the financial institution makes a maximum amount available to the customer, who can borrow any amount up to that limit and pays interest only on the amount borrowed; interest rates are higher.

§ 16 Leasing [Slide 33]

Leasing lets a firm obtain the use of a fixed asset in exchange for a series of contractual, periodic payments. The lessee, who receives the asset or the services under the lease contract, has the right to buy the asset at the end of the agreed period. There are two types: finance leasing and operating leasing.

§ 17 Short-term external resources [Slide 35]

  • Accounts payable (trade credit): financing from suppliers that arises when goods are paid for some time after they are received; suppliers allow the company to defer the payment.
  • Bank financing: short-term loans and credits, with the same features as the long-term ones, and trade discounts, in which the financial institution advances to the customer the amount of a note (bill of exchange, promissory note, etc.) before it falls due, after deducting some expenses (interest, commission, etc.).
  • Factoring: the company sells a credit it holds against a customer to a factoring company before it expires; the factoring company collects it and assumes the risk of insolvency.

§ 18 Measuring the financial structure: leverage [Slide 36]

The financial structure is the combination of the different financial resources. With $E$ for equity and $L$ for liabilities:

$$\%\,\text{Equity} = \frac{E}{E+L}, \qquad \%\,\text{Liabilities} = \frac{L}{E+L}, \qquad \text{Debt-to-equity ratio} = \frac{L}{E}$$

The debt-to-equity ratio evaluates the company's financial leverage: the degree to which it finances its operations through debt rather than its own funds. It reflects the ability of shareholders' equity to cover all outstanding debts in a business downturn, and higher leverage ratios tend to indicate higher risk for shareholders.

With total liabilities and equity of 1,000 M€, of which 700 M€ are equity and 300 M€ liabilities:

$$\%\,\text{Equity} = \frac{700}{1{,}000} = 70\%, \qquad \%\,\text{Liabilities} = \frac{300}{1{,}000} = 30\%, \qquad \frac{L}{E} = \frac{300}{700} = 0.43$$

§ 19 The cost of capital [Slide 37]

Every financial resource has a cost: equity has an implicit (opportunity) cost, and liabilities an explicit cost. The capital cost $K$ is the weighted average of the costs of all the financial resources the company obtains:

$$K_{WACC} = K_{EQ} \cdot \frac{E}{E+L} + K_L \cdot \frac{L}{E+L}$$

where $K_{EQ}$ is the cost of equity and $K_L$ the cost of liabilities, weighted by the shares of equity and liabilities in the financial structure. With the structure of the leverage example (70% equity, 30% liabilities), $K_{WACC} = 0.70\,K_{EQ} + 0.30\,K_L$.
