Exercise 1 — Demand curve and midpoint elasticities

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf

The following table presents data concerning the demand for Product X:

PointPriceQuantity
A250
B2020
C1540
D1060
E580
F0100

A) Draw the demand curve. B) What is elasticity? C) What is the sign of the coefficient of elasticity? D) Calculate the price elasticity at the mid-point of each interval.

Data and model

Six points of the demand of product X, equally spaced: from one point to the next the price falls by 5 € and the quantity rises by 20 units.

The price elasticity is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2). Between two points the percentages are taken with respect to the midpoint of the interval, as the statement asks:

$$E_d = \frac{\Delta Q / \bar Q}{\Delta P / \bar P}, \qquad \bar Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}, \quad \bar P = \frac{P_1 + P_2}{2}$$

Solution

Step 1. Part (A): the demand curve

Each step of $-5$ € in price adds 20 units, so the slope is constant and the points lie on a straight line. Through A (0, 25): $P = 25 - \frac{5}{20}\,Q = 25 - Q/4$, or $Q = 100 - 4P$.

Figure 1. The six points of the table on the straight demand line P = 25 − Q/4; the demand is elastic on the upper half, unit elastic at its midpoint and inelastic on the lower half.

Step 2. Part (B): what elasticity is

Elasticity is the sensitivity of the quantity demanded to a change in the price of the good, with both changes expressed in relative terms (%) (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2; Slide 17).

Step 3. Part (C): the sign

Negative. By the law of demand, when the price falls the quantity demanded rises, and vice versa: $\Delta Q$ and $\Delta P$ always have opposite signs. The absolute value is used to classify the demand.

Step 4. Part (D): midpoint elasticity of each interval

In every interval $\Delta Q = +20$ and $\Delta P = -5$. For A-B, $\bar Q = (0 + 20)/2 = 10$ and $\bar P = (25 + 20)/2 = 22.5$:

$$E_{AB} = \frac{20/10}{-5/22.5} = \frac{2}{-0.2222} = -9$$

The same computation for the other intervals:

Interval$\bar Q$$\bar P$$\Delta Q/\bar Q$$\Delta P/\bar P$$E_d$Demand
A-B1022.52.0000−0.2222−9.00elastic
B-C3017.50.6667−0.2857−2.33elastic
C-D5012.50.4000−0.4000−1.00unit elastic
D-E707.50.2857−0.6667−0.43inelastic
E-F902.50.2222−2.0000−0.11inelastic

Along a straight demand line the slope is constant, but the elasticity is not: it falls in absolute value as we move down the line, because $\bar P/\bar Q$ falls. The demand is elastic on the upper half, unit elastic at the middle (Q = 50, P = 12.5) and inelastic on the lower half.

Checks and official key

  • Point form: with $dQ/dP = -4$, the point elasticity $E_d = -4\,P/Q$ at the midpoint of C-D is $-4 \times 12.5/50 = -1$, matching the table.
  • Units: both ratios are dimensionless.
  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, pp. 1-2): the same demand curve; elasticity as the sensitivity of quantity to price in relative terms; negative sign by the law of demand; midpoint elasticities −9, −2.33, −1, −0.43 and −0.11. Same results.
Exercise 2

Exercise 2 — Quantity sold after a price rise

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf

The price elasticity of demand of a certain good is 2 and we are currently selling 100 units at a price of 20 €. How many units will be sold at a price of 21 €? Show your answer with the respective calculations.

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$\lvert E_d \rvert$price elasticity (absolute value)2
$Q_1$, $P_1$current quantity and price100 units, 20 €
$P_2$new price21 €
$x$units sold at 21 €unknown

Price elasticity $E_d = (\%\Delta Q)/(\%\Delta P)$, negative by the law of demand (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2). Two ways of taking the percentages give two estimates.

Solution

Step 1. Estimate with the initial values as base (Slide 17)

$$\%\Delta P = \frac{21 - 20}{20} = 5\% \qquad \%\Delta Q = E_d \cdot \%\Delta P = -2 \times 5\% = -10\%$$
$$x = 100 \times (1 - 0.10) = 90 \ \text{units}$$

Step 2. Exact value with the midpoint of the change

Taking the elasticity at the midpoint of the change from $P = 20$ to $P = 21$ and from $Q = 100$ to $Q = x$:

$$\frac{(x - 100) \big/ \frac{x + 100}{2}}{(21 - 20) \big/ \frac{21 + 20}{2}} = -2$$

The price part is $1/20.5$, so

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{2\,(x - 100)}{x + 100} \cdot 20.5 &= -2 \\ \frac{41\,(x - 100)}{x + 100} &= -2 \\ 41x - 4{,}100 &= -2x - 200 \\ 43x &= 3{,}900 \\ x &= \frac{3{,}900}{43} = 90.70 \ \text{units} \end{aligned}$$

At 21 € about 90.7 units are sold (90 units in the simpler estimate).

Checks and official key

  • Check of the midpoint value: $\Delta Q/\bar Q = (90.70 - 100)/95.35 = -0.0975$ and $\Delta P/\bar P = 1/20.5 = 0.0488$; the ratio is $-2.00$. ✓
  • Meaning: demand is elastic ($\lvert E_d \rvert = 2 > 1$), so the 5% price rise lowers sales by about 10% and revenue falls from $20 \times 100 = 2{,}000$ € to $21 \times 90.70 = 1{,}904.7$ €.
  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, p. 2): about 90 units in rough numbers, and $x = 3{,}900/43 = 90.697$ units with the midpoint formula. Same result; $3{,}900/43 = 90.6977$, which the key truncates to 90.697 and is rounded here to 90.70.
Exercise 3

Exercise 3 — Mini laptop: elasticity and revenue

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf

A company selling mini laptops is marketing a particular laptop in a market where demand corresponds to the expression $Q_d = 25000 - 40p$. This company is considering increasing the selling price of the mini laptop and would like to know what impact the increase in price will have on the total income.

a. Calculate the value of this elasticity considering the current price of its mini laptop is 500 m.u.

b. In view of the above result, state with appropriate reasons whether the firm should increase its price and the effect that total income will have due to this price increase.

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$Q_d(p)$demand$25{,}000 - 40\,p$
$p$current price500 m.u.

Point price elasticity $E_d = \dfrac{dq}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{q}$ (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2), and the relation between elasticity and income of p. 3.

Solution

Step 1. Part (a): quantity at the current price

$$q = 25{,}000 - 40 \times 500 = 25{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 5{,}000 \ \text{units}$$

Step 2. Part (a): point elasticity

The derivative of the demand is $dq/dp = -40$, so

$$E_d = \frac{dq}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{q} = -40 \times \frac{500}{5{,}000} = -4$$

Step 3. Part (b): should the firm raise the price?

$\lvert E_d \rvert = 4 > 1$: the demand is elastic. A price rise of 1% lowers the quantity sold by about 4%, so total income ($p \cdot q$) falls (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 3). The firm should not raise the price; if anything, a price cut would raise income.

Checks and official key

  • Check with the revenue function: $I(p) = p\,(25{,}000 - 40\,p)$, so $dI/dp = 25{,}000 - 80\,p$, which at $p = 500$ is $25{,}000 - 40{,}000 = -15{,}000 < 0$: income falls when the price rises. ✓
  • Check with a small change: at $p = 501$, $q = 4{,}960$ and $I = 2{,}484{,}960$ m.u., below $I(500) = 2{,}500{,}000$ m.u. ✓
  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, p. 3): $q = 5{,}000$ units, $\eta = -40 \times 500/5{,}000 = -4$, demand elastic, and a price rise would lower total revenue. Same results.
Exercise 4 · Multiple choice

Exercise 4 — Change in quantity after a price cut

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf, p. 2

If a producer lowers the prices by 20% in a given good, knowing that the elasticity of demand with respect to price is 1.5, how much do you estimate the quantity demanded will vary (as a percentage)?

a) 15% b) 30% c) 45% d) 5%

Data and model

Price change $\%\Delta P = -20\%$; price elasticity $\lvert E_d \rvert = 1.5$. From the definition $E_d = \%\Delta Q / \%\Delta P$ (Slide 17; topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 2), $\%\Delta Q = E_d \cdot \%\Delta P$.

Solution

Step 1. Percentage change in quantity

With the sign of the law of demand, $E_d = -1.5$:

$$\%\Delta Q = E_d \cdot \%\Delta P = (-1.5) \times (-20\%) = +30\%$$

The quantity demanded rises by 30%: answer b).

Step 2. Why the other options are wrong

  • a) 15% would need $\lvert E_d \rvert = 0.75$.
  • c) 45% would need $\lvert E_d \rvert = 2.25$, or a 30% price cut.
  • d) 5% would need $\lvert E_d \rvert = 0.25$, an inelastic demand.

Checks and official key

  • Meaning: demand is elastic ($1.5 > 1$), so the quantity changes more, in percentage, than the price.
  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, p. 3): answer b), $20\% \times 1.5 = 30\%$. Same answer.
Exercise 5 · Multiple choice

Exercise 5 — Elastic demand and the seller's income

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf, p. 2

When the price elasticity of demand for a normal good is relatively elastic:

a) A reduction in the price of the good involves obtaining a higher income by the offerer. b) A reduction in the price of the good involves obtaining a lower income by the offerer. c) A reduction of prices does not alter the amount of income by the offerer. d) None of the other answers is correct.

Data and model

"Relatively elastic" means $\lvert E_d \rvert > 1$ (Slide 17). The seller's income is $I = P \cdot Q$; its reaction to a price change is given by the table of topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 3.

Solution

Step 1. Effect of a price cut on income

With elastic demand, a 1% fall in price raises the quantity demanded by more than 1%. In $I = P \cdot Q$ the rise of $Q$ outweighs the fall of $P$, so income rises (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 3). Answer a).

A short numerical illustration with $\lvert E_d \rvert = 2$: a 5% price cut raises the quantity by about 10%, and income changes by the factor $0.95 \times 1.10 = 1.045$, a 4.5% rise.

Step 2. Why the other options are wrong

  • b) describes inelastic demand ($\lvert E_d \rvert < 1$), where a price cut lowers income.
  • c) describes unit elastic demand ($\lvert E_d \rvert = 1$), where income does not change.
  • d) is wrong because a) is correct.

Checks and official key

  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, p. 3): answer a). Same answer.
Exercise 6 · Multiple choice

Exercise 6 — Substitute goods from the cross effects

Original statement · Elasticity exercises STATEMENTS.pdf, p. 2

If when the price of a good "X" is reduced, an increase is induced in the quantity demanded of good "X" itself, as well as a decrease in the quantity demanded of good "Y", we say that "X" and "Y" are:

a) Complementary b) Substitutes c) Normal goods d) None of the previous

Data and model

The price of X falls, the demand for X rises and the demand for Y falls. The cross elasticity $E_{YX} = \%\Delta Q_Y / \%\Delta P_X$ is positive for substitutes and negative for complements (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 5).

Solution

Step 1. Sign of the cross elasticity

The price of X falls ($\%\Delta P_X < 0$) and the quantity of Y falls ($\%\Delta Q_Y < 0$):

$$E_{YX} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_Y}{\%\Delta P_X} = \frac{(-)}{(-)} > 0$$

A positive cross elasticity means substitutes: answer b). In words, X became cheaper and buyers moved from Y to X (topic 5.2.2.pdf, p. 5).

Step 2. Why the other options are wrong

  • a) Complementary goods would see the demand for both rise when the price of X falls.
  • c) "Normal good" describes the reaction of a good to the buyers' income, not to the price of another good, and the statement gives no income change.
  • d) is wrong because b) is correct.

Checks and official key

  • Check against the official key (PED Exercises SOL.pdf, p. 4): answer b). Same answer.
