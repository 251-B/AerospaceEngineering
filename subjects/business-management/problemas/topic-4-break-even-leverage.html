Exercise 1

Exercise 1 — A price equal to the variable cost

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf

A company's annual fixed costs are €2,000. The variable cost per unit equals its selling price, which is €2. What is the break-even point?

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$FC$annual fixed costs2,000 €
$P$selling price per unit2 €/unit
$VC$variable cost per unit2 €/unit

The break-even point is the volume at which the profit is zero [Slide 36], and with the unit margin $m = P - VC$ it is $Q_0 = FC/m$ [Slide 37].

Solution

Step 1. Unit margin

$$m = P - VC = 2 - 2 = 0 \ \text{€/unit}$$

Step 2. Break-even point

$$Q_0 = \frac{FC}{m} = \frac{2{,}000}{0} \to \infty$$

The division has no finite result: no volume makes the profit zero. Writing the profit for any volume shows why,

$$\text{Profit} = Q\,m - FC = Q \times 0 - 2{,}000 = -2{,}000 \ \text{€}$$

Every unit sold brings in exactly what it costs, so nothing is left to cover the fixed costs: the company loses 2,000 € a year whatever it sells.

Checks and official key

  • Meaning: a break-even point only exists when $m > 0$. With $m = 0$ the profit line is horizontal at $-FC$ and never reaches zero.
  • Units: €/(€/unit) = units.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 3): the key also obtains $Q^* = 2{,}000/(2 - 2) = \infty$ and concludes that the solution makes no economic sense, because the company will never cover its fixed costs. Same result.
Exercise 2

Exercise 2 — Break-even point from a known loss, and leverage at full capacity

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf

A company has a unit margin of €5.

a) Assuming that the manufacturing of 500 physical units produces a loss of €500, calculate the break-even point.

b) Represent graphically the situation of this company assuming that the maximum capacity of the plant is 900 physical units. What is the maximum profit that can be obtained?

c) Calculate the degree of operating leverage of this company for a production and sales volume of 900 physical units and interpret this result.

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$m$unit margin5 €/unit
$B(500)$profit at 500 units−500 €
$Q_{max}$maximum capacity900 units

The profit is $B = Q\,m - FC$ [Slide 39], the break-even point $Q^* = FC/m$ [Slide 37] and the operating leverage $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slide 40]. Production equals sales, so $Q$ is both.

Solution

Step 1. Part (a): fixed costs and break-even point

The fixed costs are the only unknown in the profit at 500 units:

$$\begin{aligned} B(500) &= 500\,m - FC \\ -500 &= 500 \times 5 - FC = 2{,}500 - FC \\ FC &= 2{,}500 + 500 = 3{,}000 \ \text{€} \end{aligned}$$

Then

$$Q^* = \frac{FC}{m} = \frac{3{,}000}{5} = 600 \ \text{units}$$

Step 2. Part (b): graph and maximum profit

The profit line is $B(Q) = 5\,Q - 3{,}000$, valid between 0 and the capacity of 900 units. It starts at $-3{,}000$ € (only the fixed costs) and crosses zero at 600 units. The line grows with $Q$, so the maximum profit is at the capacity limit:

$$B_{max} = B(900) = 900 \times 5 - 3{,}000 = 4{,}500 - 3{,}000 = 1{,}500 \ \text{€}$$
Figure 1. Profit B = 5q − 3,000 between 0 and the maximum capacity of 900 units: break-even at 600 units and maximum profit 1,500 € at 900 units.

Step 3. Part (c): operating leverage at 900 units

$$OL_{900} = \frac{Q\,m}{Q\,m - FC} = \frac{900 \times 5}{900 \times 5 - 3{,}000} = \frac{4{,}500}{1{,}500} = 3$$

Interpretation: a 1% change in sales changes the profit by 3% [Slide 40]. The company is at full capacity and cannot sell more, so only a fall in sales is possible: each 1% fall below 900 units cuts the profit by 3%.

Checks and official key

  • Check with the BEP form [Slide 42]: $OL = Q/(Q - Q^*) = 900/(900 - 600) = 3$, the same value.
  • Check of (a): $B(600) = 600 \times 5 - 3{,}000 = 0$.
  • Units: $FC$ in €, $Q^*$ in units, OL dimensionless.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, pp. 3-4): $FC = 3{,}000$ €, $q^* = 600$ units, maximum profit 1,500 € and $AO = 3$, with the same reading for a decrease in sales. Same results.
Exercise 3

Exercise 3 — Choosing between two production processes

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf

A company manager who wants to market a new product has to choose between two alternative production processes A and B. In both cases the selling price of the product is €20.

Process AProcess B
Fixed costs40,00020,000
Unit variable costs1315

a) Assuming that the expected level of production and sales is 10,000 physical units, decide the most convenient process according to the break-even analysis and the graphic representation.

b) If process B resulted in a higher quality product with a selling price of €25, how would this affect the choice of the most convenient process?

c) If the rise in price of the product obtained with process B (€25) caused a drop in sales from 10,000 to 6,000 physical units, which production process would the company choose?

Data and model

MagnitudeProcess AProcess B
Price $P$ (€/unit)2020
Fixed costs $FC$ (€)40,00020,000
Variable cost $VC$ (€/unit)1315
Unit margin $m = P - VC$ (€/unit)75

Profit $B = Q\,m - FC$, break-even point $Q^* = FC/m$ [Slide 37] and operating leverage $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slide 40], the index of operating risk [Slide 41].

Solution

Step 1. Part (a): profit, break-even point and leverage at 10,000 units

$$\begin{aligned} B_A &= (20 - 13) \times 10{,}000 - 40{,}000 = 70{,}000 - 40{,}000 = 30{,}000 \ \text{€} \\ B_B &= (20 - 15) \times 10{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 50{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 30{,}000 \ \text{€} \end{aligned}$$

Both processes give the same profit. The break-even points are

$$Q^*_A = \frac{40{,}000}{7} = 5{,}714.3 \ \text{units} \qquad Q^*_B = \frac{20{,}000}{5} = 4{,}000 \ \text{units}$$

and the operating leverages at 10,000 units

$$OL_A = \frac{70{,}000}{30{,}000} = 2.33 \qquad OL_B = \frac{50{,}000}{30{,}000} = 1.67$$

With the same profit, process B is chosen: its break-even point is lower, so 10,000 units are farther from it, and its leverage (operating risk) is lower. A fall in sales would hurt A's profit more.

The two profit lines cross where $7\,Q - 40{,}000 = 5\,Q - 20{,}000$, that is $2\,Q = 20{,}000$ and $Q = 10{,}000$ units: below that volume B gives more profit, above it A does. The expected volume is exactly the crossing point.

Figure 2. Profit of process A (7q − 40,000) and process B (5q − 20,000) at a price of 20 €: break-even points 5,714 and 4,000 units; both lines give 30,000 € at the expected 10,000 units.

Step 2. Part (b): process B sold at €25

The new unit margin of B is $m_B = 25 - 15 = 10$ €/unit, twice the old one. At 10,000 units:

$$B_B = 10 \times 10{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 80{,}000 \ \text{€} \qquad Q^*_B = \frac{20{,}000}{10} = 2{,}000 \ \text{units} \qquad OL_B = \frac{100{,}000}{80{,}000} = 1.25$$

Process A is unchanged (30,000 €, $OL_A = 2.33$). B now gives more profit with less operating risk: B is chosen with even more reason.

Step 3. Part (c): B at €25 but only 6,000 units sold

$$B_B = (25 - 15) \times 6{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 60{,}000 - 20{,}000 = 40{,}000 \ \text{€}$$

Process A, which keeps the price of 20 € and the 10,000 units, still gives 30,000 €. Since $40{,}000 > 30{,}000$, the company still chooses B. Its leverage is $OL_B = 60{,}000/40{,}000 = 1.5$, also below A's 2.33.

Checks and official key

  • Check of the crossing: at 10,000 units both lines give 30,000 €, as found in (a).
  • Units: profits in €, break-even points in units, OL dimensionless.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, pp. 4-5): profits 30,000 € for both processes, $AO_A = 2.33$, $AO_B = 1.66$ (1.67 rounded up here), B chosen in (a), (b) and (c), with 40,000 € > 30,000 € in (c). Same results.
Discrepancy with the official solution

Two sentences of the key do not match its own numbers. In (a) it says that "A has a lower break-even point than B"; in fact $Q^*_A = 40{,}000/7 = 5{,}714$ units is higher than $Q^*_B = 4{,}000$ units, which is why B carries less risk, as the key itself concludes. In (b) it says the price rise increases "the unit margin by 5%"; the margin goes up by 5 € (from 5 to 10 €/unit, a 100% increase). The choices of the key are not affected.

Exercise 4

Exercise 4 — Leverage after a change in volume or in price

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf

Given the following information: selling price €10; variable cost per unit €3; fixed costs €1,000.

a) How many units must be sold to start making a profit?

b) What would the profit be if the company sold 200 units?

c) What is the degree of operating leverage for 200 units sold?

d) Will the degree of operating leverage vary if there is a 50 unit increase in sales?

e) Will the degree of operating leverage vary if there is a 10% rise in price (for 200 units)?

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$P$selling price10 €/unit
$VC$variable cost per unit3 €/unit
$FC$fixed costs1,000 €
$m$unit margin, $P - VC$7 €/unit

Break-even point $Q^* = FC/m$ [Slide 37], profit $B = Q\,m - FC$ and operating leverage $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slides 39-40].

Solution

Step 1. Part (a): break-even point

$$Q^* = \frac{FC}{P - VC} = \frac{1{,}000}{10 - 3} = \frac{1{,}000}{7} = 142.86 \ \text{units}$$

Units are whole, so at 142 units the profit is still negative ($142 \times 7 - 1{,}000 = -6$ €) and at 143 it becomes positive ($143 \times 7 - 1{,}000 = 1$ €): the company starts making a profit from 143 units.

Step 2. Part (b): profit at 200 units

$$B = (10 - 3) \times 200 - 1{,}000 = 1{,}400 - 1{,}000 = 400 \ \text{€}$$

Step 3. Part (c): leverage at 200 units

$$OL_{200} = \frac{200 \times 7}{200 \times 7 - 1{,}000} = \frac{1{,}400}{400} = 3.5$$

A 1% change in sales changes the profit by 3.5%.

Step 4. Part (d): 50 more units (250 units)

$$OL_{250} = \frac{250 \times 7}{250 \times 7 - 1{,}000} = \frac{1{,}750}{750} = 2.33$$

Yes, it varies: it falls from 3.5 to 2.33, because the volume has moved away from the break-even point [Slide 42].

Step 5. Part (e): 10% higher price (200 units)

The new price is $10 \times 1.10 = 11$ €/unit, so $m = 11 - 3 = 8$ €/unit and

$$OL = \frac{200 \times 8}{200 \times 8 - 1{,}000} = \frac{1{,}600}{600} = 2.67$$

Yes, it varies: it falls from 3.5 to 2.67. The higher margin lowers the break-even point to $1{,}000/8 = 125$ units, so 200 units are now farther from it.

Checks and official key

  • Check with the BEP form [Slide 42]: (c) $200/(200 - 142.86) = 3.5$; (d) $250/(250 - 142.86) = 2.33$; (e) $200/(200 - 125) = 2.67$.
  • Units: $Q^*$ in units, profit in €, OL dimensionless.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 5): $q^* \approx 143$ units, profit 400 €, $AO = 3.5$, $2.33$ and $2.66$ (2.67 rounded up here), with the same explanation (the volume moves away from the break-even point; the price changes the break-even point). Same results.
Exercise 5

Exercise 5 — Cost structure from two observations

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf

A visit to a company has provided the following information:

  • If the company produced and sold 300 physical units, it would make a profit of €56.
  • If the company produced and sold 160 physical units, it would make zero profit.
  • The company forecasts sales of 500 physical units.

Calculate the company's fixed costs, the forecast profit, its safety margin and the degree of operating leverage.

Data and model

ObservationVolume $Q$ (units)Profit $B$ (€)
130056
2 (break-even point)1600
Forecast500?

The profit is linear in the volume, $B = Q\,m - FC$ [Slide 39]; the margin of safety is $Q - Q^*$ in units, $(Q - Q^*)\,m$ in monetary units and $(Q - Q^*)/Q^*$ in percentage [Slide 43]; the leverage is $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slide 40].

Solution

Step 1. Unit margin

Subtracting the two observations removes the fixed costs:

$$\begin{aligned} B(300) - B(160) &= (300\,m - FC) - (160\,m - FC) = (300 - 160)\,m \\ 56 - 0 &= 140\,m \\ m &= \frac{56}{140} = 0.4 \ \text{€/unit} \end{aligned}$$

Step 2. Fixed costs

At the break-even point the profit is zero:

$$0 = 160 \times 0.4 - FC \quad\Rightarrow\quad FC = 64 \ \text{€}$$

Step 3. Forecast profit (500 units)

$$B(500) = 500 \times 0.4 - 64 = 200 - 64 = 136 \ \text{€}$$

Step 4. Margin of safety

$$MS = Q - Q^* = 500 - 160 = 340 \ \text{units}$$

In monetary units, $MS = 340 \times 0.4 = 136$ €, and as a percentage of the break-even volume, $MS = 340/160 = 2.125$, that is 212.5%.

Step 5. Operating leverage

$$OL_{500} = \frac{500 \times 0.4}{500 \times 0.4 - 64} = \frac{200}{136} = 1.47$$

A 1% change in sales changes the profit by 1.47%.

Checks and official key

  • Check with observation 1: $300 \times 0.4 - 64 = 120 - 64 = 56$ €. ✓
  • Check with the BEP form [Slide 42]: $OL = 500/(500 - 160) = 1.47$. ✓
  • Note: above the break-even point the margin of safety in m.u. equals the profit (136 €), since $(Q - Q^*)\,m = Q\,m - FC$.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 6): $m = 0.4$ €/unit, $FC = 64$ €, forecast profit 136 €, $MS = 340$ units, $AO = 1.47$. Same results; the key gives the margin of safety in units only.

Exercise 6 — Leverage from the break-even point alone

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf, p. 2

On the financial pages of a Spanish newspaper it was recently announced that a manufacturing company specialized in certain automotive components had sold 100,000 units of its product last year, well above its break-even point, set at 20,000 physical units approximately. What is the degree of operating leverage for this company?

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$Q$units sold100,000 units
$Q^*$break-even point20,000 units

Price, costs and margin are not given. The form of the leverage that uses only the two volumes is the one of Slide 42.

Solution

Step 1. The leverage in terms of the break-even point

From $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slide 40] and $Q^* = FC/m$, so $FC = Q^*\,m$ [Slide 37]:

$$OL = \frac{Q\,m}{Q\,m - Q^*\,m} = \frac{m\,Q}{m\,(Q - Q^*)} = \frac{Q}{Q - Q^*}$$

The unit margin cancels.

Step 2. Value

$$OL = \frac{100{,}000}{100{,}000 - 20{,}000} = \frac{100{,}000}{80{,}000} = 1.25$$

A 1% change in sales changes the profit by 1.25%. Being far above the break-even point, the company has a leverage close to 1, a low operating risk.

Checks and official key

  • Limit: as $Q$ grows far beyond $Q^*$, $OL \to 1$; as $Q$ approaches $Q^*$ from above, $OL \to \infty$ [Slide 42]. 1.25 fits a volume five times the break-even point.
  • Units: units/units, dimensionless.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 6): $AO = 100{,}000/(100{,}000 - 20{,}000) = 1.25$, obtained by the same substitution. Same result.
Exercise 7

Exercise 7 — Sumun S.A.: break-even point and leverage

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf, p. 2

We have the following data corresponding to a certain product manufactured by Sumun S.A.:

  • Unit margin: €10
  • Annual sales volume: 2,000 units
  • Annual fixed costs: €1,000

Calculate: a) the break-even point of this product; b) its degree of operating leverage.

Data and model

SymbolMeaningValue
$m$unit margin10 €/unit
$Q$annual sales2,000 units
$FC$annual fixed costs1,000 €

Break-even point $Q^* = FC/m$ [Slide 37], operating leverage $OL = Q\,m/(Q\,m - FC)$ [Slide 40].

Solution

Step 1. Part (a): break-even point

$$Q^* = \frac{FC}{m} = \frac{1{,}000}{10} = 100 \ \text{units}$$

Step 2. Part (b): operating leverage

$$OL_{2000} = \frac{2{,}000 \times 10}{2{,}000 \times 10 - 1{,}000} = \frac{20{,}000}{19{,}000} = 1.05$$

A 1% change in sales changes the profit by about 1.05%: with sales twenty times the break-even point, the profit moves almost in proportion to the sales.

Checks and official key

  • Check with the BEP form [Slide 42]: $2{,}000/(2{,}000 - 100) = 2{,}000/1{,}900 = 1.05$. ✓
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 7): $q^* = 100$ units and $AO = 20{,}000/19{,}000 = 1.05$. Same results.
Exercise 8

Exercise 8 — ENIR, S.A.: a break-even point beyond capacity

Original statement · BEP OL Statements.pdf, p. 2

Company ENIR, S.A., a black cigarette manufacturer, has not obtained profits during year 2008. It was established two years before and its maximum production capacity amounts to 11 million packs per year. Analyzing its management, we know that in 2008, the company produced 10 million packs, with financial costs relative to its external financing of €300,000, fixed costs of commercialization and administration of €1,000,000 and other fixed costs of €1,700,000. The selling price was €1.3 per pack, which was established by the Government, as this is an industry regulated by the State.

For the following year 2009 and, maintaining the same facilities, the company expects an increase of 15% in its fixed costs, as well as a decrease of 1% in the variable cost per unit. Calculate:

a) The break-even point for year 2009.

b) Considering these plans, is it possible that the company obtains profits? Account for your answer.

Data and model

Magnitude20082009
Capacity (packs)11,000,00011,000,000
Volume $Q$ (packs)10,000,000?
Price $P$ (€/pack)1.31.3
Fixed costs $FC$ (€)3,000,000+15%
Variable cost $VC$ (€/pack)unknown−1%

Reading of the statement (the same as the official key): "has not obtained profits" is read as a profit of exactly zero in 2008, so 10 million packs was the break-even point of 2008; and the financial costs of 300,000 € are counted among the fixed costs:

$$FC_{2008} = 300{,}000 + 1{,}000{,}000 + 1{,}700{,}000 = 3{,}000{,}000 \ \text{€}$$

Break-even condition: profit $= Q\,(P - VC) - FC = 0$ [Slides 36-37].

Solution

Step 1. Variable cost per pack in 2008

$$\begin{aligned} 10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.3 - 3{,}000{,}000 - 10{,}000{,}000 \times VC &= 0 \\ 13{,}000{,}000 - 3{,}000{,}000 &= 10{,}000{,}000 \times VC \\ VC_{2008} &= \frac{10{,}000{,}000}{10{,}000{,}000} = 1.0 \ \text{€/pack} \end{aligned}$$

Step 2. Cost structure of 2009

$$FC_{2009} = 3{,}000{,}000 \times 1.15 = 3{,}450{,}000 \ \text{€} \qquad VC_{2009} = 1.0 \times 0.99 = 0.99 \ \text{€/pack}$$

so the unit margin is $m_{2009} = 1.3 - 0.99 = 0.31$ €/pack.

Step 3. Part (a): break-even point of 2009

$$Q^*_{2009} = \frac{FC}{m} = \frac{3{,}450{,}000}{0.31} = 11{,}129{,}032 \ \text{packs}$$

Step 4. Part (b): can the company make a profit?

No. The break-even point (11,129,032 packs) is above the maximum capacity of the facilities (11,000,000 packs), and the price is fixed by the Government. Even working at full capacity the company loses money:

$$B(11{,}000{,}000) = 11{,}000{,}000 \times 0.31 - 3{,}450{,}000 = 3{,}410{,}000 - 3{,}450{,}000 = -40{,}000 \ \text{€}$$

Checks and official key

  • Check of Step 1: $10{,}000{,}000 \times (1.3 - 1.0) - 3{,}000{,}000 = 0$. ✓
  • Units: €/(€/pack) = packs.
  • Check against the official key (BEP OL Statements Solutions.pdf, p. 7): $FC = 3{,}000{,}000$ €, $VC = 1.0$ €/unit, $FC_{2009} = 3{,}450{,}000$ €, $VC_{2009} = 0.99$ €, break-even point 11,129,032 units, and no profit possible because the maximum volume is 11,000,000. Same results; the key calls the packs "bottles" in its last sentence.
