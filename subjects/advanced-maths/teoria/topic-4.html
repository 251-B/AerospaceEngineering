1. Vector equations and their theory

A system of $n$ coupled first-order equations [Robinson, Eq. (25.1), p. 249]

$$\dot x_i = f_i(x_1, \dots, x_n, t), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n, \tag{25.1}$$

is written compactly with $\mathbf x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T$ and $\mathbf f = (f_1, \dots, f_n)^T$ as

$$\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x, t).$$

For $n = 1$ this is the scalar equation of Topic 1, and the theory is the same with vectors in place of numbers.

Definition 25.1 — solution of a vector IVP [Robinson, p. 250]

A solution of $\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x, t)$, $\mathbf x(t_0) = \mathbf x_0 \in \mathbb R^n$ (25.2) on an open interval $I \ni t_0$ is a differentiable function $\mathbf x : I \to \mathbb R^n$ with $\mathbf x(t_0) = \mathbf x_0$ and $\dot{\mathbf x}(t) = \mathbf f(\mathbf x(t), t)$ for all $t \in I$.

The role of $\partial f/\partial x$ is now played by the matrix of all partial derivatives, the Jacobian [Eq. (25.3), p. 250]:

$$D\mathbf f = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f_1/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_1/\partial x_n \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \partial f_n/\partial x_1 & \cdots & \partial f_n/\partial x_n\end{pmatrix}. \tag{25.3}$$
Theorem 25.2 — existence and uniqueness for systems [Robinson, p. 250]

If $\mathbf f(\mathbf x, t)$ and $D\mathbf f(\mathbf x, t)$ are continuous for $\mathbf x \in U = (a_1, b_1)\times\dots\times(a_n, b_n)$ and $c < t < d$, then for any $\mathbf x_0 \in U$ and $t_0 \in (c, d)$ the IVP (25.2) has a unique solution on some open interval containing $t_0$.

In practice: check that every $f_i$ and every partial derivative $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ is continuous.

Example — the competing species of Topic 1

For $\mathbf f = \big(x(4 - 2x - y),\ y(9 - 3x - 3y)\big)$, $D\mathbf f = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - 4x - y & -x \\ -3y & 9 - 3x - 6y\end{pmatrix}$. All entries are polynomials, hence continuous on all of $\mathbb R^2$: every initial condition has a unique local solution, and in particular two trajectories of Figure 1.1 of Topic 1 can never cross.

1.1 Higher-order equations as systems [Robinson, Sec. 25.1, pp. 251–252]

For $\ddot x = f(\dot x, x, t)$ introduce $x_1 = x$ and $x_2 = \dot x$. Then

$$\dot x_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot x_2 = \ddot x = f(x_2, x_1, t), \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = \mathbf g(\mathbf x, t) = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ f(x_2, x_1, t)\end{pmatrix}. \tag{25.4}$$

The vector initial condition $\mathbf x(t_0) = \mathbf x_0$ is exactly the pair $x(t_0)$, $\dot x(t_0)$, and $D\mathbf g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \partial f/\partial x_1 & \partial f/\partial x_2\end{pmatrix}$. Applying Theorem 25.2 gives Theorem 25.3, which is Theorem 11.1 of Topic 3. An $n$th-order equation becomes an $n$-dimensional system in the same way, with $x_k = x^{(k-1)}$.

Example — the mass–spring–damper as a system

$\ddot x + \lambda\dot x + \omega^2x = 0$ with $x_1 = x$ (position) and $x_2 = \dot x$ (velocity) becomes

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -\lambda\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2\end{pmatrix}.$$

The plane of $(x, \dot x)$ is the phase plane; we return to this example in Section 9.

2. Linear systems and the elimination method

A first-order linear system of two equations with constant coefficients is [Robinson, Eq. (26.1), p. 253]

$$\dot x = ax + by + f(t), \qquad \dot y = cx + dy + g(t). \tag{26.1}$$

It is homogeneous if $f = g = 0$ and inhomogeneous otherwise. If $b = c = 0$ the equations are uncoupled and each is solved by an integrating factor.

2.1 Reduction to one second-order equation

Assume $b \neq 0$ (otherwise exchange the roles of $x$ and $y$).

  1. Solve the first equation for $y$: $\ y = \dfrac{\dot x - ax - f(t)}{b}$ (26.2).
  2. Differentiate: $\ \dot y = \dfrac{\ddot x - a\dot x - \dot f(t)}{b}$.
  3. Substitute both into the second equation: $\ \dfrac{\ddot x - a\dot x - \dot f}{b} = cx + d\,\dfrac{\dot x - ax - f}{b} + g$.
  4. Multiply by $b$ and collect terms:
$$\ddot x - (a + d)\,\dot x + (ad - bc)\,x = \dot f(t) + b\,g(t) - d\,f(t). \tag{26.3}$$

Solve (26.3) by the methods of Topic 3, then recover $y$ from (26.2). The coefficients $a + d$ and $ad - bc$ are the trace and the determinant of the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$ — they will reappear in the eigenvalue method.

Example 26.1 (pp. 254–255): $\dot x = x + y$, $\dot y = 4x - 2y + 4e^{-2t}$, $x(0) = 0$, $y(0) = -1$
  1. Here $a = 1$, $b = 1$, $c = 4$, $d = -2$, $f = 0$, $g = 4e^{-2t}$. From the first equation $y = \dot x - x$ (26.4), so $\dot y = \ddot x - \dot x$.
  2. Substituting: $\ddot x - \dot x = 4x - 2(\dot x - x) + 4e^{-2t}$, i.e. $\ddot x + \dot x - 6x = 4e^{-2t}$ (26.5) — as (26.3) predicts: $a + d = -1$, $ad - bc = -6$, right side $b\,g = 4e^{-2t}$.
  3. Complementary function: $k^2 + k - 6 = 0$, $k = 2, -3$. Particular integral $Ce^{-2t}$: $(4 - 2 - 6)C = 4$, $C = -1$. So $x = Ae^{2t} + Be^{-3t} - e^{-2t}$.
  4. $y = \dot x - x = (2A - A)e^{2t} + (-3B - B)e^{-3t} + (2 + 1)e^{-2t} = Ae^{2t} - 4Be^{-3t} + 3e^{-2t}$.
  5. Initial data: $A + B - 1 = 0$ and $A - 4B + 3 = -1$. Subtracting, $5B = 5$: $B = 1$, $A = 0$.
$$x(t) = e^{-3t} - e^{-2t}, \qquad y(t) = 3e^{-2t} - 4e^{-3t}.$$

Check: $\dot x = -3e^{-3t} + 2e^{-2t}$ and $x + y = 2e^{-2t} - 3e^{-3t}$ ✓; $x(0) = 0$, $y(0) = -1$ ✓.

Example 26.2 (pp. 255–256): $\dot x = 2x + 5y$, $\dot y = -2x$, $x(0) = 5$, $y(0) = -4$

$y = \tfrac15(\dot x - 2x)$, so $\tfrac15(\ddot x - 2\dot x) = -2x$, i.e. $\ddot x - 2\dot x + 10x = 0$, with roots $k = 1 \pm 3i$: $x = e^t(A\cos 3t + B\sin 3t)$ and $y = \tfrac{e^t}{5}\big[(3B - A)\cos 3t - (3A + B)\sin 3t\big]$. The data give $A = 5$, $B = -5$: $x = 5e^t(\cos 3t - \sin 3t)$, $y = -e^t(4\cos 3t + 2\sin 3t)$ — oscillations with exponentially growing amplitude.

3. Matrix form, superposition and the fundamental matrix

The homogeneous system $\dot x = ax + by$, $\dot y = cx + dy$ (27.1) is, with $\mathbf x = (x, y)^T$ [Eq. (27.2), p. 259],

$$\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}. \tag{27.2}$$
Superposition [Robinson, Eq. (27.3), p. 260]

If $\mathbf x_1$ and $\mathbf x_2$ solve (27.2), so does $\mathbf x = \alpha\mathbf x_1 + \beta\mathbf x_2$: $\ \dot{\mathbf x} = \alpha\dot{\mathbf x}_1 + \beta\dot{\mathbf x}_2 = \alpha A\mathbf x_1 + \beta A\mathbf x_2 = A(\alpha\mathbf x_1 + \beta\mathbf x_2) = A\mathbf x$.

Lecture material not in Robinson (lecture map, Oct 6)

The lecture developed the general theory of linear systems — linear independence of vector solutions, the Wronskian, fundamental sets and the "homogeneous + particular" structure — which Robinson does not treat for systems. The nearest book material is the scalar version: Sec. 11.3–11.4 (independence, Wronskian) and Sec. 14.1 (homogeneous + particular). The statements below follow the lecture; the proofs mirror Topic 3.

Definitions — independence, Wronskian, fundamental matrix
  • Vector functions $\mathbf x_1(t), \mathbf x_2(t)$ are linearly independent on $I$ if $\alpha\mathbf x_1(t) + \beta\mathbf x_2(t) = \mathbf 0$ for all $t \in I$ forces $\alpha = \beta = 0$.
  • Their Wronskian is the determinant of the matrix whose columns are the solutions: $W(t) = \det\big[\mathbf x_1(t)\ \ \mathbf x_2(t)\big] = x_{11}x_{22} - x_{12}x_{21}$, where $\mathbf x_j = (x_{1j}, x_{2j})^T$.
  • Two independent solutions form a fundamental set; the matrix $\Phi(t) = [\mathbf x_1\ \ \mathbf x_2]$ is a fundamental matrix. It satisfies $\dot\Phi = A\Phi$, column by column.
Theorem — Wronskian test and general solution for systems
  1. Two solutions of $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$ are linearly independent if and only if $W(t) \neq 0$; and then $W(t) \neq 0$ for all $t$.
  2. If $\Phi$ is a fundamental matrix, every solution is $\mathbf x(t) = \Phi(t)\,\mathbf c = c_1\mathbf x_1(t) + c_2\mathbf x_2(t)$, and the IVP $\mathbf x(t_0) = \mathbf x_0$ has $\mathbf c = \Phi(t_0)^{-1}\mathbf x_0$.
Proof

If $W(t_0) \neq 0$, the columns $\mathbf x_1(t_0)$, $\mathbf x_2(t_0)$ are independent vectors, so $\alpha\mathbf x_1(t_0) + \beta\mathbf x_2(t_0) = \mathbf 0$ already forces $\alpha = \beta = 0$. Conversely, if $W(t_0) = 0$, there are $(\alpha, \beta) \neq (0, 0)$ with $\alpha\mathbf x_1(t_0) + \beta\mathbf x_2(t_0) = \mathbf 0$. Then $\mathbf z = \alpha\mathbf x_1 + \beta\mathbf x_2$ solves the system (superposition) with $\mathbf z(t_0) = \mathbf 0$; so does $\mathbf 0$, and by uniqueness (Theorem 25.2) $\mathbf z \equiv \mathbf 0$: the solutions are dependent. For (2): given any solution $\mathbf x$, the system $\Phi(t_0)\mathbf c = \mathbf x(t_0)$ is solvable because $\det\Phi(t_0) = W(t_0) \neq 0$; then $\Phi\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf x$ are solutions with the same value at $t_0$, hence equal for all $t$.

Liouville's formula — Abel's identity for systems (complementary)

Write the columns as $\mathbf x_j = (p_j, q_j)^T$, so $\dot p_j = ap_j + bq_j$, $\dot q_j = cp_j + dq_j$ and $W = p_1q_2 - q_1p_2$. By the product rule

$$\dot W = \dot p_1q_2 + p_1\dot q_2 - \dot q_1p_2 - q_1\dot p_2 = (ap_1 + bq_1)q_2 + p_1(cp_2 + dq_2) - (cp_1 + dq_1)p_2 - q_1(ap_2 + bq_2).$$

The $b$ terms ($bq_1q_2 - bq_1q_2$) and the $c$ terms ($cp_1p_2 - cp_1p_2$) cancel, leaving

$$\dot W = (a + d)(p_1q_2 - q_1p_2) = (\operatorname{tr}A)\,W \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W(t) = W(t_0)\,e^{(\operatorname{tr}A)(t - t_0)}.$$

(The computation never uses that $a, b, c, d$ are constant; with time-dependent entries $W(t) = W(t_0)\exp\int_{t_0}^t\operatorname{tr}A(s)\,ds$.) For a second-order equation turned into a system, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -q & -p\end{pmatrix}$ has trace $-p$, the solution columns are $(x_j, \dot x_j)^T$, and $W$ is exactly the scalar Wronskian $x_1\dot x_2 - x_2\dot x_1$: Abel's identity of Topic 3 is the special case.

3.1 Inhomogeneous systems

Lemma — general solution of $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + \mathbf f(t)$ (lecture; scalar analogue Robinson Sec. 14.1)

If $\mathbf x_p$ is one solution, every solution is $\mathbf x = \Phi(t)\mathbf c + \mathbf x_p(t)$.

Proof

If $\mathbf x$ solves the inhomogeneous system, then $\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_p) = (A\mathbf x + \mathbf f) - (A\mathbf x_p + \mathbf f) = A(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_p)$, so $\mathbf x - \mathbf x_p$ solves the homogeneous system and equals $\Phi\mathbf c$. Conversely $\frac{d}{dt}(\Phi\mathbf c + \mathbf x_p) = A\Phi\mathbf c + A\mathbf x_p + \mathbf f$.

Variation of parameters for systems (complementary). As in Topic 3, let the constants vary: $\mathbf x_p = \Phi(t)\mathbf c(t)$. The product rule gives $\dot{\mathbf x}_p = \dot\Phi\mathbf c + \Phi\dot{\mathbf c} = A\Phi\mathbf c + \Phi\dot{\mathbf c}$; this equals $A\mathbf x_p + \mathbf f$ exactly when $\Phi\dot{\mathbf c} = \mathbf f$. Since $\det\Phi = W \neq 0$:

$$\mathbf x_p(t) = \Phi(t)\int\Phi(s)^{-1}\,\mathbf f(s)\,ds.$$
Example 26.1 again, by a fundamental matrix

The homogeneous solutions are $e^{2t}(1, 1)^T$ and $e^{-3t}(1, -4)^T$ (Section 5), so $\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} e^{2t} & e^{-3t} \\ e^{2t} & -4e^{-3t}\end{pmatrix}$, $W = -4e^{-t} - e^{-t} = -5e^{-t}$ (check: $\operatorname{tr}A = -1$, so $W \propto e^{-t}$ ✓), and $\Phi^{-1} = \frac{1}{W}\begin{pmatrix} -4e^{-3t} & -e^{-3t} \\ -e^{2t} & e^{2t}\end{pmatrix} = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4e^{-2t} & e^{-2t} \\ e^{3t} & -e^{3t}\end{pmatrix}$. With $\mathbf f = (0, 4e^{-2t})^T$: $\Phi^{-1}\mathbf f = \tfrac45\big(e^{-4t},\ -e^{t}\big)^T$, whose antiderivative is $\big(-\tfrac15e^{-4t},\ -\tfrac45e^{t}\big)^T$. Then

$$\mathbf x_p = \Phi\begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac15e^{-4t} \\ -\tfrac45e^{t}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac15e^{-2t} - \tfrac45e^{-2t} \\ -\tfrac15e^{-2t} + \tfrac{16}{5}e^{-2t}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -e^{-2t} \\ 3e^{-2t}\end{pmatrix},$$

the same particular solution that elimination produced ✓.

4. The eigenvalue method

For the scalar equation $\dot x = ax$ the solutions are $e^{at}x_0$. By analogy try a solution that keeps a fixed direction $\mathbf v$ and changes only in size [Eq. (27.4), p. 260]:

$$\mathbf x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v, \qquad \mathbf v \text{ constant}, \ \mathbf v \neq \mathbf 0. \tag{27.4}$$

Then $\dot{\mathbf x} = \lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ and $A\mathbf x = e^{\lambda t}A\mathbf v$; equating and dividing by $e^{\lambda t} \neq 0$:

$$A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v. \tag{27.5}$$

So $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$ is a solution exactly when $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ and $\mathbf v$ a corresponding eigenvector.

4.1 Finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors [Sec. 27.2, pp. 260–261]

  1. Rewrite (27.5) as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ (27.7). If $A - \lambda I$ were invertible, the only solution would be $\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$. A non-zero $\mathbf v$ therefore requires $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
  2. Expand: $\det\begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda\end{pmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc$, giving the characteristic equation $$\lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A = 0. \tag{27.8}$$
  3. For each root $\lambda$, solve the (singular) system $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$: one row is a multiple of the other, so one equation fixes the direction of $\mathbf v$. Any non-zero multiple is also an eigenvector, $A(c\mathbf v) = c\lambda\mathbf v$.

Equation (27.8) is the characteristic equation (26.3) of the eliminated second-order equation: both methods see the same numbers. Its roots satisfy $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr}A$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det A$.

Example 27.1 (pp. 262–263): $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 6 & 3\end{pmatrix}$
  1. $(2 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 12 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda + 1) = 0$: $\lambda_1 = 6$, $\lambda_2 = -1$. (Check: sum $5 = \operatorname{tr}A$, product $-6 = \det A$ ✓.)
  2. $\lambda = 6$: $(A - 6I)\mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 6 & -3\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ gives $-4v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$, $v_2 = 2v_1$: $\mathbf v_1 = (1, 2)^T$. Check: $A\mathbf v_1 = (6, 12)^T = 6\mathbf v_1$ ✓.
  3. $\lambda = -1$: $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 6 & 4\end{pmatrix}\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ gives $3v_1 + 2v_2 = 0$: $\mathbf v_2 = (2, -3)^T$. Check: $A\mathbf v_2 = (-2, 3)^T = -\mathbf v_2$ ✓.
Triangular matrices (Examples 27.2–27.3, p. 264)

For $\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & b \\ 0 & \lambda_2\end{pmatrix}$ the characteristic polynomial is $(\lambda_1 - \lambda)(\lambda_2 - \lambda) - b\cdot 0$, so the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, and $(1, 0)^T$ is an eigenvector for $\lambda_1$. The same holds for lower-triangular matrices (with $(0, 1)^T$ for $\lambda_2$) and, with both eigenvectors along the axes, for diagonal matrices. Robinson describes these as matrices "of the form $[\lambda_1\ b;\ 0\ \lambda_2]$"; the lecture calls them triangular.

5. Distinct real eigenvalues: solution and change of basis

If $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$ are real with eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$, then $e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1$ and $e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2$ are solutions, and by superposition [Eq. (28.1), p. 270]

$$\mathbf x(t) = Ae^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1 + Be^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2. \tag{28.1}$$

This is the general solution because the two solutions are independent. Their Wronskian at $t = 0$ is $\det[\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]$, non-zero by the next result.

Proposition — eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are independent [Robinson, Appendix B, p. 384]

If $A\mathbf v_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf v_1$, $A\mathbf v_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf v_2$ with $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2 \neq \mathbf 0$ and $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, then $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$ are linearly independent.

Proof

Suppose $\alpha\mathbf v_1 + \beta\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$ (B.3). Applying $A$ gives $\alpha\lambda_1\mathbf v_1 + \beta\lambda_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$ (B.4). Multiply (B.3) by $\lambda_1$ and subtract (B.4):

$$\beta(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0.$$

Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$ and $\mathbf v_2 \neq \mathbf 0$, $\beta = 0$; then (B.3) reads $\alpha\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf 0$, so $\alpha = 0$. (Robinson argues by dividing by $\alpha$ and $\lambda_1$; subtracting as here avoids having to treat $\alpha = 0$ or $\lambda_1 = 0$ separately.)

Example 28.1 (pp. 270–271): $\dot x = x + y$, $\dot y = 4x - 2y$

$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -2\end{pmatrix}$: $(1 - \lambda)(-2 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 + \lambda - 6 = 0$, $\lambda = 2, -3$. $\lambda = 2$: $(A - 2I) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 4 & -4\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf v_1 = (1, 1)^T$. $\lambda = -3$: $(A + 3I) = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 4 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf v_2 = (1, -4)^T$. Independence directly: $\alpha(1,1) + \beta(1,-4) = \mathbf 0$ gives $\alpha + \beta = 0$, $\alpha - 4\beta = 0$, so $\alpha = \beta = 0$.

$$\mathbf x(t) = Ae^{2t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix} + Be^{-3t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -4\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is the homogeneous part of Example 26.1, as it should be.

5.1 Changing coordinates: diagonalisation [Sec. 28.2, pp. 271–274]

Because $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$ are independent, every vector can be written in the eigenvector basis, $\mathbf x = \tilde x\mathbf v_1 + \tilde y\mathbf v_2$ (28.3). In matrix form, with $P = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]$ (eigenvectors as columns),

$$\mathbf x = P\tilde{\mathbf x}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf x} = P^{-1}\mathbf x. \tag{28.4–28.5}$$

(The lecture writes $\mathbf z$ for Robinson's $\tilde{\mathbf x}$.) Differentiate the second relation; $P^{-1}$ is constant:

$$\frac{d\tilde{\mathbf x}}{dt} = P^{-1}\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = P^{-1}A\mathbf x = P^{-1}AP\,\tilde{\mathbf x}. \tag{28.7}$$

Now compute $P^{-1}AP$ column by column, using $A[\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2] = [A\mathbf v_1\ A\mathbf v_2]$:

$$AP = [\lambda_1\mathbf v_1\ \ \lambda_2\mathbf v_2] = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2\end{pmatrix} = P\Lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P^{-1}AP = \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2\end{pmatrix}.$$

So in the new coordinates the system is uncoupled [Eqs. (28.8)–(28.9)]:

$$\dot{\tilde x} = \lambda_1\tilde x, \quad \dot{\tilde y} = \lambda_2\tilde y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tilde x = Ae^{\lambda_1t}, \quad \tilde y = Be^{\lambda_2t},$$

and $\mathbf x = P\tilde{\mathbf x} = Ae^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1 + Be^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2$ recovers (28.1).

Example 28.2 (pp. 274–276): decoupling $\dot x = x + y$, $\dot y = 4x - 2y$

$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -4\end{pmatrix}$, $\det P = -4 - 1 = -5 \neq 0$, and with $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a\end{pmatrix}$ (Appendix B), $P^{-1} = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $\tilde x = \tfrac15(4x + y)$, $\tilde y = \tfrac15(x - y)$. Then

$$P^{-1}AP = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -4\end{pmatrix} = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ 2 & 12\end{pmatrix} = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & -15\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{pmatrix}\ ✓.$$

Robinson stresses that this is not the efficient way to solve the system — (28.1) is — but it is the key to drawing it.

6. Phase portraits: nodes and saddles

A phase portrait shows the curves traced in the $(x, y)$ plane by solutions, with arrows for increasing $t$. The origin is always a stationary solution of $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$ (and the only one when $\det A \neq 0$). Draw first the uncoupled system, then "twist" it.

6.1 The uncoupled system [Sec. 28.3, pp. 276–278]

Solutions of $\dot{\tilde x} = \lambda_1\tilde x$, $\dot{\tilde y} = \lambda_2\tilde y$ are $\tilde x = \tilde x_0e^{\lambda_1t}$, $\tilde y = \tilde y_0e^{\lambda_2t}$ (28.12). To find the curve they trace, eliminate $t$: $e^{t} = (\tilde x/\tilde x_0)^{1/\lambda_1}$, so

$$\tilde y = \tilde y_0\,e^{\lambda_2t} = \tilde y_0\left(\frac{\tilde x}{\tilde x_0}\right)^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1} = K\,\tilde x^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}, \qquad K = \tilde y_0\,\tilde x_0^{-\lambda_2/\lambda_1}.$$

(Equivalently, by the chain rule $\dfrac{d\tilde y}{d\tilde x} = \dfrac{\dot{\tilde y}}{\dot{\tilde x}} = \dfrac{\lambda_2\tilde y}{\lambda_1\tilde x}$, a separable equation.) The axes themselves are trajectories: data on an axis stay on it.

  • Stable node ($\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < 0$): every solution tends to $\mathbf 0$. Since $|\lambda_2| < |\lambda_1|$, the $\tilde y$ component decays more slowly, so trajectories arrive tangent to the slow eigendirection.
  • Unstable node ($0 < \lambda_1 < \lambda_2$): the same picture with arrows reversed; solutions leave the origin tangent to the eigendirection of smaller $|\lambda|$.
  • Saddle ($\lambda_2 < 0 < \lambda_1$): $\tilde x$ grows while $\tilde y$ decays; the curves $\tilde x\,\tilde y^{\,r} = K$ ($r = -\lambda_1/\lambda_2 > 0$) are hyperbola-like. Only solutions starting on the $\tilde y$-axis reach the origin.

6.2 Back to the original coordinates [Sec. 28.4, p. 279]

The map $\mathbf x = P\tilde{\mathbf x}$ sends the $\tilde x$-axis to the line through $\mathbf v_1$ and the $\tilde y$-axis to the line through $\mathbf v_2$. Straight lines stay straight and the origin stays fixed, so the picture is the uncoupled one, sheared and rotated. Practical recipe:

  1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
  2. Draw the eigenvector lines; put arrows pointing away from the origin if $\lambda > 0$ and towards it if $\lambda < 0$.
  3. Fill in the other trajectories consistently with the type (node or saddle), tangent to the slow direction near the origin and parallel to the fast one far away.

6.3 Stable and unstable manifolds [Sec. 28.5, pp. 281–282]

Definitions

The stable manifold $W^s(\mathbf 0)$ is the set of initial points whose trajectories tend to the origin as $t \to +\infty$; the unstable manifold $W^u(\mathbf 0)$ is the set whose trajectories tend to the origin as $t \to -\infty$.

$W^s(\mathbf 0)$$W^u(\mathbf 0)$
Stable nodethe whole plane$\{\mathbf 0\}$
Saddlethe eigenvector line of the negative eigenvaluethe eigenvector line of the positive eigenvalue
Unstable node$\{\mathbf 0\}$the whole plane

At a saddle the stable manifold is a separatrix: points on one side are carried to infinity along one branch of $W^u$, points on the other side along the opposite branch. In Example 28.3, initial data above the line $y = -4x$ end up with $x, y \to +\infty$; data below it with $x, y \to -\infty$.

Terminology

The lecture notes mention a "saddle node"; Robinson says only "saddle point" or "saddle" and reserves "node" for stable and unstable nodes, so the intended term is almost certainly "saddle". (In bifurcation theory, Topic 2, a saddle-node is something else.)

Section 07 • Robinson Ch. 29 (beyond the Oct 8 lecture)

7. Complex eigenvalues: spirals and centres

Let $\lambda_\pm = \rho \pm i\omega$ ($\omega > 0$) with eigenvectors $\boldsymbol\eta_\pm = \mathbf v_1 \pm i\mathbf v_2$, where $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$ are real (for a real matrix the eigenvector of $\bar\lambda$ is the conjugate of that of $\lambda$).

7.1 Real solutions [Sec. 29.1, pp. 285–286]

  1. For a real solution take conjugate coefficients: $\mathbf x = Ce^{\lambda_+t}\boldsymbol\eta_+ + C^*e^{\lambda_-t}\boldsymbol\eta_- = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\big[Ce^{(\rho + i\omega)t}(\mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2)\big]$ (29.1), with $C = \alpha + i\beta$.
  2. Euler: $Ce^{i\omega t} = (\alpha\cos\omega t - \beta\sin\omega t) + i(\beta\cos\omega t + \alpha\sin\omega t)$. Multiply by $\mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2$ and keep the real part: $(\alpha\cos\omega t - \beta\sin\omega t)\mathbf v_1 - (\beta\cos\omega t + \alpha\sin\omega t)\mathbf v_2$.
  3. With $A = 2\alpha$, $B = -2\beta$:
$$\mathbf x(t) = e^{\rho t}\Big[\big(A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\big)\mathbf v_1 + \big(B\cos\omega t - A\sin\omega t\big)\mathbf v_2\Big]. \tag{29.2}$$
Example 29.1 (p. 286): $\dot x = 2x + 5y$, $\dot y = -2x$

From Example 27.5, $\lambda = 1 \pm 3i$ and, for $\lambda = 1 + 3i$, the first row $(1 - 3i)v_1 + 5v_2 = 0$ gives $\boldsymbol\eta = (5,\ -1 + 3i)^T = (5, -1)^T + i(0, 3)^T$. (Check: $A\boldsymbol\eta = (10 - 5 + 15i,\ -10)^T = (5 + 15i,\ -10)^T$ and $(1 + 3i)\boldsymbol\eta = (5 + 15i,\ -1 + 3i - 3i - 9)^T$ ✓.) By (29.2), $x = 5e^t(A\cos 3t + B\sin 3t)$, $y = e^t\big[(3B - A)\cos 3t - (3A + B)\sin 3t\big]$, the same as Example 26.2.

7.2 Canonical form and polar coordinates [Sec. 29.2, pp. 287–290]

Taking real and imaginary parts of $A(\mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2) = (\rho + i\omega)(\mathbf v_1 + i\mathbf v_2)$:

$$A\mathbf v_1 = \rho\mathbf v_1 - \omega\mathbf v_2, \qquad A\mathbf v_2 = \omega\mathbf v_1 + \rho\mathbf v_2. \tag{29.3}$$

So with $P = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]$ (invertible, Exercise 29.4), $AP = P\begin{pmatrix} \rho & \omega \\ -\omega & \rho\end{pmatrix}$ and in the coordinates $\mathbf x = P\tilde{\mathbf x}$:

$$\dot{\tilde x} = \rho\tilde x + \omega\tilde y, \qquad \dot{\tilde y} = -\omega\tilde x + \rho\tilde y. \tag{29.7}$$

Use polar coordinates $\tilde x = r\cos\theta$, $\tilde y = r\sin\theta$:

  1. $r^2 = \tilde x^2 + \tilde y^2$. Differentiate (chain rule): $2r\dot r = 2\tilde x\dot{\tilde x} + 2\tilde y\dot{\tilde y} = 2\tilde x(\rho\tilde x + \omega\tilde y) + 2\tilde y(-\omega\tilde x + \rho\tilde y) = 2\rho r^2$, so $\dot r = \rho r$.
  2. $\theta = \tan^{-1}(\tilde y/\tilde x)$. Chain and quotient rules: $\dot\theta = \dfrac{1}{1 + (\tilde y/\tilde x)^2}\cdot\dfrac{\tilde x\dot{\tilde y} - \tilde y\dot{\tilde x}}{\tilde x^2} = \dfrac{\tilde x\dot{\tilde y} - \tilde y\dot{\tilde x}}{r^2} = \dfrac{-\omega\tilde x^2 - \omega\tilde y^2}{r^2} = -\omega$.
  3. Integrate: $r(t) = r(0)e^{\rho t}$, $\theta(t) = \theta(0) - \omega t$.
  • $\rho < 0$: stable spiral — the radius decays while the angle turns uniformly.
  • $\rho > 0$: unstable spiral.
  • $\rho = 0$: centre — closed orbits (ellipses in the original coordinates), periodic with period $2\pi/\omega$; stable but not attracting.
Direction of rotation (Robinson Sec. 29.3, Example 29.3)

The sign of $\omega$ alone does not tell you the sense of rotation in the original plane, because $P$ may reflect. Check a single velocity instead: on the positive $y$-axis ($x = 0$, $y > 0$) evaluate $\dot x$. If $\dot x > 0$ the motion at the top is to the right: clockwise. For $\dot x = 2x + 5y$, $\dot y = -2x$: $\dot x = 5y > 0$, clockwise; confirmed on the positive $x$-axis, where $\dot y = -2x < 0$ (downwards).

Section 08 • Robinson Ch. 30 (beyond the Oct 8 lecture)

8. A repeated eigenvalue: stars and improper nodes

8.1 Stars [Sec. 30.1, p. 295]

If $A = \lambda I$, every vector is an eigenvector and $\mathbf x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\mathbf x(0)$: solutions move along straight lines through the origin. A stable star if $\lambda < 0$, an unstable star if $\lambda > 0$.

8.2 Improper nodes [Sec. 30.2, pp. 295–298]

If $A \neq \lambda I$ there is only one eigenvector direction $\mathbf v$, so (28.1) cannot be used. Robinson builds a second basis vector:

  1. Choose any $\mathbf v_2$ not parallel to $\mathbf v$ and set $\mathbf v_1 = (A - \lambda I)\mathbf v_2$. One shows $\mathbf v_1$ is an eigenvector (Robinson p. 296; equivalently $(A - \lambda I)^2 = O$ by Cayley–Hamilton, Exercises 30.2–30.3). Hence $A\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_1 + \lambda\mathbf v_2$ (30.1).
  2. With $P = [\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2]$: $AP = [\lambda\mathbf v_1\ \ \mathbf v_1 + \lambda\mathbf v_2] = P\begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda\end{pmatrix}$, so $\dot{\tilde x} = \lambda\tilde x + \tilde y$, $\dot{\tilde y} = \lambda\tilde y$ (30.2)–(30.3).
  3. The second equation gives $\tilde y = Ae^{\lambda t}$. The first becomes $\dot{\tilde x} - \lambda\tilde x = Ae^{\lambda t}$; integrating factor $e^{-\lambda t}$: $\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-\lambda t}\tilde x\big) = A$, so $e^{-\lambda t}\tilde x = At + B$.
$$\mathbf x(t) = \big[Ate^{\lambda t} + Be^{\lambda t}\big]\mathbf v_1 + Ae^{\lambda t}\mathbf v_2. \tag{30.4}$$

The factor $te^{\lambda t}$ is the vector version of the repeated-root solution of Topic 3. As $t \to \pm\infty$ the $t$-term dominates, so trajectories become parallel to $\mathbf v_1$ at both ends and bend in an S shape: an improper node, stable if $\lambda < 0$ and unstable if $\lambda > 0$.

Example 30.1 (p. 298): $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$

$\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 9 = 0$, $\lambda = 3$ twice, eigenvector $(2, 1)^T$ only. Choosing $\mathbf v_2 = (1, 0)^T$: $\mathbf v_1 = (A - 3I)\mathbf v_2 = (2, 1)^T$, an eigenvector as claimed. Orientation of the S: on $x = 0$, $y > 0$, $\dot x = -4y < 0$, so trajectories cross the positive $y$-axis moving left. Unstable improper node.

9.1 The trace–determinant shortcut (complementary)

Since the eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - T\lambda + D = 0$ with $T = \operatorname{tr}A = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ and $D = \det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2$, the type can be read off $T$ and $D$ without computing the eigenvalues:

  • $D < 0$: real eigenvalues of opposite sign — saddle.
  • $D > 0$ and $T^2 > 4D$: real, same sign as $T$ — node (stable if $T < 0$).
  • $D > 0$ and $T^2 < 4D$: complex with $\rho = T/2$ — spiral (stable if $T < 0$), or centre if $T = 0$.
  • $T^2 = 4D$: repeated eigenvalue — star or improper node.
The mass–spring–damper in the phase plane

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ (Section 1), $T = -\lambda$ and $D = \omega^2 > 0$, and the characteristic equation $\mu^2 + \lambda\mu + \omega^2 = 0$ is the auxiliary equation of Topic 3. The damping regimes are exactly the portrait types:

DampingConditionPhase portrait in $(x, \dot x)$
none$\lambda = 0$ ($T = 0$)centre: energy ellipses $\tfrac12\dot x^2 + \tfrac12\omega^2x^2 = E$
under-damped$\lambda^2 < 4\omega^2$stable spiral, clockwise (on $x = 0$, $\dot x > 0$ means $x$ increases)
critical$\lambda^2 = 4\omega^2$stable improper node ($A \neq \lambda I$ always, since the top-left entry is $0$)

The same classification underlies flight dynamics: linearising the longitudinal equations of an aircraft about trimmed flight gives a matrix whose complex eigenvalue pairs are the short-period and phugoid modes, and their real parts decide whether the aircraft returns to trim after a gust.
