Section 01

1. Direct Integration & Separable Differential Equations

The most elementary ordinary differential equations are those that can be resolved directly by quadrature—that is, by finding an antiderivative.

1.1 Direct Integration ($y' = f(x)$)

When the derivative of the unknown state $y(x)$ depends exclusively on the independent variable $x$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x) \tag{1.1} $$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC), if $f(x)$ is continuous on an open interval $I$, the general solution is the family of antiderivatives:

$$ y(x) = \int f(x) \, dx + C = F(x) + C $$

For an Initial Value Problem (IVP) with initial datum $y(x_0) = y_0$, we write the solution using dummy integration variables:

$$ \mathbf{y(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^x f(s) \, ds} \tag{1.2} $$

1.2 Separable Differential Equations ($y' = g(x) h(y)$)

A first-order ODE is separable if the velocity field factors multiplicatively:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = g(x) h(y) \tag{1.3} $$
Rigorous Justification via the Chain Rule

While physicists often manipulate differentials casually as $\frac{dy}{h(y)} = g(x) dx$, this operation is mathematically justified by the chain rule of differential calculus:

  1. Equilibrium roots: First determine all constant roots $y^*$ such that $h(y^*) = 0$. The constant function $y(x) \equiv y^*$ is an exact solution for all $x$.
  2. Non-equilibrium states ($h(y) \neq 0$): Divide equation (1.3) by $h(y(x))$: $$ \frac{1}{h(y(x))} \frac{dy}{dx} = g(x) $$
  3. Let $H(y)$ be an antiderivative of $\frac{1}{h(y)}$, so that $H'(y) = \frac{1}{h(y)}$. By the chain rule: $$ \frac{d}{dx} \left[ H(y(x)) \right] = H'(y(x)) \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{h(y(x))} \frac{dy}{dx} $$
  4. Integrating both sides with respect to $x$ across $[x_0, x]$: $$ \int_{x_0}^x \frac{d}{ds}\left[ H(y(s)) \right] ds = \int_{x_0}^x g(s) \, ds \implies H(y(x)) - H(y(x_0)) = \int_{x_0}^x g(s) \, ds $$
  5. By change of variables ($u = y(s), du = y'(s) ds$): $$ \mathbf{\int_{y_0}^{y(x)} \frac{1}{h(u)} \, du = \int_{x_0}^x g(s) \, ds} \tag{1.4} $$

1.3 Non-Elementary Gaussian Integrals & Finite-Time Blow-Up Thresholds

In aerospace diffusion processes and thermal boundary layer analysis, differential equations frequently lead to the non-elementary Gaussian integral:

$$ \int_0^\infty e^{-s^2} \, ds = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \tag{1.5} $$
Blow-Up Threshold Analysis (Problem 2.2)

Consider the separable nonlinear problem modeling exothermic combustion:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = e^{-t^2} y^2, \quad y(0) = y_0 > 0 $$

Separating variables and integrating across $[0, t]$:

$$ \int_{y_0}^{y(t)} \frac{du}{u^2} = \int_0^t e^{-s^2} ds \implies -\frac{1}{y(t)} + \frac{1}{y_0} = \int_0^t e^{-s^2} ds $$ $$ \mathbf{y(t) = \frac{y_0}{1 - y_0 \int_0^t e^{-s^2} \, ds}} \tag{1.6} $$

Since $\int_0^t e^{-s^2} ds$ increases monotonically toward the upper bound $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$ as $t \to \infty$:

  • Subcritical Regime ($y_0 < \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}$): The denominator never vanishes. The solution exists globally $\forall t \ge 0$, with asymptotic ceiling $y(\infty) = \frac{y_0}{1 - y_0 \sqrt{\pi}/2} < \infty$.
  • Critical Threshold ($y_0 = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}$): The denominator approaches $0$ as $t \to \infty$, causing blow-up at infinite time ($y(t) \to +\infty$ as $t \to \infty$).
  • Supercritical Regime ($y_0 > \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}$): The denominator hits zero at a finite blow-up time $t^*$: $$ \int_0^{t^*} e^{-s^2} ds = \frac{1}{y_0} < \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \implies \lim_{t \to t^{*-}} y(t) = +\infty $$ Thermal runaway occurs in finite time $t^*$.
Section 02

2. First-Order Linear Equations & The Integrating Factor Method

A first-order linear ordinary differential equation can always be normalized into the standard canonical form:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} + p(t) x(t) = q(t) \tag{2.1} $$

where $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ are continuous functions on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

2.1 Formal Derivation of the Integrating Factor $\mu(t)$

We seek a strictly positive multiplier $\mu(t) > 0$ such that multiplying equation (2.1) transforms the left-hand side into the exact derivative of the product $\mu(t) x(t)$:

$$ \mu(t) \frac{dx}{dt} + \mu(t) p(t) x(t) = \mu(t) q(t) \tag{2.2} $$

By the product rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \mu(t) x(t) \right] = \mu(t) \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{d\mu}{dt} x(t) \tag{2.3} $$

Equating (2.2) and (2.3) requires:

$$ \frac{d\mu}{dt} x(t) = \mu(t) p(t) x(t) \implies \frac{d\mu}{dt} = p(t) \mu(t) $$

Separating variables for $\mu > 0$:

$$ \int \frac{d\mu}{\mu} = \int p(t) \, dt \implies \ln \mu(t) = \int p(t) \, dt \implies \mathbf{\mu(t) = \exp\left( \int p(t) \, dt \right)} \tag{2.4} $$

2.2 Closed-Form General Solution

Substituting $\mu(t)$ into (2.2):

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \mu(t) x(t) \right] = \mu(t) q(t) $$

Integrating with respect to $t$:

$$ \mu(t) x(t) = \int \mu(t) q(t) \, dt + C \implies \mathbf{x(t) = \frac{1}{\mu(t)} \left[ \int \mu(t) q(t) \, dt + C \right]} \tag{2.5} $$
Definite IVP Representation: Transient vs. Forced Response

With initial datum $x(t_0) = x_0$ and $\mu(t) = \exp\left(\int_{t_0}^t p(s) ds\right)$ (so $\mu(t_0) = 1$):

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = \underbrace{x_0 e^{-\int_{t_0}^t p(s) ds}}_{x_h(t) \text{ (Transient)}} + \underbrace{\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\int_s^t p(u) du} q(s) \, ds}_{x_p(t) \text{ (Forced Steady State)}}} \tag{2.6} $$

2.3 Asymptotic Relaxation: Proof of $x(t) \to \frac{b}{a}$ (Problem 2.3)

Consider the dissipative dynamical model:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} + \left( a + \frac{1}{t} \right) x = b \quad (a > 0, \; b \in \mathbb{R}) $$

1. Integrating factor: $$ \int \left(a + \frac{1}{t}\right) dt = at + \ln t \implies \mu(t) = e^{at + \ln t} = t e^{at} $$

2. General solution via integration by parts ($\int t e^{at} dt = \frac{t e^{at}}{a} - \frac{e^{at}}{a^2}$): $$ x(t) = \frac{1}{t e^{at}} \left[ b \int t e^{at} dt + C \right] = \frac{1}{t e^{at}} \left[ b \left( \frac{t e^{at}}{a} - \frac{e^{at}}{a^2} \right) + C \right] $$ $$ x(t) = \frac{b}{a} - \frac{b}{a^2 t} + \frac{C}{t} e^{-at} \tag{2.7} $$

3. Taking the long-time asymptotic limit as $t \to \infty$: $$ \lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = \frac{b}{a} - 0 + 0 = \mathbf{\frac{b}{a}} $$ The system undergoes exponential dissipation of its initial condition, asymptotically reaching the steady equilibrium $\frac{b}{a}$.

Section 03

3. Exact Differential Equations & Special Integrating Factors

Consider a first-order differential equation expressed in differential form:

$$ M(x, y) \, dx + N(x, y) \, dy = 0 \tag{3.1} $$

3.1 Definition and the Euler-Cauchy-Schwarz Criterion

Equation (3.1) is exact on a simply connected domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ if there exists a $C^2$ scalar potential function $F(x, y)$ such that its total differential satisfies $dF = M dx + N dy$. In that case, the ODE is $dF = 0$, with solution:

$$ \mathbf{F(x, y) = C} \tag{3.2} $$
Theorem: Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Exactness

Let $M, N, \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ be continuous on simply connected $D$. The form (3.1) is exact if and only if:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}} \quad \forall (x, y) \in D \tag{3.3} $$

Proof of Necessity: If $F$ exists, $M = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}$ and $N = \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}$. By Schwarz's theorem on the equality of mixed second partial derivatives of $C^2$ functions: $$ \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial y \partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x \partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} \quad \blacksquare $$

3.2 Systematic Reconstruction of the Potential Function $F(x, y)$

  1. Integrate $M(x, y)$ with respect to $x$ holding $y$ constant: $$ F(x, y) = \int M(x, y) \, dx + h(y) \tag{3.4} $$
  2. Differentiate (3.4) with respect to $y$ and equate to $N(x, y)$: $$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int M(x, y) \, dx \right) + h'(y) = N(x, y) $$
  3. Isolate $h'(y)$: $$ h'(y) = N(x, y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int M(x, y) \, dx \right) \tag{3.5} $$ Due to condition (3.3), the right-hand side is guaranteed to be completely independent of $x$.
  4. Integrate $h'(y)$ to find $h(y)$, yielding the implicit solution $F(x, y) = C$.

3.3 Special Integrating Factors for Non-Exact Equations

If $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \neq \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$, we seek a multiplier $\mu(x, y)$ such that $\mu M dx + \mu N dy = 0$ is exact:

  • Factor depending solely on $x$ ($\mu = \mu(x)$): $$ \phi(x) \equiv \frac{\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}}{N} \implies \mathbf{\mu(x) = \exp\left( \int \phi(x) \, dx \right)} \tag{3.6} $$
  • Factor depending solely on $y$ ($\mu = \mu(y)$): $$ \psi(y) \equiv \frac{\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}}{M} \implies \mathbf{\mu(y) = \exp\left( \int \psi(y) \, dy \right)} \tag{3.7} $$
Universal Exactness of Separated Equations (Problem 2.6)

Every separable equation $f(x) + g(y) \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \iff f(x) dx + g(y) dy = 0$ is unconditionally exact: $$ M(x) = f(x) \implies \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 0, \quad N(y) = g(y) \implies \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 0 $$ Since $0 = 0$, exactness holds universally, with potential function $F(x, y) = \int f(x) dx + \int g(y) dy = C$.

Section 04

4. Nonlinear Substitutions & Bernoulli Equations

A powerful strategy for resolving nonlinear first-order ODEs is transforming the dependent variable to linearize the equation.

4.1 The Canonical Bernoulli Equation

Introduced by Jacob Bernoulli in 1695, the Bernoulli differential equation is:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} - a(x) y = b(x) y^\alpha \quad (\alpha \in \mathbb{R}) \tag{4.1} $$
The Linearizing Transformation $z = y^{1-\alpha}$

For $\alpha \neq 0, 1$, equation (4.1) is nonlinear. We linearize it through the following derivation:

  1. Divide (4.1) by $y^\alpha$ (for $y \neq 0$): $$ y^{-\alpha} \frac{dy}{dx} - a(x) y^{1-\alpha} = b(x) \tag{4.2} $$
  2. Define the new dependent variable: $$ z(x) \equiv y(x)^{1-\alpha} \tag{4.3} $$
  3. Differentiating by the chain rule: $$ \frac{dz}{dx} = (1 - \alpha) y^{-\alpha} \frac{dy}{dx} \implies y^{-\alpha} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{1 - \alpha} \frac{dz}{dx} $$
  4. Substitute into (4.2) and multiply by $(1 - \alpha)$: $$ \mathbf{\frac{dz}{dx} - (1 - \alpha) a(x) z(x) = (1 - \alpha) b(x)} \tag{4.4} $$

Equation (4.4) is a standard linear first-order ODE for $z(x)$, solved via integrating factor. The original solution is recovered via $y(x) = [z(x)]^{1/(1-\alpha)}$.

4.2 Scale-Invariant Homogeneous Equations ($u = y/x$)

An ODE of the form $\frac{dy}{dx} = F\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$ is invariant under uniform scale transformations $(x, y) \to (\lambda x, \lambda y)$.

Setting $u(x) \equiv \frac{y(x)}{x} \implies y(x) = x u(x)$, differentiation yields $\frac{dy}{dx} = u + x \frac{du}{dx}$. Substituting:

$$ u + x \frac{du}{dx} = F(u) \implies \mathbf{\frac{du}{F(u) - u} = \frac{dx}{x}} \tag{4.5} $$

Equation (4.5) is always separable.

Section 05

5. Well-Posedness, Picard-Lindelöf Theorem & Analytical Pathologies

Consider the initial value problem:

$$ \begin{cases} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(t, y) \\ y(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} \tag{5.1} $$

5.1 The Picard-Lindelöf Theorem & Lipschitz Continuity

A function $f(t, y)$ is Lipschitz continuous with respect to $y$ on domain $D$ if $\exists L < \infty$ such that:

$$ |f(t, y_1) - f(t, y_2)| \le L |y_1 - y_2| \quad \forall (t, y_1), (t, y_2) \in D \tag{5.2} $$

A continuous bounded partial derivative $\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right| \le L$ is a sufficient condition for (5.2). Under continuity and Lipschitz conditions on a rectangle $R$, Picard-Lindelöf guarantees the existence of a unique solution on $I = [t_0 - h, t_0 + h]$.

5.2 Analytical Dissection of Pathologies

Pathology 1: Non-Lipschitz Branching Non-Uniqueness (Problem 2.14)

Consider $y' = 3 y^{2/3}$ with $y(0) = 0$:

  • Derivative: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2 y^{-1/3} \implies \lim_{y \to 0} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \infty$. The Lipschitz condition fails at the origin.
  • Dual Solutions: (1) Trivial equilibrium: $y_1(t) \equiv 0$;
    (2) Separation: $\int y^{-2/3} dy = \int 3 dt \implies 3 y^{1/3} = 3t \implies y_2(t) = t^3$.
  • Both satisfy $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=0$. Determinism is breached: solutions can branch infinitely at any delay $c \ge 0$.
Pathology 2: Domain Singularity vs. Picard-Lindelöf (Problem 2.15)

Consider $y' = \frac{2y+1}{t}$ with $y(0) = -\frac{1}{2}$:

General solution for $t \neq 0$: $y(t) = -\frac{1}{2} + C t^2$. For any value of $C \in \mathbb{R}$, $y(0) = -\frac{1}{2}$. Does this contradict Picard-Lindelöf?

No! The vector field $f(t, y) = \frac{2y+1}{t}$ has a division by zero at $t = 0$. Because $f$ is not even defined (let alone continuous) at the initial point, Picard's theorem does not apply!

5.3 Independent Uniqueness Proof via the Direct Difference Method

Energy Difference Method (Problem 2.17)

To prove that an IVP has a unique solution without invoking Picard-Lindelöf:

  1. Suppose $y_1(t)$ and $y_2(t)$ are two solutions to $y' = f(t, y)$ with $y_1(t_0) = y_2(t_0) = y_0$.
  2. Define the difference function $w(t) \equiv y_1(t) - y_2(t)$, satisfying $w(t_0) = 0$.
  3. Construct the Lyapunov energy function $E(t) \equiv [w(t)]^2 \ge 0$.
  4. Differentiate with respect to $t$: $\dot{E}(t) = 2 w(t) \dot{w}(t) = 2 w(t) [f(t, y_1) - f(t, y_2)]$.
  5. If $\dot{E}(t) \le 0$ for all $t \ge t_0$, then $0 \le E(t) \le E(t_0) = 0 \implies E(t) \equiv 0 \implies w(t) \equiv 0$.
  6. Therefore $y_1(t) \equiv y_2(t)$, proving unconditional uniqueness.
Section 06

6. Qualitative Analysis of Autonomous ODEs & Phase Lines

For an autonomous system $\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x)$, global qualitative trajectories can be characterized completely without analytical integration.

6.1 Linear Stability Criterion for 1D Equilibria

Stationary states satisfy $f(x^*) = 0$. Expanding around $x^*$ for small perturbations $\xi(t) = x(t) - x^*$:

$$ \dot{\xi} \approx f'(x^*) \xi \implies \xi(t) = \xi_0 e^{f'(x^*) t} \tag{6.1} $$
  • $f'(x^*) < 0$: Asymptotically Stable Attractor (Sink). Trajectories converge to $x^*$.
  • $f'(x^*) > 0$: Unstable Repellor (Source). Trajectories diverge away from $x^*$.
  • $f'(x^*) = 0$: Non-hyperbolic / Semistable. Higher-order Taylor derivatives dictate flow.

6.2 The No-Crossing Theorem and Invariant Confinement Corridors

The No-Crossing Theorem (Problem 2.12)

By Picard-Lindelöf uniqueness, two distinct solutions $y_1(t)$ and $y_2(t)$ of a smooth ODE $y' = f(t, y)$ can never intersect or touch in the $(t, y)$ plane:

$$ y_1(t_0) < y_2(t_0) \implies y_1(t) < y_2(t) \quad \forall t \text{ in their common domain} $$

Consequence: Any known particular solution acts as an impenetrable barrier. If $y_1(t) = -t-1$ and $y_2(t) = t^2+1$ are solutions, a third solution starting at $y(0) = 0$ is forever trapped inside the invariant corridor:

$$ -t - 1 < y(t) < t^2 + 1 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} $$

6.3 Supercritical Pitchfork Bifurcations: $\dot{x} = x(\kappa^2 - x^2)$

In aerospace structural stability and roll-pitch coupling, qualitative dynamics change as parameters pass critical thresholds.

Consider $\dot{x} = x(\kappa^2 - x^2)$ with $\kappa > 0$ (Problem 2.16):

  • Equilibria: $f(x) = 0 \implies x_1^* = -\kappa, \quad x_2^* = 0, \quad x_3^* = +\kappa$.
  • Stability Derivative: $f'(x) = \kappa^2 - 3x^2$.
    • At $x_2^* = 0$: $f'(0) = \kappa^2 > 0 \implies$ Unstable Repellor.
    • At $x_{1,3}^* = \pm\kappa$: $f'(\pm\kappa) = \kappa^2 - 3\kappa^2 = -2\kappa^2 < 0 \implies$ Asymptotically Stable Attractors.
  • Phase Flow: The state space $\mathbb{R}$ splits into four invariant corridors: $(-\infty, -\kappa) \to -\kappa$, $(-\kappa, 0) \to -\kappa$, $(0, \kappa) \to +\kappa$, and $(\kappa, \infty) \to +\kappa$. This is the canonical model of a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation.
