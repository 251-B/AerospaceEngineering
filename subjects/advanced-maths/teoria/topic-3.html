Second-Order Linear ODEs:
General Theory and Constant Coefficients

A rigorous academic presentation of the analytical and algebraic theory of second-order linear differential equations. Section 1 Introduction, Canonical Forms & Physical Oscillations

In engineering mechanics and aerospace applications, dynamic laws are overwhelmingly formulated as second-order differential equations. Newton's Second Law ($\mathbf{F} = m \mathbf{a} = m \ddot{\mathbf{x}}$) establishes that the second time derivative of displacement—the acceleration—is determined by forces which may depend on time $t$, position $x(t)$, and velocity $\dot{x}(t)$.

1.1 General and Normalized Standard Forms

A general second-order linear ordinary differential equation in the unknown real-valued function $x(t)$ defined on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ takes the form:

General Form $$ a_2(t) \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + a_1(t) \frac{dx}{dt} + a_0(t) x(t) = g(t) $$

where $a_2(t), a_1(t), a_0(t)$, and $g(t)$ are given continuous functions on $I$. On any open subinterval where the leading coefficient function does not vanish ($a_2(t) \neq 0$), we divide through by $a_2(t)$ to obtain the normalized canonical standard form:

Normalized Standard Form $$ \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + p(t) \frac{dx}{dt} + q(t) x(t) = f(t) \quad \iff \quad x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = f(t) $$

where the normalized coefficient functions and non-homogeneous source term are defined by:

$$ p(t) \equiv \frac{a_1(t)}{a_2(t)}, \quad q(t) \equiv \frac{a_0(t)}{a_2(t)}, \quad f(t) \equiv \frac{g(t)}{a_2(t)} $$
Singular Points of the Differential Equation

Any point $t_s \in \mathbb{R}$ at which $a_2(t_s) = 0$ is a singular point of the differential equation. At such points, $p(t)$ or $q(t)$ may become unbounded, and the fundamental existence and uniqueness theory does not guarantee the smooth continuation of solutions across $t_s$. Analytical domain boundaries must always be chosen within intervals where $a_2(t) \neq 0$.

1.2 Physical Paradigm: Damped Mechanical Oscillators

Consider a classic spring-mass-damper system, representative of an aircraft oleo-pneumatic landing gear strut, an aeroelastic wing section, or a satellite attitude suspension:

$$ m \ddot{x} + c \dot{x} + k x = F_{\text{ext}}(t) $$

Dividing by the inertia mass $m > 0$:

$$ \ddot{x} + \frac{c}{m}\dot{x} + \frac{k}{m}x = \frac{F_{\text{ext}}(t)}{m} \implies \ddot{x} + 2\zeta \omega_n \dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \frac{F_{\text{ext}}(t)}{m} $$

where $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the undamped natural frequency and $\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}}$ is the damping ratio. Notice that in order to uniquely predict the trajectory of the mass forwards and backwards in time, knowing the initial position $x(t_0) = x_0$ alone is insufficient; one must also prescribe its initial velocity $\dot{x}(t_0) = y_0$. This physical requirement establishes the mathematical structure of the Second-Order Initial Value Problem (IVP).

Definition: Second-Order Initial Value Problem (IVP)

Given an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, continuous functions $p, q, f: I \to \mathbb{R}$, a base point $t_0 \in I$, and arbitrary initial data $x_0, y_0 \in \mathbb{R}$, the Second-Order Initial Value Problem consists of finding a twice-differentiable function $x: I \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:

$$ \begin{cases} x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = f(t) \\ x(t_0) = x_0 \\ x'(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} $$

Section 2 Existence and Uniqueness for Second-Order IVPs

The bedrock of linear differential equation theory is that solutions to linear Initial Value Problems not only exist and are strictly unique, but their domain of definition is determined completely and globally by the continuity of the coefficient functions.

Theorem 11.1 (Robinson: Fundamental Existence and Uniqueness Theorem)

Let the coefficient functions $p(t)$, $q(t)$, and the forcing term $f(t)$ be continuous on an open interval $I = (a, b) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Let $t_0 \in I$ be any arbitrary point, and let $x_0, y_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary prescribed constants.

Then there exists a unique solution $x(t)$ to the initial value problem:

$$ \begin{cases} x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = f(t) \\ x(t_0) = x_0 \\ x'(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} $$

Furthermore, this unique solution $x(t)$ is twice continuously differentiable ($x \in C^2(I)$) and is defined on the entire interval $I$.

2.1 Vectorization: Reduction to a First-Order $2 \times 2$ Planar System

The rigorous proof of Theorem 11.1 rests on converting the scalar second-order ODE into an equivalent system of two coupled first-order ODEs. We introduce the phase-space state vector $\mathbf{X}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2$:

$$ \mathbf{X}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t) \\ x_2(t) \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} x(t) \\ x'(t) \end{pmatrix} $$

Differentiating $\mathbf{X}(t)$ with respect to time $t$:

$$ x_1'(t) = x'(t) = x_2(t) $$ $$ x_2'(t) = x''(t) = -q(t)x(t) - p(t)x'(t) + f(t) = -q(t)x_1(t) - p(t)x_2(t) + f(t) $$

Expressing this in matrix-vector form yields the non-autonomous first-order planar system:

Equivalent $2 \times 2$ First-Order System $$ \frac{d\mathbf{X}}{dt} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -q(t) & -p(t) \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{X}(t) + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ f(t) \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{X}(t_0) = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{pmatrix} $$

Because the matrix entries $0, 1, -q(t), -p(t)$ and the vector $(0, f(t))^T$ are continuous on $I$, the vector field $\mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{X}) = A(t)\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}(t)$ is globally Lipschitz continuous with respect to $\mathbf{X}$ on every compact subinterval $[t_1, t_2] \subset I$. By the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem for vector systems, there exists a unique solution $\mathbf{X}(t)$ defined for all $t \in I$. The first component $x_1(t) = x(t)$ provides the unique solution to the original scalar equation.

2.2 Absence of Finite-Time Blow-Up in Linear Equations

A profound property of linear differential equations—in sharp contrast to nonlinear equations—is that solutions cannot undergo finite-time blow-up within the interval of continuity $I$.

  • Nonlinear contrast: For the nonlinear IVP $x' = x^2, x(0) = 1$, the right-hand side is $C^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, yet the unique solution $x(t) = \frac{1}{1-t}$ experiences finite-time blow-up at $t^* = 1$.
  • Linear global guarantee: In a linear ODE $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = f(t)$, the maximal interval of existence of the solution $x(t)$ is guaranteed to be at least as large as the common interval of continuity of $p(t), q(t)$, and $f(t)$. Singularities in the solution can only occur where $p(t), q(t)$, or $f(t)$ are singular, or where the leading coefficient $a_2(t) = 0$.
Example: Determining the Maximal Interval of Existence

Consider the initial value problem:

$$ (t^2 - 4) x'' + 3t x' + x = \ln(t + 1), \quad x(1) = 2, \quad x'(1) = -1 $$

First, put the equation into normalized canonical form:

$$ x'' + \frac{3t}{(t-2)(t+2)} x' + \frac{1}{(t-2)(t+2)} x = \frac{\ln(t+1)}{(t-2)(t+2)} $$

Identify the domains of continuity of the individual terms:

  • Leading coefficient $a_2(t) = t^2 - 4 = 0 \implies t = \pm 2$.
  • Source term $f(t) = \frac{\ln(t+1)}{t^2 - 4}$ requires $t + 1 > 0 \implies t > -1$ and $t \neq 2$.

The continuous intervals for all coefficients simultaneously are $(-1, 2)$ and $(2, \infty)$. Since the initial base point is $t_0 = 1 \in (-1, 2)$, Theorem 11.1 guarantees that the unique solution $x(t)$ exists on the entire interval $I = (-1, 2)$.

Section 3 The Linear Operator $L$ & The Superposition Principle

The algebraic structure of solutions to linear differential equations is governed by operator theory on infinite-dimensional function spaces.

3.1 Definition of the Differential Operator $L$

Let $C^2(I)$ denote the vector space of real-valued functions defined on an open interval $I$ with continuous second derivatives, and let $C^0(I)$ denote the space of continuous functions on $I$. We define the second-order linear differential operator $L: C^2(I) \to C^0(I)$ by:

Linear Differential Operator $$ L[x](t) \equiv x''(t) + p(t)x'(t) + q(t)x(t) $$

With this operator notation:

  • The non-homogeneous equation is written compactly as: $L[x] = f$.
  • The homogeneous equation (where $f(t) \equiv 0$) is written as: $L[x] = 0$.

3.2 Formal Proof of Operator Linearity

Recall that an operator $L$ is linear if it preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication: $L[\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2] = \alpha L[x_1] + \beta L[x_2]$ for all $x_1, x_2 \in C^2(I)$ and all $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$.

Theorem: Linearity of the Differential Operator $L$

Proof:

Let $x_1, x_2 \in C^2(I)$ and let $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$. Consider the linear combination $y(t) = \alpha x_1(t) + \beta x_2(t)$. By the fundamental linearity of ordinary differentiation:

$$ y'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\left[\alpha x_1(t) + \beta x_2(t)\right] = \alpha x_1'(t) + \beta x_2'(t) $$ $$ y''(t) = \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left[\alpha x_1(t) + \beta x_2(t)\right] = \alpha x_1''(t) + \beta x_2''(t) $$

Substituting $y(t), y'(t)$, and $y''(t)$ into the definition of $L$:

$$ \begin{aligned} L[\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2] &= (\alpha x_1'' + \beta x_2'') + p(t)(\alpha x_1' + \beta x_2') + q(t)(\alpha x_1 + \beta x_2) \\ &= \left[ \alpha x_1'' + \alpha p(t)x_1' + \alpha q(t)x_1 \right] + \left[ \beta x_2'' + \beta p(t)x_2' + \beta q(t)x_2 \right] \\ &= \alpha \left[ x_1'' + p(t)x_1' + q(t)x_1 \right] + \beta \left[ x_2'' + p(t)x_2' + q(t)x_2 \right] \\ &= \alpha L[x_1] + \beta L[x_2] \end{aligned} $$

This completes the proof. $\blacksquare$

3.3 The Superposition Principle and the Solution Subspace $\ker(L)$

An immediate and powerful consequence of the linearity of $L$ is the Principle of Superposition for homogeneous differential equations:

The Superposition Principle for Homogeneous Equations

If $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are any two solutions of the homogeneous equation $L[x] = 0$ on the interval $I$, then for any arbitrary constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, the linear combination:

$$ x(t) = c_1 x_1(t) + c_2 x_2(t) $$

is also a solution of $L[x] = 0$ on $I$.

Proof: Applying linearity: $L[c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2] = c_1 L[x_1] + c_2 L[x_2] = c_1(0) + c_2(0) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

In algebraic terms, the set of all solutions to the homogeneous equation is precisely the kernel (or null space) of $L$:

$$ S_H \equiv \{ x \in C^2(I) : L[x] = 0 \} = \ker(L) $$

Since $L$ is linear, $\ker(L)$ is an authentic vector subspace of $C^2(I)$, closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

3.4 Affine Structure of the Non-Homogeneous Solution

For the non-homogeneous equation $L[x] = f(t)$, the solution set is an affine subspace:

$$ x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t) $$

where $x_h \in \ker(L)$ is the general complementary solution to the homogeneous equation, and $x_p \in C^2(I)$ is any particular solution satisfying $L[x_p] = f$. Indeed, if $x_A$ and $x_B$ are any two solutions of $L[x] = f$, their difference satisfies:

$$ L[x_A - x_B] = L[x_A] - L[x_B] = f - f = 0 \implies x_A - x_B \in \ker(L) $$

Section 4 Linear Independence of Functions & The Wronskian Determinant

Having established that the set of homogeneous solutions $\ker(L)$ is a vector space, our central objective is to determine its dimension and construct a basis.

4.1 Definition of Linear Independence

Definition 11.2 (Robinson): Linearly Independent Functions

Two functions $x_1, x_2: I \to \mathbb{R}$ are said to be linearly dependent on an interval $I$ if there exist constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, not both zero ($c_1^2 + c_2^2 \neq 0$), such that:

$$ c_1 x_1(t) + c_2 x_2(t) = 0 \quad \text{for all } t \in I $$

Conversely, $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are linearly independent on $I$ if the only linear combination that vanishes identically on $I$ is the trivial one:

$$ c_1 x_1(t) + c_2 x_2(t) = 0 \quad \forall t \in I \implies c_1 = 0 \text{ and } c_2 = 0 $$

For exactly two functions, linear dependence on $I$ simply means that one function is a constant multiple of the other: $x_2(t) = k x_1(t)$ or $x_1(t) = k x_2(t)$ for all $t \in I$.

4.2 Fitting Initial Conditions: Derivation of the Wronskian

Suppose we have found two solutions $x_1(t), x_2(t)$ of $L[x] = 0$. By superposition, $x(t) = c_1 x_1(t) + c_2 x_2(t)$ is a solution for any $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$. Can we always find constants $c_1, c_2$ to satisfy arbitrary prescribed initial conditions at an arbitrary base point $t_0 \in I$?

$$ \begin{cases} x(t_0) = x_0 \\ x'(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} $$

Evaluating $x(t)$ and its derivative $x'(t) = c_1 x_1'(t) + c_2 x_2'(t)$ at $t = t_0$ produces the linear algebraic system:

$$ \begin{cases} c_1 x_1(t_0) + c_2 x_2(t_0) = x_0 \\ c_1 x_1'(t_0) + c_2 x_2'(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} \iff \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t_0) & x_2(t_0) \\ x_1'(t_0) & x_2'(t_0) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{pmatrix} $$

By elementary linear algebra, this $2 \times 2$ system has a unique solution $(c_1, c_2)$ for any prescribed vector $(x_0, y_0)^T$ if and only if the coefficient matrix is invertible—that is, its determinant is non-zero.

Definition: The Wronskian Determinant $$ W[x_1, x_2](t) \equiv \det \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t) & x_2(t) \\ x_1'(t) & x_2'(t) \end{pmatrix} = x_1(t) x_2'(t) - x_2(t) x_1'(t) $$

By Cramer's Rule, when $W(t_0) \neq 0$, the unique coefficients are given explicitly by:

$$ c_1 = \frac{x_0 x_2'(t_0) - y_0 x_2(t_0)}{W[x_1, x_2](t_0)}, \quad c_2 = \frac{y_0 x_1(t_0) - x_0 x_1'(t_0)}{W[x_1, x_2](t_0)} $$

4.3 Constructive Proof that $\dim(\ker(L)) = 2$

Theorem: Dimension of the Solution Space

Let $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ be continuous on the open interval $I$. The vector space of solutions to the homogeneous second-order linear differential equation:

$$ S_H = \ker(L) = \{ x \in C^2(I) : x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0 \} $$

has dimension exactly 2: $\dim(\ker(L)) = 2$.

Rigorous Constructive Proof:

  • 1 Fix an arbitrary base point $t_0 \in I$.
  • 2 By Theorem 11.1 (Existence and Uniqueness), there exist two unique solutions $u_1(t)$ and $u_2(t)$ on $I$ defined by the canonical initial conditions: $$ \begin{cases} u_1(t_0) = 1 \\ u_1'(t_0) = 0 \end{cases} \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{cases} u_2(t_0) = 0 \\ u_2'(t_0) = 1 \end{cases} $$
  • 3 Compute the Wronskian of $\{u_1, u_2\}$ at $t_0$: $$ W[u_1, u_2](t_0) = \det \begin{pmatrix} u_1(t_0) & u_2(t_0) \\ u_1'(t_0) & u_2'(t_0) \end{pmatrix} = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = (1)(1) - (0)(0) = 1 \neq 0 $$ Since $W(t_0) = 1 \neq 0$, the solutions $u_1$ and $u_2$ are linearly independent.
  • 4 Now, let $x(t)$ be any arbitrary solution of $L[x] = 0$ on $I$. Let its values at $t_0$ be $x(t_0) = x_0$ and $x'(t_0) = y_0$.
  • 5 Construct the linear combination function $v(t) \equiv x_0 u_1(t) + y_0 u_2(t)$. Since $u_1, u_2 \in \ker(L)$, by superposition $L[v] = 0$. Evaluating the initial values of $v(t)$ at $t_0$: $$ v(t_0) = x_0 u_1(t_0) + y_0 u_2(t_0) = x_0(1) + y_0(0) = x_0 $$ $$ v'(t_0) = x_0 u_1'(t_0) + y_0 u_2'(t_0) = x_0(0) + y_0(1) = y_0 $$
  • 6 Both $x(t)$ and $v(t)$ satisfy the identical homogeneous ODE $L[y] = 0$ and the identical initial conditions $(x_0, y_0)$ at $t_0$. By the Uniqueness Theorem (Theorem 11.1), they must coincide on the entire interval $I$: $$ x(t) \equiv v(t) = x_0 u_1(t) + y_0 u_2(t) \quad \forall t \in I $$
  • 7 Therefore, $\{u_1, u_2\}$ spans $\ker(L)$. Because $\{u_1, u_2\}$ is both linearly independent and spans $\ker(L)$, it constitutes a basis for $\ker(L)$. Since the basis contains exactly two functions: $$ \mathbf{\dim(\ker(L)) = 2} \quad \blacksquare $$
Exam Warning: Wronskian of Arbitrary Functions vs. ODE Solutions

Do not confuse the Wronskian of arbitrary functions with the Wronskian of solutions to a linear ODE:

  • For arbitrary functions, $W[f, g](t) \equiv 0$ does not imply linear dependence. For instance, $f(t) = t^3$ and $g(t) = |t|^3$ on $\mathbb{R}$ are linearly independent on $(-1, 1)$, yet $W[f, g](t) \equiv 0$ everywhere!
  • For solutions of $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0$ with continuous coefficients, the equivalence is absolute: $W(t) \neq 0 \iff \{x_1, x_2\}$ are linearly independent. This crucial equivalence is guaranteed by Abel's Identity.

Section 5 Abel's Identity & The Wronskian Dichotomy

Abel's Theorem establishes a remarkable differential equation governing the evolution of the Wronskian of any two solutions, independent of the coefficient $q(t)$.

Theorem 11.3 (Abel's Theorem / Liouville's Formula)

Let $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ be any two solutions of the homogeneous equation $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0$ on the open interval $I$, where $p(t)$ and $q(t)$ are continuous. Then their Wronskian $W(t) = W[x_1, x_2](t)$ satisfies the first-order linear differential equation:

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = -p(t) W(t) $$

Consequently, for any fixed reference point $t_0 \in I$:

Abel's Identity $$ W(t) = W(t_0) \exp\left( -\int_{t_0}^t p(s) \, ds \right) $$

5.1 Complete Step-by-Step Derivation

We begin directly from the definition of the Wronskian:

$$ W(t) = x_1(t) x_2'(t) - x_2(t) x_1'(t) $$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $t$ using the product rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dW}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}\left[ x_1(t) x_2'(t) \right] - \frac{d}{dt}\left[ x_2(t) x_1'(t) \right] \\ &= \left[ x_1'(t) x_2'(t) + x_1(t) x_2''(t) \right] - \left[ x_2'(t) x_1'(t) + x_2(t) x_1''(t) \right] \end{aligned} $$

Notice that the first-derivative cross terms are identical and cancel out: $x_1'(t) x_2'(t) - x_2'(t) x_1'(t) = 0$. Thus:

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = x_1(t) x_2''(t) - x_2(t) x_1''(t) $$

Now, because $x_1$ and $x_2$ are solutions to $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0$, we substitute their second derivatives:

$$ x_1''(t) = -p(t) x_1'(t) - q(t) x_1(t) $$ $$ x_2''(t) = -p(t) x_2'(t) - q(t) x_2(t) $$

Substituting into the expression for $\frac{dW}{dt}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{dW}{dt} &= x_1(t) \left[ -p(t) x_2'(t) - q(t) x_2(t) \right] - x_2(t) \left[ -p(t) x_1'(t) - q(t) x_1(t) \right] \\ &= -p(t) x_1(t) x_2'(t) - q(t) x_1(t) x_2(t) + p(t) x_2(t) x_1'(t) + q(t) x_1(t) x_2(t) \end{aligned} $$

The terms proportional to $q(t)$ cancel identically: $-q(t) x_1 x_2 + q(t) x_1 x_2 = 0$. Regrouping the remaining terms:

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = -p(t) \left[ x_1(t) x_2'(t) - x_2(t) x_1'(t) \right] = -p(t) W(t) $$

This is a first-order linear separable ODE. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t$:

$$ \int_{W(t_0)}^{W(t)} \frac{dW}{W} = -\int_{t_0}^t p(s) \, ds \implies \ln\left| \frac{W(t)}{W(t_0)} \right| = -\int_{t_0}^t p(s) \, ds $$

Exponentiating both sides yields Abel's Identity:

$$ W(t) = W(t_0) \exp\left( -\int_{t_0}^t p(s) \, ds \right) \quad \blacksquare $$

5.2 The Fundamental Wronskian Dichotomy

Because the exponential function $\exp\left(-\int_{t_0}^t p(s) ds\right)$ is strictly positive for all $t \in I$ whenever $p(s)$ is continuous, Abel's Identity proves the Wronskian Dichotomy Principle:

The Wronskian Dichotomy Principle

For any two solutions $x_1(t), x_2(t)$ of $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0$ on an interval $I$ with continuous $p, q$:

  • Either: $W(t) \neq 0$ for all $t \in I$ (meaning $\{x_1, x_2\}$ is linearly independent everywhere on $I$).
  • Or: $W(t) \equiv 0$ for all $t \in I$ (meaning $\{x_1, x_2\}$ is linearly dependent everywhere on $I$).

The Wronskian of solutions can never vanish at an isolated point! If $W(t_0) = 0$ at a single point $t_0 \in I$, then $W(t)$ is identically zero across the entire interval $I$.

5.3 Application: Reduction of Order (d'Alembert's Formula)

If one non-zero solution $x_1(t)$ of $x'' + p(t)x' + q(t)x = 0$ is known, Abel's Identity allows us to construct a second, linearly independent solution $x_2(t)$ via the ansatz $x_2(t) = v(t) x_1(t)$:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = x_1 (v' x_1 + v x_1') - (v x_1) x_1' = x_1(t)^2 v'(t) $$

Equating this to Abel's Identity $W(t) = C \exp\left(-\int p(t) dt\right)$ (setting $C = 1$ for a basis function):

$$ x_1(t)^2 v'(t) = \exp\left(-\int p(t) dt\right) \implies v'(t) = \frac{\exp\left(-\int p(t) dt\right)}{[x_1(t)]^2} $$

Integrating $v'(t)$ gives d'Alembert's Reduction of Order Formula:

Reduction of Order Formula $$ x_2(t) = x_1(t) \int \frac{\exp\left( -\int p(t) \, dt \right)}{[x_1(t)]^2} \, dt $$

Section 6 Constant-Coefficient Homogeneous ODEs

We now focus on the fundamental class of equations where the coefficient functions are real constants:

Constant-Coefficient Homogeneous Equation $$ a \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + b \frac{dx}{dt} + c x(t) = 0 \quad (a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, \; a \neq 0) $$

6.1 The Exponential Ansatz and the Characteristic Equation

Motivated by first-order equations where the rate of change is proportional to the function, we test the exponential trial ansatz:

$$ x(t) = e^{kt} $$

where $k$ is an unknown constant parameter. Differentiating $x(t)$ with respect to $t$:

$$ x'(t) = k e^{kt}, \quad x''(t) = k^2 e^{kt} $$

Substitute these into the differential equation:

$$ a (k^2 e^{kt}) + b (k e^{kt}) + c (e^{kt}) = 0 \implies (a k^2 + b k + c) e^{kt} = 0 $$

Since $e^{kt} \neq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, this holds if and only if $k$ satisfies the characteristic algebraic polynomial:

Characteristic Equation $$ a k^2 + b k + c = 0 $$

The roots are given by the quadratic formula:

$$ k = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} $$

The algebraic sign of the discriminant $\Delta \equiv b^2 - 4ac$ uniquely partitions the dynamic response into three exhaustive regimes:

Regime Discriminant Characteristic Roots Fundamental Set $\{x_1, x_2\}$ General Solution $x(t)$ Wronskian $W(t)$
Case 1: Overdamped $\Delta > 0$ $k_1 \neq k_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ $\{e^{k_1 t}, e^{k_2 t}\}$ $c_1 e^{k_1 t} + c_2 e^{k_2 t}$ $(k_2 - k_1) e^{(k_1+k_2)t} \neq 0$
Case 2: Critically Damped $\Delta = 0$ $k = -\frac{b}{2a}$ (mult. 2) $\{e^{kt}, t e^{kt}\}$ $(c_1 + c_2 t) e^{kt}$ $e^{2kt} \neq 0$
Case 3: Underdamped $\Delta < 0$ $k = \rho \pm i\omega \quad (\omega > 0)$ $\{e^{\rho t}\cos\omega t, e^{\rho t}\sin\omega t\}$ $e^{\rho t}(A \cos\omega t + B \sin\omega t)$ $\omega e^{2\rho t} \neq 0$

6.2 Case 1: Distinct Real Roots ($\Delta = b^2 - 4ac > 0$)

When $\Delta > 0$, the characteristic polynomial yields two distinct real numbers:

$$ k_1 = \frac{-b + \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}, \quad k_2 = \frac{-b - \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a} \quad (k_1 \neq k_2) $$

This produces two exponential solutions: $x_1(t) = e^{k_1 t}$ and $x_2(t) = e^{k_2 t}$. Verifying their linear independence via the Wronskian determinant:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} e^{k_1 t} & e^{k_2 t} \\ k_1 e^{k_1 t} & k_2 e^{k_2 t} \end{pmatrix} = e^{k_1 t} (k_2 e^{k_2 t}) - e^{k_2 t} (k_1 e^{k_1 t}) = (k_2 - k_1) e^{(k_1 + k_2)t} $$

Since $k_1 \neq k_2$ and the exponential never vanishes, $W(t) \neq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Thus, $\{e^{k_1 t}, e^{k_2 t}\}$ forms a fundamental set of solutions, and the general solution is:

Case 1 General Solution $$ x(t) = c_1 e^{k_1 t} + c_2 e^{k_2 t} \quad (c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}) $$

6.3 Case 2: Repeated Real Root ($\Delta = b^2 - 4ac = 0$)

When the discriminant vanishes, the characteristic polynomial possesses a single repeated real root of multiplicity 2:

$$ k = -\frac{b}{2a} \implies 2a k + b = 0 $$

The ansatz yields only one exponential solution: $x_1(t) = e^{kt}$. Since $\dim(\ker(L)) = 2$, we require a second, linearly independent solution.

Theorem: The Repeated Root Solution is $x_2(t) = t e^{kt}$

Proof 1 (Direct Differential Verification):

Let $x_2(t) = t e^{kt}$. Differentiating using the product rule:

$$ x_2'(t) = 1 \cdot e^{kt} + t \cdot (k e^{kt}) = (1 + kt) e^{kt} $$ $$ x_2''(t) = k e^{kt} + k(1 + kt) e^{kt} = (2k + k^2 t) e^{kt} $$

Substitute $x_2, x_2'$, and $x_2''$ into the operator $L[x_2] = a x_2'' + b x_2' + c x_2$:

$$ \begin{aligned} L[x_2] &= a (2k + k^2 t) e^{kt} + b (1 + kt) e^{kt} + c (t) e^{kt} \\ &= \left[ t (a k^2 + b k + c) + (2a k + b) \right] e^{kt} \end{aligned} $$

Examine the two bracketed terms:

  1. $a k^2 + b k + c = 0$ because $k$ is a root of the characteristic polynomial.
  2. $2a k + b = 2a\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) + b = -b + b = 0$.

Therefore: $L[x_2] = [t \cdot 0 + 0] e^{kt} \equiv 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

Proof 2 (Derivation via Reduction of Order):

Using d'Alembert's reduction of order formula with $p = b/a = -2k$ and $x_1(t) = e^{kt}$:

$$ x_2(t) = e^{kt} \int \frac{\exp\left( -\int \frac{b}{a} dt \right)}{[e^{kt}]^2} \, dt = e^{kt} \int \frac{e^{- (b/a) t}}{e^{2kt}} \, dt = e^{kt} \int \frac{e^{2kt}}{e^{2kt}} \, dt = e^{kt} \int 1 \, dt = t e^{kt} $$

This confirms that $x_2(t) = t e^{kt}$ is an exact solution. $\blacksquare$

Now compute the Wronskian of $\{e^{kt}, t e^{kt}\}$:

$$ W[e^{kt}, t e^{kt}](t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} e^{kt} & t e^{kt} \\ k e^{kt} & (1 + kt) e^{kt} \end{pmatrix} = e^{kt}(1 + kt)e^{kt} - t e^{kt}(k e^{kt}) = e^{2kt} \neq 0 $$

The Wronskian is non-zero everywhere. Thus, the general solution for Case 2 is:

Case 2 General Solution $$ x(t) = (c_1 + c_2 t) e^{kt} = c_1 e^{kt} + c_2 t e^{kt} \quad (c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}) $$

6.4 Case 3: Complex Conjugate Roots ($\Delta = b^2 - 4ac < 0$)

When $\Delta < 0$, $\sqrt{\Delta} = \sqrt{-(4ac - b^2)} = i \sqrt{4ac - b^2}$ where $4ac - b^2 > 0$. The roots form a complex conjugate pair:

$$ k = \rho \pm i \omega $$

with real part $\rho$ and imaginary frequency $\omega > 0$ given by:

$$ \rho = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad \omega = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} $$

The exponential trial ansatz generates two complex-valued solutions:

$$ z_1(t) = e^{(\rho + i\omega)t} = e^{\rho t} e^{i\omega t}, \quad z_2(t) = e^{(\rho - i\omega)t} = e^{\rho t} e^{-i\omega t} $$

Derivation via Euler's Formula

Recall Euler's Formula, which relates complex exponentials to trigonometric functions:

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta $$

Applying Euler's formula to $z_1(t)$ and $z_2(t)$:

$$ z_1(t) = e^{\rho t} \left[ \cos(\omega t) + i \sin(\omega t) \right] $$ $$ z_2(t) = e^{\rho t} \left[ \cos(-\omega t) + i \sin(-\omega t) \right] = e^{\rho t} \left[ \cos(\omega t) - i \sin(\omega t) \right] $$

Because the differential operator $L$ has strictly real coefficients, the real and imaginary parts of any complex solution are individually real-valued solutions! Using the Superposition Principle:

$$ u(t) \equiv \frac{z_1(t) + z_2(t)}{2} = e^{\rho t} \cos(\omega t) $$ $$ v(t) \equiv \frac{z_1(t) - z_2(t)}{2i} = e^{\rho t} \sin(\omega t) $$

Wronskian of the Real Oscillatory Solutions

To prove that $\{u(t), v(t)\}$ forms an authentic real basis, we calculate their Wronskian determinant:

$$ u'(t) = e^{\rho t} \left( \rho \cos\omega t - \omega \sin\omega t \right) $$ $$ v'(t) = e^{\rho t} \left( \rho \sin\omega t + \omega \cos\omega t \right) $$

Substituting into $W[u, v](t) = u v' - v u'$:

$$ \begin{aligned} W[u, v](t) &= \left( e^{\rho t} \cos\omega t \right) \left[ e^{\rho t}(\rho \sin\omega t + \omega \cos\omega t) \right] - \left( e^{\rho t} \sin\omega t \right) \left[ e^{\rho t}(\rho \cos\omega t - \omega \sin\omega t) \right] \\ &= e^{2\rho t} \left[ \rho \cos\omega t \sin\omega t + \omega \cos^2\omega t - \rho \sin\omega t \cos\omega t + \omega \sin^2\omega t \right] \\ &= e^{2\rho t} \left[ \omega (\cos^2\omega t + \sin^2\omega t) \right] \\ &= \mathbf{\omega e^{2\rho t}} \end{aligned} $$

Since $\omega > 0$ and the exponential never vanishes, $W(t) = \omega e^{2\rho t} \neq 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Therefore, $\{e^{\rho t} \cos\omega t, e^{\rho t} \sin\omega t\}$ is a true real fundamental solution set, and the general real solution is:

Case 3 General Real Solution $$ x(t) = e^{\rho t} \left[ A \cos(\omega t) + B \sin(\omega t) \right] \quad (A, B \in \mathbb{R}) $$

6.5 Amplitude-Phase Polar Representation

In vibration mechanics, acoustics, and flutter analysis, the Cartesian linear combination $A \cos\omega t + B \sin\omega t$ is rewritten as a single shifted harmonic cosine:

Amplitude-Phase Form $$ x(t) = M e^{\rho t} \cos(\omega t - \phi) $$

Using the cosine addition formula $\cos(\omega t - \phi) = \cos\omega t \cos\phi + \sin\omega t \sin\phi$:

$$ M \cos(\omega t - \phi) = (M \cos\phi) \cos\omega t + (M \sin\phi) \sin\omega t $$

Equating coefficients with $A \cos\omega t + B \sin\omega t$:

$$ A = M \cos\phi, \quad B = M \sin\phi $$

Squaring and adding both equations yields the initial amplitude $M$:

$$ A^2 + B^2 = M^2 (\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = M^2 \implies \mathbf{M = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}} $$

Dividing the second equation by the first yields the phase lag $\phi$:

$$ \tan\phi = \frac{B}{A} \implies \mathbf{\phi = \text{atan2}(B, A) \in (-\pi, \pi]} $$
  • Pseudo-frequency: $\omega = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}$ (rad/s)
  • Pseudo-period: $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$ (s)
  • Decaying Exponential Envelope: $\pm M e^{\rho t}$ (bounds oscillations when $\rho < 0$)
