1. What a differential equation is

Physical laws are rarely statements about quantities themselves; they are statements about how quantities change. Newton's second law relates force to the rate of change of momentum, a mass balance relates the net inflow to the rate of change of the stored mass, and a population model relates the number of births per year to the current population. Writing any of these laws down produces an equation in which the unknown is a function, and that function appears together with some of its derivatives. Such an equation is a differential equation.

Definition — independent and dependent variables

The independent variables are the arguments of the unknown function (e.g. $t$ in $x(t)$, $x$ in $y(x)$, both $x$ and $y$ in $G(x,y)$). The unknown function itself is the dependent variable. A differential equation is an equation relating a dependent variable, its derivatives and the independent variables. [Robinson, Sec. 3.1, p. 11]

Notation. Robinson uses a dot for derivatives with respect to time and a prime for derivatives with respect to $x$:

$$\dot z = \frac{dz}{dt}, \qquad \ddot z = \frac{d^2 z}{dt^2}, \qquad y' = \frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad y'' = \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}.$$

Time is the preferred independent variable throughout this course.

1.1 Ordinary versus partial differential equations

The most important distinction depends on how many independent variables there are. With one independent variable only ordinary derivatives can occur; with two or more, the derivatives are partial.

Definition — ODE and PDE [Robinson, Sec. 3.1, pp. 11–12]
  • An ordinary differential equation (ODE) has exactly one independent variable, so only ordinary derivatives appear.
  • A partial differential equation (PDE) has more than one independent variable, so the derivatives are partial derivatives.

Aerospace relevance

The Navier–Stokes equations (3.3) are the model behind every computational fluid dynamics code used to design wings, intakes and nozzles. They are PDEs; this course deals with ODEs only [Robinson, p. 12]. ODEs still appear constantly in aerospace work: flight-path and attitude dynamics ($t$ is the only independent variable), structural vibration modes, and the self-similar reductions of the flow equations, such as the boundary-layer equation of Section 2.

2. Order and autonomy

Definition — order [Robinson, Sec. 3.2, Eq. (3.4), p. 13]

The order of a differential equation is the order of the highest derivative that occurs. Formally, an $n$th-order ODE for $y(t)$ is an equation of the form

$$F\!\left(\frac{d^n y}{dt^n},\ \frac{d^{n-1} y}{dt^{n-1}},\ \dots,\ \frac{dy}{dt},\ y,\ t\right) = 0, \tag{3.4}$$

in which $d^n y/dt^n$ really occurs in $F$.

The last condition is not pedantry. If $F(\ddot y,\dot y,y,t) = y - t$, then "$F = 0$" is the algebraic relation $y = t$, which contains no derivative at all and so is not a differential equation [p. 13].

Definition — autonomous equation [Robinson, p. 13]

If the independent variable $t$ does not occur explicitly in the equation, as in $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(y)$, the equation is autonomous. Otherwise it is non-autonomous.

Autonomy has a clean physical meaning: the law does not depend on the clock. If $y(t)$ solves $\dot y = f(y)$, then so does the time-shifted function $z(t) = y(t - s)$ for any fixed $s$. To see this, apply the chain rule with the inner function $u(t) = t - s$, $du/dt = 1$:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{dy}{du}\Big|_{u=t-s}\cdot\frac{du}{dt} = f\big(y(t-s)\big)\cdot 1 = f\big(z(t)\big).$$

So an experiment started today and the same experiment started tomorrow evolve identically. This is what makes the geometric phase-line method of Topic 2 work.

Higher order means more state, not more difficulty in principle

Any second-order equation $\ddot x = f(\dot x, x, t)$ is equivalent to a first-order system for the two unknowns $x_1 = x$, $x_2 = \dot x$:

$$\dot x_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot x_2 = f(x_2, x_1, t).$$

The first equation is the definition of $x_2$; the second is the original equation rewritten. The same trick ($x_k = y^{(k-1)}$, $k = 1,\dots,n$) turns any $n$th-order equation into $n$ first-order ones. This is developed in Topic 4 [Robinson, Sec. 25.1, p. 251 for $n = 2$; Exercise 25.1, p. 252 for general $n$].

3. Linear and nonlinear equations

Linearity is the classification with the largest practical consequences. Robinson's summary is blunt: linear problems are relatively "easy" (an explicit solution can usually be found) and nonlinear problems are "hard" (explicit solutions exist only in very particular cases) [p. 13].

Definition — linear ODE (lecture note, Sep 9; Robinson Eq. (3.5), p. 14)

An $n$th-order ODE is linear if it can be written in the form

$$a_n(x)\,y^{(n)}(x) + \dots + a_2(x)\,y''(x) + a_1(x)\,y'(x) + a_0(x)\,y(x) = b(x) \tag{1}$$

for some functions $a_i$ ($i = 0,1,\dots,n$) and $b$ of the independent variable only. If the ODE cannot be written in this form, it is nonlinear. A linear equation is homogeneous if $b \equiv 0$ and inhomogeneous otherwise.

Robinson writes the same definition with $t$ as the independent variable and $f(t)$ on the right, $a_n(t)\,y^{(n)} + \dots + a_1(t)\,\dot y + a_0(t)\,y = f(t)$, and summarises it as "only multiples of $y$ and its derivatives occur" [Eq. (3.5), p. 14]. The coefficients may be as nonlinear as they like in the independent variable; what matters is how the unknown appears.

3.1 A practical test

Read each term of the equation and ask what it does to the unknown $y$. The equation is linear exactly when every term is one of:

  1. a function of the independent variable alone (it goes into $b$);
  2. a function of the independent variable multiplied by $y$ or by one derivative $y^{(k)}$, to the first power.

Anything else makes the equation nonlinear: powers such as $y^2$ or $(y')^2$, products such as $y\,y'$, and nonlinear functions of the unknown such as $\sin y$, $e^{y}$ or $\sqrt{y'}$.

3.3 Why linearity matters: the superposition principle

Collect the left-hand side of (1) into an operator $L$ acting on functions with $n$ derivatives, $L[y] = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k(x)\,y^{(k)}$. Then a linear ODE is simply $L[y] = b$.

Proposition — $L$ is a linear operator

For any constants $c_1, c_2$ and any $n$-times differentiable $y_1, y_2$: $\ L[c_1y_1 + c_2y_2] = c_1L[y_1] + c_2L[y_2]$.

Proof

Differentiation is linear, $\dfrac{d^k}{dx^k}(c_1y_1 + c_2y_2) = c_1y_1^{(k)} + c_2y_2^{(k)}$ for each $k$. Therefore

$$L[c_1y_1 + c_2y_2] = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k\big(c_1y_1^{(k)} + c_2y_2^{(k)}\big) = c_1\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_ky_1^{(k)} + c_2\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_ky_2^{(k)} = c_1L[y_1] + c_2L[y_2].$$

Two consequences drive the whole course:

  • Homogeneous case. If $L[y_1] = 0$ and $L[y_2] = 0$ then $L[c_1y_1 + c_2y_2] = c_1\cdot 0 + c_2\cdot 0 = 0$: sums and multiples of solutions are solutions (Topic 3, Topic 4).
  • Inhomogeneous case. If $L[y_p] = b$ and $L[y_h] = 0$ then $L[y_h + y_p] = 0 + b = b$: general solution = homogeneous solution + one particular solution (Robinson Sec. 14.1).

For a nonlinear equation both statements fail. For $y' = y^2$: $y_1 = 1/(1-t)$ is a solution, but $2y_1$ is not, since $(2y_1)' = 2y_1^2 \neq (2y_1)^2 = 4y_1^2$.

4. Systems of ODEs

Most models have more than one unknown. A system of ODEs couples several dependent variables that share one independent variable. Robinson's first example (which he interprets as two competing species only much later, p. 326) has unknowns $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ [Eq. (3.9), p. 15]:

$$\dot x = x\,(4 - 2x - y), \qquad \dot y = y\,(9 - 3x - 3y). \tag{3.9}$$

Each species grows when rare (the constant terms $4x$ and $9y$), suffers from crowding by its own kind ($-2x^2$, $-3y^2$) and from competition with the other ($-xy$, $-3xy$). The system is first order, autonomous and nonlinear (it contains $x^2$, $y^2$ and $xy$).

Where the solutions settle

A point where both right-hand sides vanish is a constant (equilibrium) solution. From $x(4 - 2x - y) = 0$ we need $x = 0$ or $y = 4 - 2x$; from $y(9 - 3x - 3y) = 0$ we need $y = 0$ or $y = 3 - x$. Combining the four options:

  • $x = 0,\ y = 0$: the point $(0,0)$;
  • $x = 0,\ y = 3 - x$: the point $(0,3)$;
  • $y = 4 - 2x,\ y = 0$: the point $(2,0)$;
  • $y = 4 - 2x,\ y = 3 - x$: subtracting, $0 = 1 - x$, so $x = 1$ and $y = 2$: the point $(1,2)$.

The figure, which Robinson calls a phase diagram or phase portrait, shows that every solution with both species present tends to $(1,2)$ as $t \to +\infty$: the species coexist [p. 15; species reading, p. 326]. The vector notation $\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf f(\mathbf x, t)$ and the full theory of systems are the subject of Topic 4.

5. Modelling with Newton's second law: free fall and drag

5.1 From momentum balance to $F = ma$

Newton's second law states that the change $\Delta p$ in the momentum of a body equals the applied force $F$ times the time $\Delta t$ over which it acts, $\Delta p = F\,\Delta t$ [Robinson, Sec. 5.3, p. 30]. Dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ (Robinson notes this step is somewhat imprecise) gives

$$\frac{dp}{dt} = F(t).$$

With $p = mv$, the product rule gives

$$\frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(mv) = \frac{dm}{dt}\,v + m\,\frac{dv}{dt}.$$

For constant mass $dm/dt = 0$, so $m\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = F(t)$: the familiar $F = ma$. (For variable-mass bodies such as rockets, $F = d(mv)/dt$ does not apply: the momentum carried away by the exhaust must be included in a balance over the whole system, which is outside this course.)

5.2 Free fall under gravity

Setup. A body of mass $m$ is released from rest at height $h$ above the ground. Hypotheses: constant gravity $g$, no air resistance, motion along a vertical line. One degree of freedom: the height $x(t)$. The lecture derived the model from Sec. 5.3; the book itself leaves this case as Exercise 5.6 (an apple falling from height $h$).

SymbolMeaningSI unit
$x(t)$Height above the ground (axis pointing up)m
$v(t) = \dot x$Vertical velocity, positive upwardsm/s
$g$Gravitational acceleration, $\approx 9.81$m/s²
$h$Release heightm

Frame. Take the $x$ axis vertical, pointing up, with origin on the ground. The only force is the weight, which points down, so $F = -mg$.

Derivation. Newton's law gives the second-order linear IVP

$$m\,\ddot x = -mg, \qquad x(0) = h, \qquad \dot x(0) = 0.$$
  1. Divide by $m \neq 0$ and write $v = \dot x$: $\ \dot v = -g$. Integrate both sides from $0$ to $t$ (dummy variable $\tilde t$). An antiderivative of the constant $-g$ is $-g\tilde t$, so by Barrow's rule $$v(t) - v(0) = \int_0^t (-g)\,d\tilde t = \big[-g\tilde t\,\big]_{0}^{t} = -gt - (-g\cdot 0) = -gt.$$ With $v(0) = 0$: $\ v(t) = -gt$.
  2. Now $\dot x = -gt$. An antiderivative of $-g\tilde t$ is $-\tfrac12 g\tilde t^{\,2}$ (check: $\frac{d}{d\tilde t}(-\tfrac12 g\tilde t^2) = -g\tilde t$), so $$x(t) - x(0) = \int_0^t (-g\tilde t)\,d\tilde t = \Big[-\tfrac12 g\tilde t^{\,2}\Big]_0^t = -\tfrac12 g t^2 - 0.$$ With $x(0) = h$: $\ x(t) = h - \tfrac12 g t^2$.
  3. The impact time $t^*$ solves $x(t^*) = 0$: $\ h = \tfrac12 g t^{*2}$, so $t^* = \sqrt{2h/g}$ (the positive root, since $t^* > 0$). The impact velocity is $v(t^*) = -g\sqrt{2h/g} = -\sqrt{2gh}$.
Result and checks
$$x(t) = h - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad v(t) = -gt, \qquad t^* = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}, \qquad |v(t^*)| = \sqrt{2gh}.$$
  • Dimensions: $[g t^2] = \text{m s}^{-2}\cdot\text{s}^2 = \text{m}$ ✓; $[\sqrt{h/g}] = \sqrt{\text{m}/(\text{m s}^{-2})} = \text{s}$ ✓; $[\sqrt{gh}] = \sqrt{\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}} = \text{m/s}$ ✓.
  • Energy: $\tfrac12 m v(t^*)^2 = \tfrac12 m (2gh) = mgh$: the potential energy lost equals the kinetic energy gained ✓.
  • Mass independence: $m$ cancelled in step 1 — without drag every body falls the same way.
Example 5.4 (Robinson pp. 30–31; numbers from the caption of Fig. 5.6): a braking car — the same method, horizontally

A car of mass $m$ moving at $v_0$ brakes with a constant force $k$. Newton: $m\dot v = -k$. Integrating from $0$ to $t$, $v(t) - v_0 = \int_0^t (-k/m)\,d\tilde t = -kt/m$, so $v(t) = v_0 - kt/m$. It stops when $v(t_c) = 0$, i.e. $t_c = m v_0/k$. Integrating $\dot x = v_0 - kt/m$ from $0$ to $t_c$:

$$x(t_c) - x(0) = \Big[v_0\tilde t - \frac{k\tilde t^{\,2}}{2m}\Big]_0^{t_c} = v_0t_c - \frac{kt_c^2}{2m} = \frac{mv_0^2}{k} - \frac{mv_0^2}{2k} = \frac{mv_0^2}{2k}.$$

With Robinson's data $m = 1000\ \text{kg}$, $k = 6500\ \text{N}$, $v_0 = 30\ \text{mph} = 13.41\ \text{m/s}$: $t_c = 1000 \times 13.41/6500 = 2.06\ \text{s}$ and the stopping distance is $1000 \times 13.41^2/(2\times 6500) = 13.8\ \text{m}$ (about 45 ft, as in his Fig. 5.7). Dimensions: $[mv_0^2/k] = \text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-2}/(\text{kg m s}^{-2}) = \text{m}$ ✓.

5.3 Free fall with quadratic air drag: terminal velocity

At the speeds of a skydiver or a descending capsule, air resistance is not negligible. Robinson models it with a drag force proportional to the square of the speed and opposite to the motion [Sec. 7.5.2, Eq. (7.5), p. 52]:

$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - kv|v|, \tag{7.5}$$

where now $v$ is the downward velocity and $k > 0$ (units kg/m) collects air density, drag coefficient and frontal area. The term $v|v|$ (rather than $v^2$) makes the drag oppose the motion whatever its direction. For a body released from rest, $v \geq 0$ and the equation becomes

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = g - \frac{k}{m}v^2.$$

This equation is first order, autonomous and nonlinear ($v^2$). Its right-hand side vanishes at $v^* = \sqrt{mg/k}$, the terminal velocity: if the body ever reaches $v^*$, weight and drag balance and it stays there. Robinson's skydiver ($m = 100\ \text{kg}$, $k \approx 1/3\ \text{kg/m}$, $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$) has $v^* = \sqrt{100 \times 9.8 \times 3} \approx 54.2\ \text{m/s}$ [p. 53].

Explicit solution (Robinson Exercise 8.6, worked here in full)

Write $w_T = \sqrt{mg/k}$, so that $k/m = g/w_T^2$ and $\dot v = \dfrac{g}{w_T^2}\,(w_T^2 - v^2)$. For $0 \le v < w_T$ we may divide (separation of variables, justified in Topic 2):

$$\frac{dv}{w_T^2 - v^2} = \frac{g}{w_T^2}\,dt.$$
  1. Partial fractions: since $w_T^2 - v^2 = (w_T - v)(w_T + v)$, $$\frac{1}{w_T^2 - v^2} = \frac{1}{2w_T}\left(\frac{1}{w_T - v} + \frac{1}{w_T + v}\right).$$ Check: $\dfrac{(w_T + v) + (w_T - v)}{2w_T(w_T - v)(w_T + v)} = \dfrac{2w_T}{2w_T(w_T^2 - v^2)}$ ✓.
  2. Antiderivatives. For $\int \frac{dv}{w_T - v}$ substitute $u = w_T - v$, $du = -dv$: $\int \frac{-du}{u} = -\ln|u| = -\ln(w_T - v)$ (here $w_T - v > 0$). Directly, $\int\frac{dv}{w_T + v} = \ln(w_T + v)$. Hence $$\int\frac{dv}{w_T^2 - v^2} = \frac{1}{2w_T}\ln\frac{w_T + v}{w_T - v}.$$
  3. Barrow's rule between $v(0) = 0$ and $v(t)$ on the left, $0$ and $t$ on the right: $$\frac{1}{2w_T}\left[\ln\frac{w_T + v}{w_T - v}\right]_{0}^{v(t)} = \frac{1}{2w_T}\left(\ln\frac{w_T + v(t)}{w_T - v(t)} - \ln 1\right) = \frac{g}{w_T^2}\,\big[\tilde t\,\big]_0^t = \frac{g t}{w_T^2}.$$
  4. Multiply by $2w_T$ and exponentiate: $\dfrac{w_T + v}{w_T - v} = e^{2gt/w_T}$. Solve for $v$: $w_T + v = (w_T - v)e^{2gt/w_T}$, so $v\,(1 + e^{2gt/w_T}) = w_T\,(e^{2gt/w_T} - 1)$ and, dividing numerator and denominator by $e^{gt/w_T}$, $$v(t) = w_T\,\frac{e^{gt/w_T} - e^{-gt/w_T}}{e^{gt/w_T} + e^{-gt/w_T}} = w_T\tanh\!\left(\frac{g t}{w_T}\right).$$
Interpretation and limiting cases
  • Long times: $\tanh s \to 1$ as $s \to \infty$, so $v(t) \to w_T$: the body approaches terminal velocity but never exceeds it.
  • Short times: $\tanh s = s - s^3/3 + \dots$, so $v(t) \approx w_T \cdot gt/w_T = gt$: the drag-free result of 5.2 is recovered while $v \ll w_T$.
  • Vanishing drag: $k \to 0$ gives $w_T \to \infty$ and again $v \to gt$ for every fixed $t$ (this is the limit Robinson asks for in Ex. 8.6).
  • Time scale: $w_T/g$ is the time to reach about $76\%$ of $w_T$ ($\tanh 1 \approx 0.76$); for the skydiver $54.2/9.8 \approx 5.5\ \text{s}$.
  • Dimensions: $[k v^2] = \text{kg m}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2} = \text{N}$ ✓; $[\sqrt{mg/k}] = \sqrt{\text{kg m s}^{-2}/(\text{kg m}^{-1})} = \text{m/s}$ ✓; $[gt/w_T] = 1$ ✓.
Aerospace connection

In aerodynamic notation the drag is $D = \tfrac12\rho\,C_D A\,v^2$, so $k = \tfrac12\rho C_D A$ and $w_T = \sqrt{2mg/(\rho C_D A)}$. Terminal velocity grows as the air thins ($\rho$ decreases with altitude), which is why a re-entering capsule or a high-altitude jumper falls much faster in the upper atmosphere. That refinement makes $k$ depend on height and is beyond this topic.

6. Population models: Malthus and Verhulst

6.1 Malthus: exponential growth

Balance law. In a short time $\Delta t$, a population $p$ gains about $\beta p\,\Delta t$ births and loses about $\delta p\,\Delta t$ deaths, with constant per-capita rates $\beta$, $\delta$. Hence $\Delta p \approx (\beta - \delta)\,p\,\Delta t$; dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ gives the model proposed by Malthus in 1798 [Robinson, Sec. 8.3, Eq. (8.5), p. 62]:

$$\frac{dp}{dt} = kp, \qquad k = \beta - \delta > 0. \tag{8.5}$$

This is the linear equation $\dot x = \lambda x$ of Sec. 8.2, which Robinson calls "absolutely fundamental". We solve it once in full, with $x(t_0) = x_0$ [Sec. 8.2, pp. 61–62].

  1. If $x_0 = 0$ then $x(t) \equiv 0$ is a solution, and by uniqueness (Section 8) the only one.
  2. If $x_0 \neq 0$, then $x(t) \neq 0$ for all $t$ (again by uniqueness), so we may divide by $x$: $\ \dfrac{1}{x}\dfrac{dx}{dt} = \lambda$.
  3. The left side is $\dfrac{d}{dt}\ln|x(t)|$ by the chain rule ($\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = 1/x$ for $x \neq 0$). Integrate from $t_0$ to $t$: $$\Big[\ln|x|\Big]_{x_0}^{x(t)} = \int_{t_0}^{t}\lambda\,d\tilde t = \lambda\,(t - t_0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln|x(t)| - \ln|x_0| = \lambda(t - t_0).$$
  4. Exponentiate: $\ |x(t)|/|x_0| = e^{\lambda(t - t_0)}$. A solution cannot cross $x = 0$ (step 2), so $x(t)$ and $x_0$ have the same sign and the moduli can be removed.
$$x(t) = x_0\,e^{\lambda(t - t_0)}, \qquad\text{so for Malthus}\qquad p(t) = p(t_0)\,e^{k(t - t_0)}.$$

Doubling time. $p$ doubles after $d$ with $2 = e^{kd}$, i.e. $d = \ln 2/k$, independent of the starting population. (For $\lambda < 0$ the same argument gives the half-life $\ln 2/|\lambda|$ of radioactive decay.)

Census test of the Malthus model (Robinson Sec. 8.3, pp. 63–64)

Population of Great Britain and Ireland: 1801: 16 345 646; 1851: 27 533 755; 1901: 41 609 091 (Eq. 8.6).

Fitting $k$ from 1801 and 1851, $p(1851) = p(1801)e^{50k}$:

$$k = \frac{\ln p(1851) - \ln p(1801)}{50} = \frac{\ln(27\,533\,755/16\,345\,646)}{50} = \frac{0.52145}{50} = 0.01043\ \text{yr}^{-1}.$$
  • Prediction for 1901: $p(1801)e^{100k} = 46.4$ million (true: 41.6 million) — reasonable.
  • Prediction for 2001: $p(1801)e^{200k} = 131.6$ million (true: about 63 million, UK plus Republic of Ireland) — more than twice too large.
  • Doubling time: $\ln 2/0.01043 = 66.5$ years. Robinson rounds $k$ to $0.010$ first and quotes 69 years; both describe the same fit.

6.2 Verhulst: logistic growth

Unbounded exponential growth ignores limited resources. The logistic equation keeps the Malthus rate for small populations but reduces it as $p$ approaches a maximum sustainable population $M$ [Sec. 8.5, Eq. (8.12), p. 66]:

$$\frac{dp}{dt} = kp\left(1 - \frac{p}{M}\right), \qquad k > 0,\ M > 0. \tag{8.12}$$
  • For $p \ll M$, $p^2/M$ is negligible and $\dot p \approx kp$: Malthus is recovered, so $k$ is the growth rate of small populations.
  • For $0 < p < M$, $\dot p > 0$: the population grows. For $p > M$, $\dot p < 0$: it shrinks.
  • The equation is first order, autonomous and nonlinear (the term $-kp^2/M$).

The equilibria are the zeros of $kp(1 - p/M)$: $p = 0$ and $p = M$. The phase-line analysis of Topic 2 shows that $p = 0$ is unstable and $p = M$ is stable, so every positive population eventually settles at $M$.

Explicit solution (Robinson pp. 66–67), every step shown

  1. Separate the variables (valid while $p \neq 0, M$, which by uniqueness then holds for all $t$), multiplying top and bottom by $M$: $$\frac{M}{p\,(M - p)}\,dp = k\,dt.$$
  2. Partial fractions: we want $\dfrac{M}{p(M - p)} = \dfrac{\alpha}{p} + \dfrac{\beta}{M - p}$, i.e. $M = \alpha(M - p) + \beta p$ for all $p$. Setting $p = 0$ gives $\alpha = 1$; setting $p = M$ gives $\beta = 1$. So (Eq. 8.13) $$\left(\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{M - p}\right)dp = k\,dt.$$
  3. Antiderivatives: $\int dp/p = \ln|p|$; for the second, substitute $u = M - p$, $du = -dp$, so $\int\frac{dp}{M - p} = -\int\frac{du}{u} = -\ln|M - p|$. Hence $\int\left(\frac1p + \frac{1}{M-p}\right)dp = \ln|p| - \ln|M - p|$.
  4. Barrow's rule between $t_0$ and $t$: $$\Big[\ln|p| - \ln|M - p|\Big]_{p(t_0)}^{p(t)} = \Big[k\tilde t\,\Big]_{t_0}^{t} \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln\frac{p(t)\,|M - p(t_0)|}{|M - p(t)|\,p(t_0)} = k(t - t_0).$$ ($p > 0$, so $|p| = p$.)
  5. $M - p(t)$ never changes sign (a solution cannot cross the equilibrium $p = M$), so the moduli cancel. Exponentiating: $$\frac{p(t)\,\big(M - p(t_0)\big)}{\big(M - p(t)\big)\,p(t_0)} = e^{k(t - t_0)}.$$
  6. Solve for $p(t)$. Write $E = e^{k(t-t_0)}$ and $p_0 = p(t_0)$: $\ p\,(M - p_0) = (M - p)\,p_0E$, so $p\,(M - p_0 + p_0E) = Mp_0E$ and
Logistic solution
$$p(t) = M\,\frac{p_0\,e^{k(t - t_0)}}{M - p_0 + p_0\,e^{k(t - t_0)}}.$$
  • Check at $t = t_0$: $E = 1$, $p = Mp_0/M = p_0$ ✓.
  • Long time: divide top and bottom by $E$: $p = Mp_0/\big((M - p_0)e^{-k(t-t_0)} + p_0\big) \to Mp_0/p_0 = M$ ✓.
  • Small population: if $p_0E \ll M$, the denominator is $\approx M$ and $p \approx p_0e^{k(t-t_0)}$ (Malthus) ✓.
  • Inflection: $\ddot p = \frac{d}{dt}\big[kp - kp^2/M\big] = k\dot p\,(1 - 2p/M)$ by the chain rule, so the growth is fastest at $p = M/2$.
Census fit of the logistic model (Robinson pp. 67–68)

With $\alpha = e^{50k}$, $p_0 = p(1801)$, $p_1 = p(1851)$, $p_2 = p(1901)$, the solution requires $p_1 = \dfrac{Mp_0\alpha}{M - p_0 + p_0\alpha}$ and $p_2 = \dfrac{Mp_0\alpha^2}{M - p_0 + p_0\alpha^2}$. Eliminating $M$ gives

$$\alpha = \frac{p_2(p_1 - p_0)}{p_0(p_2 - p_1)} = 2.0234, \qquad M = \frac{p_1(2p_0p_2 - p_1p_2 - p_0p_1)}{p_0p_2 - p_1^2} = 83.1\ \text{million}, \tag{8.14}$$

so $k = \ln(2.0234)/50 = 0.0141\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. The model then predicts $66.8$ million for 2001, against about $63$ million observed — far better than Malthus' $131.6$ million (values recomputed from the census data; they agree with Robinson's).

7. Initial value problems and well-posedness

A differential equation alone has many solutions: $\dot x = \lambda x$ is solved by $Ae^{\lambda t}$ for every constant $A$. One extra piece of information, the state at one instant, selects one of them.

Definition — initial value problem (IVP) [Robinson, Sec. 6.1, Eq. (6.2), p. 38]

An initial value problem is a differential equation together with an initial condition:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x, t), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0. \tag{6.2}$$
Definition 6.1 — solution of an IVP [Robinson, p. 40]

Given an open interval $I$ that contains $t_0$, a solution of (6.2) on $I$ is a differentiable function $x(t)$ defined on $I$ with $x(t_0) = x_0$ and $\dot x(t) = f(x(t), t)$ for all $t \in I$.

The definition deliberately allows a solution to live on a limited interval $I$ rather than on all of $\mathbb R$. Section 9 shows that this is unavoidable even for very simple equations.

7.1 The philosophy of a well-posed problem

Lecture terminology (Sep 10) — Hadamard well-posedness

A mathematical model is called well posed if

  1. a solution exists (otherwise the model predicts nothing);
  2. the solution is unique (otherwise the model cannot decide between futures);
  3. the solution depends continuously on the data (a small error in the measured $x_0$ produces a small error in $x(t)$, at least over finite times).

The words "well-posed" and "Picard's theorem" are lecture terminology; Robinson never uses them. He discusses existence and uniqueness in Sec. 6.1 and states the result as Theorem 6.2 (lecture map, notes on differences).

Robinson gives the physical reading of uniqueness: if $t$ is time and the initial condition is specified "now", a unique solution means the equation can be used to predict the future; uniqueness is equivalent to the model being deterministic [p. 39]. It also legitimises guessing: if a guessed function satisfies the IVP, it is the solution.

7.2 Existence is not automatic

An IVP with no solution (Robinson p. 38)
$$x^2 + t^2\,\frac{dx}{dt} = 0, \qquad x(0) = c, \quad c \neq 0.$$

Evaluate the equation at $t = 0$: the second term vanishes and $x(0)^2 = 0$, i.e. $x(0) = 0$. This contradicts $x(0) = c \neq 0$, so no differentiable solution exists. The culprit is visible in the explicit form $\dot x = -x^2/t^2$: the right-hand side is not even defined at $t = 0$.

8. Picard's existence and uniqueness theorem

Theorem 6.2 (Picard's theorem in the lecture) [Robinson, p. 40]

If $f(x, t)$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, t)$ are continuous for $a < x < b$ and $c < t < d$, then for any $x_0 \in (a, b)$ and $t_0 \in (c, d)$ the initial value problem

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x, t), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0$$

has a unique solution on some open interval $I$ containing $t_0$.

Three features deserve attention:

  • The hypothesis is easy to check: continuity of $f$ and of one partial derivative, in a rectangle around $(x_0, t_0)$.
  • The derivative is with respect to the unknown $x$, not $t$. Nothing is required of $\partial f/\partial t$.
  • The conclusion is local: it says nothing about how large $I$ is (Robinson Fig. 6.2). Section 9 shows that $I$ can indeed be short.

8.1 The Lipschitz condition

Definition — Lipschitz continuity [Robinson, footnote to Thm 6.2, Eq. (6.5), p. 40]

$f$ is Lipschitz continuous in $x$ if there is a constant $L$ such that

$$|f(x, t) - f(y, t)| \leq L\,|x - y| \tag{6.5}$$

for all $x, y$ in the region considered. Robinson notes that this weaker hypothesis already suffices for Theorem 6.2.

Proposition — a bounded derivative implies Lipschitz [Robinson, Exercise 6.2, p. 44]

If $f$ is differentiable in $x$ with $\left|\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right| \leq L$ for $a \le x \le b$, then $|f(x,t) - f(y,t)| \le L|x - y|$ there.

Proof (Mean Value Theorem)

Fix $t$ and $a \le y < x \le b$. By the Mean Value Theorem there is $\xi \in (y, x)$ with $f(x,t) - f(y,t) = (x - y)\,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\xi, t)$. Taking absolute values and using the bound, $|f(x,t) - f(y,t)| = |x - y|\,\left|\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(\xi,t)\right| \le L\,|x - y|$. The case $x = y$ is trivial, and $x < y$ follows by symmetry.

A continuous function on a closed bounded rectangle is bounded, so the hypothesis of Theorem 6.2 gives $|\partial f/\partial x| \le L$ on any closed sub-rectangle, and hence a Lipschitz bound. The converse is false: $f(x) = |x|$ is Lipschitz with $L = 1$ (because $\big||x| - |y|\big| \le |x - y|$) but not differentiable at $0$.

8.2 Proof of uniqueness — and of continuous dependence

Robinson leaves the proofs as guided exercises. The uniqueness argument (Exercise 6.3) is short, and the same estimate also delivers the third Hadamard condition.

Proposition — stability estimate

Suppose $|f(x,t) - f(y,t)| \le L|x - y|$ and let $x(t)$, $y(t)$ solve $\dot x = f(x, t)$ with $x(t_0) = x_0$, $y(t_0) = y_0$. Then for $t \ge t_0$

$$|x(t) - y(t)| \le |x_0 - y_0|\,e^{L(t - t_0)}.$$

In particular, if $x_0 = y_0$ then $x(t) = y(t)$: the solution is unique.

Proof (Robinson Exercises 6.3 and 9.7)
  1. Let $z(t) = x(t) - y(t)$. Subtracting the two equations: $\dot z = f(x(t),t) - f(y(t),t)$.
  2. Let $Z(t) = z(t)^2 = |z|^2$. By the chain rule with outer function $u \mapsto u^2$: $$\frac{dZ}{dt} = 2z\,\frac{dz}{dt} = 2z\,\big[f(x,t) - f(y,t)\big].$$
  3. Bound it with the Lipschitz condition: $\ \dfrac{dZ}{dt} \le 2|z|\,|f(x,t) - f(y,t)| \le 2|z|\cdot L|z| = 2L\,Z$.
  4. Multiply by the positive factor $e^{-2Lt}$ and use the product rule: $$\frac{d}{dt}\Big(e^{-2Lt}Z\Big) = e^{-2Lt}\dot Z - 2Le^{-2Lt}Z = e^{-2Lt}\big(\dot Z - 2LZ\big) \le 0.$$
  5. A function with non-positive derivative is non-increasing, so for $t \ge t_0$: $e^{-2Lt}Z(t) \le e^{-2Lt_0}Z(t_0)$, i.e. $Z(t) \le Z(t_0)\,e^{2L(t - t_0)}$. Taking square roots: $|z(t)| \le |z(t_0)|\,e^{L(t - t_0)}$.
  6. If $x_0 = y_0$ then $z(t_0) = 0$, so $|z(t)| \le 0$ and $x(t) = y(t)$ for all $t \ge t_0$.

Interpretation. Two solutions that start $\varepsilon$ apart remain within $\varepsilon e^{L(t-t_0)}$ of each other: the error can grow, but at most exponentially, and it tends to zero with $\varepsilon$ on any finite time interval. This is exactly continuous dependence on the initial data. For $\dot x = \lambda x$ with $\lambda \ge 0$ (so $L = \lambda$) the bound is attained: $|x(t) - y(t)| = |x_0 - y_0|e^{\lambda(t-t_0)}$; for $\lambda < 0$ the solutions actually approach each other.

8.3 Idea of the existence proof: Picard iteration

Existence is harder than uniqueness. Robinson's Exercise 6.4 sketches it for $\dot x = f(x)$, $x(0) = x_0$ [pp. 44–45].

  1. Integral form. Integrating the ODE from $0$ to $t$ (Barrow: $\int_0^t \dot x\,d\tilde t = x(t) - x(0)$) gives the equivalent integral equation $$x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f\big(x(\tilde t)\big)\,d\tilde t. \tag{E6.5}$$ Conversely, differentiating (E6.5) with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus returns $\dot x = f(x)$, and $t = 0$ returns $x(0) = x_0$.
  2. Iteration. Start from the constant guess $x_0(t) = x_0$ and improve it repeatedly: $$x_{n+1}(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f\big(x_n(\tilde t)\big)\,d\tilde t. \tag{E6.6}$$
  3. Contraction. Using (E6.4) $|f(x) - f(y)| \le L|x - y|$, for $0 \le t \le 1/(2L)$: $$|x_{n+1}(t) - x_n(t)| \le \int_0^t L\,|x_n - x_{n-1}|\,d\tilde t \le L\,t\,\max|x_n - x_{n-1}| \le \tfrac12\max|x_n - x_{n-1}|. \tag{E6.7}$$ Each step halves the maximum difference, so the differences shrink like $2^{-n}$ and $x_n(t)$ converges to some $x_\infty(t)$.
  4. Limit. Letting $n \to \infty$ in (E6.6) shows that $x_\infty$ satisfies (E6.5), so it solves the IVP; the previous proposition shows it is the only one.
Illustration: Picard iterates for $\dot x = x$, $x(0) = 1$

Here $f(x) = x$ and $L = 1$. Each iterate integrates the previous one:

$$x_1 = 1 + \int_0^t 1\,d\tilde t = 1 + t, \qquad x_2 = 1 + \int_0^t (1 + \tilde t)\,d\tilde t = 1 + \Big[\tilde t + \tfrac{\tilde t^2}{2}\Big]_0^t = 1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2},$$ $$x_3 = 1 + \Big[\tilde t + \tfrac{\tilde t^2}{2} + \tfrac{\tilde t^3}{6}\Big]_0^t = 1 + t + \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{t^3}{6}, \qquad\dots,\qquad x_n = \sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{t^j}{j!} \;\longrightarrow\; e^t.$$

9. When the theorem fails or stops short

9.1 Loss of uniqueness: $\dot x = \sqrt{x}$

Robinson's example [Eq. (6.3), p. 39]:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \sqrt{x}, \qquad x(0) = 0. \tag{6.3}$$

The obvious solution is $x(t) \equiv 0$. But for any $c > 0$ the function $$x_c(t) = \begin{cases} 0, & t \le c,\\[2pt] \dfrac{(t - c)^2}{4}, & t > c\end{cases}$$ is also a solution. Check for $t > c$ (chain rule with inner function $t - c$): $\dot x_c = \dfrac{2(t - c)}{4}\cdot 1 = \dfrac{t - c}{2}$, while $\sqrt{x_c} = \sqrt{(t-c)^2/4} = \dfrac{|t - c|}{2} = \dfrac{t - c}{2}$ ✓. At $t = c$ both one-sided derivatives are $0$, so $x_c$ is differentiable there. The solution "waits" at $0$ and then "decides" at the arbitrary time $c$ to leave [p. 39]: the model is not deterministic.

Why Theorem 6.2 does not apply. $f(x) = x^{1/2}$ is continuous, but $f'(x) = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} \to \infty$ as $x \downarrow 0$, so $\partial f/\partial x$ is not continuous at the initial point $x_0 = 0$ [p. 40; Fig. 6.3, p. 41]. Nor is $f$ Lipschitz near $0$: $\dfrac{|f(x) - f(0)|}{|x - 0|} = \dfrac{\sqrt x}{x} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt x} \to \infty$ as $x \downarrow 0$, so no constant $L$ works. Note the logic: the theorem gives a sufficient condition; its failure here is consistent with the non-uniqueness, not a proof of it — the explicit family $x_c$ is the proof.

The same phenomenon for $\dot x = x^{1/3}$ (Robinson Exercise 6.1 (iii))

With $x(0) = 0$, $x \equiv 0$ is a solution. Separating the variables for $x > 0$: $x^{-1/3}dx = dt$. An antiderivative of $x^{-1/3}$ is $\tfrac32 x^{2/3}$, so integrating from the time $c$ at which $x$ leaves $0$:

$$\Big[\tfrac32 x^{2/3}\Big]_{0}^{x(t)} = \big[\tilde t\,\big]_c^t \;\Longrightarrow\; \tfrac32 x(t)^{2/3} = t - c \;\Longrightarrow\; x(t) = \left(\frac{2(t - c)}{3}\right)^{3/2},\quad t \ge c.$$

Check: $\dot x = \tfrac32\left(\tfrac{2(t-c)}{3}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\tfrac23 = \left(\tfrac{2(t-c)}{3}\right)^{1/2} = x^{1/3}$ ✓. Again infinitely many solutions (and their mirror images $-x$), because $f'(x) = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3}$ blows up at $x = 0$.

9.2 Finite-time blow-up: $\dot x = x^2$

Robinson's second cautionary example [Sec. 6.3, Eq. (6.6), p. 41]:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = x^2, \qquad x(0) = x_0. \tag{6.6}$$

Here $f(x) = x^2$ and $f'(x) = 2x$ are continuous everywhere, so Theorem 6.2 guarantees a unique solution near $t = 0$ for every $x_0$. For $x_0 \neq 0$ the solution is [Eq. (6.7), p. 42]

$$x(t) = \frac{1}{x_0^{-1} - t}. \tag{6.7}$$

Verification. At $t = 0$, $x = 1/x_0^{-1} = x_0$ ✓. Write $x = u^{-1}$ with $u(t) = x_0^{-1} - t$, $du/dt = -1$. Chain rule: $$\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{d}{du}\big(u^{-1}\big)\cdot\frac{du}{dt} = \big(-u^{-2}\big)\cdot(-1) = \frac{1}{(x_0^{-1} - t)^2} = x(t)^2\ ✓.$$ By uniqueness this is the solution. (Topic 2 derives it by separation of variables, Robinson Example 8.1.)

  • $x_0 > 0$: the denominator $x_0^{-1} - t$ decreases to $0$ at $t^* = 1/x_0$, so $x(t) \to +\infty$ as $t \uparrow 1/x_0$: the solution blows up in finite time. Backwards in time it decays to $0$. Maximal interval of existence: $(-\infty, 1/x_0)$.
  • $x_0 < 0$: the blow-up happens in the past, $x \to -\infty$ as $t \downarrow 1/x_0 < 0$. Maximal interval: $(1/x_0, +\infty)$.
  • $x_0 = 0$: $x \equiv 0$, defined for all $t \in \mathbb R$.
Why the interval in Theorem 6.2 must be "some interval"

$f(x) = x^2$ is as smooth as a function can be, yet solutions do not exist for all time. The reason is that $f$ is Lipschitz on every bounded interval but not on all of $\mathbb R$: $|x^2 - y^2| = |x + y|\,|x - y|$ and $|x + y|$ is unbounded. Fast growth ($\dot x \sim x^2$) feeds on itself. Compare $\dot x = |x|$, whose solutions $x_0e^{t}$ (for $x_0 > 0$) exist forever although its phase line looks identical (Robinson Sec. 7.5.3).

A one-million-dollar open problem (Robinson Sec. 6.4, pp. 42–43)

It is still not known whether the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations have unique solutions that exist for all positive times. Every CFD computation of a wing or an engine assumes they do, yet one cannot prove that a numerical approximation is close to the "true solution" without knowing that such a solution exists. The question is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems: prove global existence and uniqueness, or find one initial condition for which the solution breaks down.
