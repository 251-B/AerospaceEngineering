Section 01

1. Definition and Classification of Differential Equations

In physical and engineering sciences, fundamental balance laws—such as Newton's second law of motion, Fourier's conduction law, or the Navier-Stokes momentum equations—relate physical quantities to their rates of change. Mathematically, these relations are formulated as differential equations: equations containing an unknown function (the dependent variable) and one or more of its derivatives.

1.1 Ordinary vs. Partial Differential Equations

The primary bifurcation in differential equation theory concerns the number of independent variables upon which the unknown function depends:

  • Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs): The unknown dependent variable $y = y(x)$ or $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}(t)$ depends on a single independent variable. Consequently, all derivatives appearing in the equation are ordinary derivatives: $$ F\left(x, y(x), \frac{dy}{dx}, \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}, \dots, \frac{d^n y}{dx^n}\right) = 0 $$ Aerospace examples: Trajectory of a spacecraft as a function of time $t$, altitude and velocity of a launch vehicle during boost phase, or temperature relaxation of a turbine blade.
  • Partial Differential Equations (PDEs): The unknown function $u = u(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k)$ depends on two or more independent variables (e.g., spatial coordinates $(x, y, z)$ and time $t$). The equation contains partial derivatives: $$ F\left(x, t, u, \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}, \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x \partial t}, \dots\right) = 0 $$ Aerospace examples: Pressure field over an airfoil $p(x, y, z, t)$ (Navier-Stokes), temperature distribution inside a re-entry heat shield $T(x, y, z, t)$ (Heat Equation), or acoustic wave propagation in a turbofan intake (Wave Equation).

1.2 Order of a Differential Equation

The order of an ODE or PDE is defined as the order of the highest derivative occurring in the equation.

  • First-order ODE: $\frac{dy}{dt} = -k y$ (Radioactive decay, order 1).
  • Second-order ODE: $m \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} + c \frac{dx}{dt} + k x = F(t)$ (Damped harmonic oscillator, order 2).
  • Fourth-order ODE: $E I \frac{d^4 w}{dx^4} = q(x)$ (Euler-Bernoulli beam deflection under aerodynamic load, order 4).
State Space Equivalence

Any $n$-th order scalar ODE $y^{(n)} = F(t, y, y', \dots, y^{(n-1)})$ can always be reformulated as an equivalent system of $n$ coupled first-order ODEs by defining state variables: $$ x_1 = y, \quad x_2 = y', \quad x_3 = y'', \quad \dots, \quad x_n = y^{(n-1)} $$ yielding the first-order vector system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$.

1.3 Autonomous vs. Non-Autonomous ODEs

A first-order system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$ is classified according to explicit time dependence:

  • Autonomous ODE: The vector field does not depend explicitly on the independent variable (time $t$): $$ \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) $$ The velocity vector at any point in state space is time-invariant. Physical trajectories with identical initial states are simply time-shifted copies of each other. Examples include conservative mechanical oscillators and unforced population dynamics.
  • Non-Autonomous ODE: The vector field depends explicitly on time: $$ \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x}) $$ Trajectories passing through the same spatial point at different times experience different velocity vectors. Examples include systems driven by time-dependent external forces (e.g., gust response on a wing, periodic engine excitation).

2. The Official UC3M Linearity Criterion

Linearity is the single most consequential structural property of differential equations. It governs whether the principle of linear superposition holds, permitting the construction of general solutions from linear combinations of basis functions, Fourier series, and Green's functions.

Official Department Definition (definition_linear_ODE.pdf)

An $n$-th order ordinary differential equation is linear if and only if it can be written in the form:

$$ a_n(x) y^{(n)}(x) + a_{n-1}(x) y^{(n-1)}(x) + \dots + a_1(x) y'(x) + a_0(x) y(x) = b(x) \tag{2.1} $$

where $a_0(x), a_1(x), \dots, a_n(x)$ and $b(x)$ are specified functions defined on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, with $a_n(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \in I$. If an equation cannot be brought into this form, it is nonlinear.

2.1 The Linear Differential Operator & Superposition

Define the differential operator $L$ acting on smooth functions $y \in C^n(I)$:

$$ L[y] \equiv \sum_{k=0}^n a_k(x) \frac{d^k y}{dx^k}(x) $$

The operator $L$ is a linear operator on the vector space of differentiable functions because for any scalars $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ and functions $y_1, y_2 \in C^n(I)$:

$$ L[c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2] = c_1 L[y_1] + c_2 L[y_2] \tag{2.2} $$

Consequently:

  • Homogeneous Equation ($b(x) \equiv 0$): $L[y] = 0$. The solutions form a vector subspace of dimension $n$. If $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are solutions, then any linear combination $c_1 y_1(x) + c_2 y_2(x)$ is also an exact solution.
  • Inhomogeneous Equation ($b(x) \not\equiv 0$): The general solution is $y(x) = y_h(x) + y_p(x)$, where $y_h$ is the general solution of the associated homogeneous problem ($L[y_h] = 0$) and $y_p$ is any particular solution ($L[y_p] = b(x)$).

Exam Warning: Dependent vs. Independent Variable Pitfall

A chronic source of error in aerospace mathematics examinations is confusing nonlinear functions of the independent variable with nonlinearities in the dependent variable.

  • Permissible in Linear ODEs: Terms like $\sin(t) \cdot y$, $e^{x^2} \cdot y''$, or $\frac{1}{x} \cdot y'$ do not violate linearity. They are simply variable coefficient functions $a_k(x)$.
  • Prohibited in Linear ODEs: Terms like $\sin(y)$, $e^{y'}$, $\sqrt{y}$, $(y')^3$, or $y \cdot y'$. Any nonlinear operation on the unknown function or its derivatives renders the equation nonlinear.

3. Fundamental First-Order Physical Models

The universal foundation of physical modeling across continuum mechanics and aerospace thermodynamics is the Macroscopic Conservation Law over a control volume $V$:

$$ \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = \dot{\Phi}_{\text{in}} - \dot{\Phi}_{\text{out}} + \dot{\Phi}_{\text{generation}} - \dot{\Phi}_{\text{consumption}} \tag{3.1} $$

Depending on whether $\Phi$ represents scalar population count, radioactive nuclei, thermal enthalpy, or chemical solute, equation (3.1) yields the canonical foundational first-order linear models.

3.1 Malthusian Population Dynamics

Thomas Malthus (1798) postulated that under unlimited environmental resources, the rate of increase of a population $x(t)$ is directly proportional to the current population:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = k x(t), \quad x(0) = x_0 > 0 \tag{3.2} $$

Separating variables:

$$ \int_{x_0}^{x(t)} \frac{d\xi}{\xi} = \int_0^t k \, ds \implies \ln\left(\frac{x(t)}{x_0}\right) = k t \implies \mathbf{x(t) = x_0 e^{kt}} $$
Doubling Time Derivation

The doubling time $t_d$ is the time elapsed when $x(t_d) = 2x_0$:

$$ x_0 e^{k t_d} = 2 x_0 \implies e^{k t_d} = 2 \implies k t_d = \ln 2 \implies \mathbf{t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k}} \tag{3.3} $$

Asymptotic Regimes: (1) $k > 0 \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = +\infty$ (Malthusian explosion); (2) $k = 0 \implies x(t) \equiv x_0$ (Static equilibrium); (3) $k < 0 \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 0$ (Exponential extinction).

3.2 Radioactive Isotope Decay & Half-Life Deduction

Radioactive disintegration is a quantum stochastic Poisson process. Macroscopically, for a sample of $N(t)$ unstable isotopes, the disintegration rate is strictly proportional to the number of active nuclei:

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -k N(t), \quad k > 0 \tag{3.4} $$

where $k$ is the radioactive decay constant $[\text{s}^{-1}]$ or $[\text{yr}^{-1}]$. Integrating from $N(0) = N_0$:

$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-kt} \tag{3.5} $$
Deduction of the Half-Life Formula

The half-life $t_{1/2}$ is the time required for half of the initial isotopes to disintegrate:

$$ N(t_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{2} N_0 \implies N_0 e^{-k t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} N_0 \implies e^{-k t_{1/2}} = 2^{-1} $$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$ -k t_{1/2} = -\ln 2 \implies \mathbf{t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}} \iff \mathbf{k = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}} \tag{3.6} $$

Aerospace Application (ProblemsCh1 - Ex. 1.3): Plutonium-239 ($^{239}\text{Pu}$), used in Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs) for deep space probes (e.g., Voyager, Cassini, Curiosity), has $t_{1/2} \approx 24{,}000\text{ years}$. $$ k = \frac{\ln 2}{24{,}000\text{ yr}} \approx 2.8881 \times 10^{-5}\text{ yr}^{-1} \approx 9.158 \times 10^{-13}\text{ s}^{-1} $$

3.3 Newton's Law of Cooling

For a body of mass $m$, surface area $A_s$, and specific heat capacity $c_p$, the first law of thermodynamics states that the rate of internal thermal energy loss equals the convective heat loss to the ambient medium at temperature $T_A(t)$:

$$ m c_p \frac{dT}{dt} = -h A_s (T(t) - T_A(t)) $$

Defining the lumped thermal relaxation constant $k \equiv \frac{h A_s}{m c_p} > 0$ $[\text{s}^{-1}]$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dT}{dt} = -k \left( T(t) - T_A(t) \right)} \tag{3.7} $$

For a constant ambient temperature $T_A$, this is a first-order linear ODE with solution:

$$ T(t) = T_A + (T_0 - T_A) e^{-kt} \tag{3.8} $$

As $t \to \infty$, $T(t) \to T_A$, establishing thermal equilibrium asymptotically with characteristic relaxation timescale $\tau = 1/k$.

3.4 Mass Balance in CSTR Saline Mixing Tanks

Consider a Continuous Stirred-Tank Reactor (CSTR) containing an initial volume $V_0$ of brine with dissolved salt mass $x(t)$. Fluid flows in at volumetric rate $Q_{\text{in}}$ with salt concentration $C_{\text{in}}$, and the well-stirred mixture flows out at rate $Q_{\text{out}}$ with concentration $C_{\text{out}}(t) = \frac{x(t)}{V(t)}$.

Applying conservation of species mass $\frac{dx}{dt} = \dot{m}_{\text{in}} - \dot{m}_{\text{out}}$:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = Q_{\text{in}} C_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}} \frac{x(t)}{V(t)} \tag{3.9} $$

When flow rates match ($Q_{\text{in}} = Q_{\text{out}} = Q$), the liquid volume is constant ($V(t) \equiv V_0$):

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{Q}{V_0} x(t) = Q C_{\text{in}} \tag{3.10} $$
  • Pure Washout Regime ($C_{\text{in}} = 0$, ProblemsCh1 - Ex. 1.6): With $V_0 = 100\text{ L}$, $Q = 1\text{ L/min}$, and $x_0 = 1\text{ kg}$: $$ \frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{x}{100} \implies x(t) = e^{-t/100}\text{ kg} \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 0 $$
  • Constant Saline Inflow ($C_{\text{in}} = s\text{ kg/L}$): Using integrating factor $\mu(t) = e^{t/100}$: $$ x(t) = 100 s + (1 - 100 s) e^{-t/100}\text{ kg} \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 100 s\text{ kg} $$

3.5 Kinematics of Free Fall Motion under Gravity

A point mass $m$ released from rest in a uniform gravitational field $\vec{g} = -g \hat{j}$ with zero aerodynamic resistance obeys Newton's 2nd law:

$$ m \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -m g \implies \mathbf{\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -g} \tag{3.11} $$

Expressing as a two-stage successive integration from $t = 0$ with $y(0) = y_0$ and $y'(0) = v_0 = 0$:

$$ \int_0^t \frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{dy}{ds}\right) ds = \int_0^t -g \, ds \implies v(t) = -gt $$ $$ \int_0^t \frac{dy}{ds} \, ds = \int_0^t -gs \, ds \implies \mathbf{y(t) = y_0 - \frac{1}{2} g t^2} \tag{3.12} $$

4. Non-Linear Modeling: The Verhulst Logistic Equation

In physical systems, unconstrained exponential growth cannot continue indefinitely. Environmental resistance—such as finite space, nutrient depletion, or aerodynamic drag—acts as a self-limiting negative feedback mechanism.

In 1838, Pierre-François Verhulst modified Malthus' linear model by stipulating that the per-capita growth rate $\frac{1}{p}\frac{dp}{dt}$ decreases linearly with population density until reaching the carrying capacity $M$:

$$ \frac{1}{p} \frac{dp}{dt} = k \left( 1 - \frac{p}{M} \right) \iff \mathbf{\frac{dp}{dt} = k p \left( 1 - \frac{p}{M} \right)} \tag{4.1} $$

4.1 Phase-Line Qualitative Dynamics

Equation (4.1) is an autonomous, first-order, nonlinear ODE. The equilibrium states satisfy $f(p) = 0$:

$$ f(p) = kp - \frac{k}{M} p^2 = 0 \implies p^*_1 = 0, \quad p^*_2 = M $$

Linearizing the vector field by computing $f'(p) = k - \frac{2k}{M}p$:

  • At $p^*_1 = 0$ (Extinction): $f'(0) = k > 0 \implies$ Unstable Node (Repellor). Any small initial population grows away from zero.
  • At $p^*_2 = M$ (Carrying Capacity): $f'(M) = k - 2k = -k < 0 \implies$ Asymptotically Stable Node (Attractor). All positive trajectories converge to $M$.

4.2 Rigorous Analytical Solution via Partial Fractions

Let $p(t_0) = p_0 > 0$ with $p_0 \neq M$. Let us integrate without skipping any algebraic steps:

Step 1: Separation of Variables:

$$ \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{k}{M} p (M - p) \implies \frac{dp}{p(M - p)} = \frac{k}{M} \, dt $$

Step 2: Partial Fraction Decomposition:

$$ \frac{1}{p(M - p)} = \frac{A}{p} + \frac{B}{M - p} \implies 1 = A(M - p) + B p $$

Setting $p = 0 \implies A M = 1 \implies A = \frac{1}{M}$.
Setting $p = M \implies B M = 1 \implies B = \frac{1}{M}$.
Substituting back and multiplying by $M$:

$$ \left( \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{M - p} \right) dp = k \, dt $$

Step 3: Definite Integration:

$$ \int_{p_0}^p \left( \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{M - u} \right) du = \int_{t_0}^t k \, ds $$

Noting that $\int \frac{1}{M-u} du = -\ln|M-u|$ due to the negative derivative of the inner function:

$$ \left[ \ln|u| - \ln|M - u| \right]_{p_0}^p = k(t - t_0) \implies \left[ \ln\left| \frac{u}{M - u} \right| \right]_{p_0}^p = k(t - t_0) $$ $$ \ln\left| \frac{p(M - p_0)}{(M - p) p_0} \right| = k(t - t_0) $$

Step 4: Algebraic Inversion:

$$ \frac{p(M - p_0)}{(M - p) p_0} = e^{k(t - t_0)} \implies p(M - p_0) = p_0 (M - p) e^{k(t - t_0)} $$ $$ p \left[ (M - p_0) + p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} \right] = M p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} $$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $e^{k(t - t_0)}$:

The Exact Logistic Formula
$$ \mathbf{p(t) = \frac{M p_0}{p_0 + (M - p_0) e^{-k(t - t_0)}}} \tag{4.2} $$

4.3 Asymptotic Limits and Malthusian Recovery

  • Long-Term Saturation ($t \to \infty$): Since $k > 0$, $\lim_{t\to\infty} e^{-k(t-t_0)} = 0$. $$ \lim_{t \to \infty} p(t) = \frac{M p_0}{p_0 + (M - p_0) \cdot 0} = \mathbf{M} $$ The carrying capacity $M$ acts as a universal global attractor for all positive initial states $p_0 > 0$.
  • Infinite Capacity Limit ($M \to \infty$ — Recovery of Malthus): Dividing numerator and denominator of (4.2) by $M$: $$ p(t) = \frac{p_0}{\frac{p_0}{M} + \left( 1 - \frac{p_0}{M} \right) e^{-k(t - t_0)}} $$ Taking the mathematical limit as environmental saturation vanishes ($M \to \infty$): $$ \lim_{M \to \infty} p(t) = \frac{p_0}{0 + (1 - 0) e^{-k(t - t_0)}} = \mathbf{p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)}} $$ Verhulst's nonlinear model reduces strictly and smoothly to Malthus' linear exponential law!

5. Solvability, Well-Posedness and Picard-Lindelöf Theorem

Before attempting to solve a differential equation analytically or numerically, an engineer must ascertain whether the mathematical model makes physical sense.

5.1 Hadamard's Definition of Well-Posedness

In 1902, Jacques Hadamard formulated the three indispensable requirements for a well-posed physical problem:

  1. Existence: At least one solution $y(t)$ exists satisfying both the ODE and initial conditions.
  2. Uniqueness: Exactly one solution exists (deterministic causality).
  3. Continuous Dependence (Stability): Small perturbations in initial data produce small variations in the solution over finite time.

5.2 The Initial Value Problem (IVP) and Volterra Integral Form

Consider the first-order scalar IVP:

$$ \begin{cases} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(t, y) \\ y(t_0) = y_0 \end{cases} \tag{5.1} $$

Integrating both sides from $t_0$ to $t$ transforms the differential problem into an equivalent Volterra Integral Equation:

$$ \mathbf{y(t) = y_0 + \int_{t_0}^t f(s, y(s)) \, ds} \tag{5.2} $$

Equation (5.2) incorporates the initial condition $y(t_0) = y_0$ automatically. Picard's iterative scheme defines the sequence:

$$ y_0(t) = y_0, \quad y_{k+1}(t) = y_0 + \int_{t_0}^t f(s, y_k(s)) \, ds, \quad k \ge 0 $$

5.3 Picard-Lindelöf Theorem & The Lipschitz Condition

Picard-Lindelöf Existence and Uniqueness Theorem

Let $R = \{ (t, y) : |t - t_0| \le a, \; |y - y_0| \le b \}$ be a closed rectangle. Suppose:

  1. $f(t, y)$ is continuous on $R$, so $|f(t, y)| \le M$ on $R$.
  2. $f(t, y)$ satisfies a Lipschitz condition with respect to $y$ on $R$: $$ |f(t, y_1) - f(t, y_2)| \le L |y_1 - y_2|, \quad \forall (t, y_1), (t, y_2) \in R \tag{5.3} $$ (Sufficient condition: $\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right| \le L < \infty$ on $R$).

Then, the IVP (5.1) has a unique solution $y(t)$ defined on the interval $[t_0 - h, t_0 + h]$, where: $$ h = \min\left( a, \, \frac{b}{M} \right) $$

5.4 Nonlinear Pathologies: Failure Modes

Pathology 1: Non-Uniqueness via Lipschitz Failure ($y' = y^{1/3}$)

Consider the IVP:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = y^{1/3}, \quad y(0) = 0 $$
  • Continuity: $f(t, y) = y^{1/3}$ is continuous on all $\mathbb{R}^2$. Peano's Theorem guarantees that at least one solution exists.
  • Lipschitz Failure: Compute the partial derivative: $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{1}{3 y^{2/3}} \implies \lim_{y \to 0} \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = +\infty $$ The derivative is unbounded near $y = 0$, violating the Lipschitz condition.
  • Infinite Solutions: (1) Trivial equilibrium: $y_1(t) \equiv 0$.
    (2) Separation of variables for $y \neq 0$: $\int y^{-1/3} dy = \int dt \implies \frac{3}{2} y^{2/3} = t \implies y_2(t) = \left(\frac{2}{3}t\right)^{3/2}$ and $y_3(t) = -\left(\frac{2}{3}t\right)^{3/2}$.
    (3) Infinite branching family for any delay $c \ge 0$: $$ y(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \le t \le c \\ \pm \left( \frac{2}{3}(t - c) \right)^{3/2} & t > c \end{cases} $$

Verdict: Determinism is completely destroyed because the Lipschitz condition is breached at the initial state.

Pathology 2: Finite-Time Blow-Up ($y' = y^2$)

Consider the smooth IVP:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = y^2, \quad y(0) = y_0 > 0 $$
  • Regularity: $f(y) = y^2$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y$ are smooth polynomials on every compact domain. Picard-Lindelöf guarantees that a unique local solution exists.
  • Analytical Solution: Separating variables: $$ \int_{y_0}^{y(t)} \frac{d\eta}{\eta^2} = \int_0^t ds \implies -\frac{1}{y(t)} + \frac{1}{y_0} = t \implies \mathbf{y(t) = \frac{y_0}{1 - y_0 t}} $$
  • Singularity / Blow-Up: As $t \to \left(\frac{1}{y_0}\right)^-$: $$ \lim_{t \to (1/y_0)^-} y(t) = +\infty $$

Verdict: In nonlinear equations, even infinitely smooth velocity fields can cause solutions to escape to infinity in finite time $T^* = \frac{1}{y_0}$. This never happens in linear ODEs with continuous coefficients!

6. Continuum Reduction: Laminar Viscous Poiseuille Flow

The full incompressible Navier-Stokes equations constitute a system of four coupled nonlinear partial differential equations. However, in axisymmetric geometries with fully developed laminar flow, the equations reduce identically to a linear second-order ODE.

6.1 Reduction from the Navier-Stokes System

Consider steady, laminar flow through a circular pipe of radius $a$. In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ with axis along the centerline:

  • Steady flow: $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = 0$.
  • Axisymmetry: $\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} = 0$, $u_\theta = 0$.
  • Unidirectional flow: $u_r = 0$. By continuity $\nabla \cdot \vec{u} = \frac{\partial u_z}{\partial z} = 0 \implies u_z = V(r)$.
  • Convective acceleration: $(\vec{u} \cdot \nabla)\vec{u} = u_r \frac{\partial V}{\partial r} \hat{k} + V(r) \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} \hat{k} = \mathbf{0}$. The nonlinear convective term vanishes identically!

The axial momentum Navier-Stokes equation reduces to:

$$ 0 = -\frac{dp}{dz} + \mu \left[ \frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) \right] $$

Defining the constant positive driving parameter $P \equiv -\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{dp}{dz} > 0$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) = -P} \tag{6.1} $$

6.2 Step-by-Step Integration & Physical Boundary Conditions

First Integration (Velocity Gradient):

Multiply equation (6.1) by $r$ and integrate:

$$ \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) = -P r \implies r \frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{P r^2}{2} + c $$ $$ \frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{P r}{2} + \frac{c}{r} \tag{6.2} $$
Physical Regularity Condition at Centerline ($r = 0$)

As $r \to 0^+$, the term $\frac{c}{r} \to \pm\infty$ if $c \neq 0$. An infinite velocity gradient would generate infinite viscous shear stress $\tau_{rz} = \mu \frac{dV}{dr}$ and infinite viscous dissipation on the pipe centerline, which is physically impossible without a line source of momentum. Hence, physical regularity requires: $$ \mathbf{c = 0} $$ Consequently, the velocity gradient is well-behaved: $\frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{P r}{2}$.

Second Integration (Velocity Field):

$$ V(r) = \int -\frac{P r}{2} \, dr = -\frac{P r^2}{4} + C_2 \tag{6.3} $$
No-Slip Boundary Condition at Wall ($r = a$)

Real viscous fluids adhere to solid impermeable boundaries at the molecular level (no-slip condition): $$ V(a) = 0 \implies -\frac{P a^2}{4} + C_2 = 0 \implies C_2 = \frac{P a^2}{4} $$

The Classical Hagen-Poiseuille Parabolic Profile
$$ \mathbf{V(r) = \frac{P}{4}\left( a^2 - r^2 \right) = \frac{1}{4\mu}\left(-\frac{dp}{dz}\right)\left( a^2 - r^2 \right)} \tag{6.4} $$

6.3 Macroscopic Aerospace Quantities

Aerospace Engineering Quantity Analytical Formula Physical Description
Maximum Centerline Velocity $V_{\max} = V(0) = \dfrac{P a^2}{4}$ Peak flow speed achieved at the pipe axis $r = 0$.
Volumetric Flow Rate ($Q$) $Q = \displaystyle\int_0^a V(r) \cdot 2\pi r \, dr = \dfrac{\pi P a^4}{8} = \dfrac{\pi a^4}{8\mu}\left(-\dfrac{dp}{dz}\right)$ The celebrated Hagen-Poiseuille Law: flow rate scales with the fourth power of pipe radius ($a^4$).
Average Velocity ($V_{\text{avg}}$) $V_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{Q}{\pi a^2} = \dfrac{P a^2}{8} = \dfrac{1}{2} V_{\max}$ Mean bulk velocity across the circular section is exactly half the maximum velocity.
Wall Shear Stress ($\tau_w$) $\tau_w = -\left.\mu \dfrac{dV}{dr}\right|_{r=a} = \dfrac{\mu P a}{2} = \dfrac{a}{2}\left(-\dfrac{dp}{dz}\right)$ Skin friction acting on the pipe boundary, balancing the axial pressure drop.
