Exercise 1

Linear Independence of Canonical Solution Pairs via Wronskian

Wronskian Determinant • Fundamental Bases
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

By finding the Wronskian of the following pairs of functions, show that they are linearly independent:

  1. $x_1(t) = e^{k_1 t}$ and $x_2(t) = e^{k_2 t}$ with $k_1 \neq k_2$
  2. $x_1(t) = e^{k t}$ and $x_2(t) = t e^{k t}$
  3. $x_1(t) = e^{\rho t}\sin(\omega t)$ and $x_2(t) = e^{\rho t}\cos(\omega t)$ with $\omega \neq 0$
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

For two continuously differentiable functions $x_1, x_2 \in C^1(I)$, the Wronskian determinant is defined as: $$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} x_1(t) & x_2(t) \\ \dot{x}_1(t) & \dot{x}_2(t) \end{pmatrix} = x_1(t)\dot{x}_2(t) - x_2(t)\dot{x}_1(t) $$ If $W[x_1, x_2](t_0) \neq 0$ at even a single point $t_0 \in I$, the functions $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are strictly linearly independent on the interval $I$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation
Part (i): Distinct Exponential Modes $x_1(t) = e^{k_1 t}, \; x_2(t) = e^{k_2 t}$ ($k_1 \neq k_2$)

Differentiating each function:

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = k_1 e^{k_1 t}, \qquad \dot{x}_2(t) = k_2 e^{k_2 t}$$

Formulating the Wronskian determinant:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = e^{k_1 t}\left( k_2 e^{k_2 t} \right) - e^{k_2 t}\left( k_1 e^{k_1 t} \right) = (k_2 - k_1) e^{(k_1 + k_2)t} $$

Since the exponential function satisfies $e^{(k_1 + k_2)t} > 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and $k_1 \neq k_2 \implies k_2 - k_1 \neq 0$:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) \neq 0 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} \implies \text{Linearly Independent on } \mathbb{R} $$
Part (ii): Repeated Root Secular Mode $x_1(t) = e^{kt}, \; x_2(t) = t e^{kt}$

Differentiating using the product rule on $x_2(t)$:

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = k e^{kt}, \qquad \dot{x}_2(t) = 1 \cdot e^{kt} + t(k e^{kt}) = (1 + kt)e^{kt}$$

Formulating the Wronskian determinant:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = e^{kt}\left[ (1 + kt)e^{kt} \right] - t e^{kt}\left[ k e^{kt} \right] = e^{2kt} \left[ (1 + kt) - kt \right] = e^{2kt} $$

Since $e^{2kt} > 0$ for every real $t$, $W(t)$ is strictly positive everywhere:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = e^{2kt} \neq 0 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} \implies \text{Linearly Independent on } \mathbb{R} $$
Part (iii): Conjugate Complex Modes $x_1(t) = e^{\rho t}\sin(\omega t), \; x_2(t) = e^{\rho t}\cos(\omega t)$ ($\omega \neq 0$)

Differentiating via product rule:

$$\dot{x}_1(t) = e^{\rho t}\left[ \rho \sin(\omega t) + \omega \cos(\omega t) \right]$$ $$\dot{x}_2(t) = e^{\rho t}\left[ \rho \cos(\omega t) - \omega \sin(\omega t) \right]$$

Assembling $W(t) = x_1 \dot{x}_2 - x_2 \dot{x}_1$ and factoring $e^{2\rho t}$:

$$ W(t) = e^{2\rho t}\left[ \sin(\omega t)\left(\rho \cos(\omega t) - \omega \sin(\omega t)\right) - \cos(\omega t)\left(\rho \sin(\omega t) + \omega \cos(\omega t)\right) \right] $$ $$ W(t) = e^{2\rho t}\left[ \rho\sin\omega t\cos\omega t - \omega\sin^2\omega t - \rho\cos\omega t\sin\omega t - \omega\cos^2\omega t \right] $$

The cross terms $\rho\sin\omega t\cos\omega t$ cancel identically. Applying $\sin^2\omega t + \cos^2\omega t = 1$:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = e^{2\rho t}\left[ -\omega(\sin^2\omega t + \cos^2\omega t) \right] = -\omega e^{2\rho t} $$

Because $\omega \neq 0$ and $e^{2\rho t} > 0$:

$$ W[x_1, x_2](t) = -\omega e^{2\rho t} \neq 0 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} \implies \text{Linearly Independent on } \mathbb{R} $$
Phase 4: Interpretation & Master Results
✓ Master Wronskian Summary

(i) Distinct Exponentials: $W[e^{k_1 t}, e^{k_2 t}](t) = \mathbf{(k_2 - k_1) e^{(k_1 + k_2)t}} \neq 0$

(ii) Critical Damping Secular Mode: $W[e^{kt}, t e^{kt}](t) = \mathbf{e^{2kt}} > 0$

(iii) Damped Harmonic Oscillations: $W[e^{\rho t}\sin\omega t, e^{\rho t}\cos\omega t](t) = \mathbf{-\omega e^{2\rho t}} \neq 0$

Aerospace Engineering Context
These three fundamental pairs represent the complete basis spanning the null space of any linear time-invariant (LTI) mechanical system $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0$: overdamped aerodynamic divergence/decay, critically damped actuation, and flutter oscillation.
Exercise 2

Abel's Identity and the Wronskian Dichotomy

Abel's Formula • Wronskian Dichotomy
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

Show that the Wronskian for two solutions $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ of the second order differential equation $$ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + p_1(t)\frac{dx}{dt} + p_2(t)x = 0 $$ satisfies $\frac{dW(t)}{dt} = -p_1(t)W(t)$. Deduce either that $W(t) = 0$ for all $t$, or that $W(t) \neq 0$ for all $t$.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Assume $p_1(t), p_2(t)$ are continuous functions on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Let $x_1(t), x_2(t) \in C^2(I)$ be solutions of $L[x] = \ddot{x} + p_1\dot{x} + p_2 x = 0$. We compute the time derivative of $W(t) = x_1\dot{x}_2 - x_2\dot{x}_1$ and replace the second derivatives using the governing ODE.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Step 1: Differentiating $W(t)$

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left( x_1 \dot{x}_2 - x_2 \dot{x}_1 \right) = \left( \dot{x}_1 \dot{x}_2 + x_1 \ddot{x}_2 \right) - \left( \dot{x}_2 \dot{x}_1 + x_2 \ddot{x}_1 \right) $$

The product terms $\dot{x}_1\dot{x}_2$ and $\dot{x}_2\dot{x}_1$ cancel:

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = x_1 \ddot{x}_2 - x_2 \ddot{x}_1 \tag{1} $$

Step 2: Substituting Second Derivatives from the ODE

$$\ddot{x}_1 = -p_1(t)\dot{x}_1 - p_2(t)x_1, \qquad \ddot{x}_2 = -p_1(t)\dot{x}_2 - p_2(t)x_2$$

Substituting into (1):

$$ \frac{dW}{dt} = x_1\left( -p_1\dot{x}_2 - p_2 x_2 \right) - x_2\left( -p_1\dot{x}_1 - p_2 x_1 \right) $$ $$ \frac{dW}{dt} = -p_1(t)\left[ x_1 \dot{x}_2 - x_2 \dot{x}_1 \right] - p_2(t)\left[ x_1 x_2 - x_2 x_1 \right] $$

Since $x_1 x_2 - x_2 x_1 \equiv 0$ and $x_1 \dot{x}_2 - x_2 \dot{x}_1 = W(t)$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dW(t)}{dt} = -p_1(t) W(t)} \tag{2} $$

Step 3: Integrating to Abel's Formula

Equation (2) is a separable first-order linear ODE. Integrating from $t_0$ to $t$:

$$ \int_{W(t_0)}^{W(t)} \frac{dW}{W} = -\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds \implies \ln\left| \frac{W(t)}{W(t_0)} \right| = -\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds $$ $$ \mathbf{W(t) = W(t_0) \exp\left( -\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds \right)} \tag{3} $$

Step 4: Deduction of the Wronskian Dichotomy

Because $p_1(s)$ is continuous on $I$, the integral $\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds$ is finite for all $t \in I$. Consequently, the exponential term is strictly positive:

$$ \exp\left( -\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds \right) > 0 \quad \forall t \in I $$
  • If $W(t_0) = 0$: $W(t) = 0 \times \exp(\dots) = 0$ for all $t \in I$.
  • If $W(t_0) \neq 0$: Since neither factor is zero, $W(t) \neq 0$ for all $t \in I$.
Phase 4: Interpretation & Master Results
✓ Abel's Identity & Wronskian Dichotomy
$$ \boxed{\frac{dW}{dt} = -p_1(t)W(t) \implies W(t) = W(t_0)\exp\left( -\int_{t_0}^t p_1(s)\,ds \right)} $$

Conclusion: For any two solutions of a linear homogeneous second-order ODE with continuous coefficients, the Wronskian is either identically zero everywhere on $I$, or non-zero at every point of $I$. It can never cross zero isolatedly.

Exercise 3

Peano's Counterexample: Wronskian Vanishing vs Linear Independence

Peano Counterexample • Rigorous Analysis
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

We have seen that, if $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are two solutions of a linear homogeneous second order differential equation, then they are linearly independent if and only if their Wronskian is non-zero. This is not true for general functions that are not the solutions of some differential equation.

  1. Check that, if $f(t) = t^2|t|$, then $\frac{df}{dt} = 3t|t|$ (straightforward for $t \neq 0$; use the formal definition of the derivative at $t = 0$).
  2. Considering $f_1(t) = t^2|t|$ and $f_2(t) = t^3$, show that, although these two functions are linearly independent on $\mathbb{R}$, their Wronskian is identically zero.
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Linear independence on a function space requires that $c_1 f_1(t) + c_2 f_2(t) = 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ implies $c_1 = c_2 = 0$. The Wronskian is $W[f_1, f_2](t) = f_1(t)f_2'(t) - f_2(t)f_1'(t)$. This problem demonstrates why the condition "$W(t) \equiv 0 \implies \text{linearly dependent}$" fails for arbitrary functions that do not satisfy a common regular linear ODE.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation
Part (i): Differentiating $f(t) = t^2|t|$

For $t > 0$: $|t| = t \implies f(t) = t^3 \implies f'(t) = 3t^2 = 3t(t) = 3t|t|$.

For $t < 0$: $|t| = -t \implies f(t) = -t^3 \implies f'(t) = -3t^2 = 3t(-t) = 3t|t|$.

At $t = 0$ (Formal Newton Difference Quotient):

$$ f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(0 + h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h|h| $$

Testing left and right limits: $\lim_{h \to 0^+} h^2 = 0$, $\lim_{h \to 0^-} (-h^2) = 0$. Both equal $0$, so $f'(0) = 0$.

Evaluating $3t|t|$ at $t = 0$: $3(0)|0| = 0$. Thus, everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{df}{dt} = 3t|t|} $$
Part (ii): Linear Independence of $f_1(t) = t^2|t|$ and $f_2(t) = t^3$ on $\mathbb{R}$

Assume constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $c_1 f_1(t) + c_2 f_2(t) = 0$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ c_1 t^2|t| + c_2 t^3 = 0 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} $$

Pick $t = 1$: $c_1(1)^2|1| + c_2(1)^3 = c_1 + c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = -c_1$.

Pick $t = -1$: $c_1(-1)^2|-1| + c_2(-1)^3 = c_1(1) + c_2(-1) = c_1 - c_2 = 0 \implies c_1 - (-c_1) = 2c_1 = 0 \implies c_1 = 0, \; c_2 = 0$.

Since only the trivial combination works: $f_1$ and $f_2$ are linearly independent on $\mathbb{R}$.

Wronskian Determinant on $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ W[f_1, f_2](t) = f_1(t)f_2'(t) - f_2(t)f_1'(t) = \left( t^2|t| \right)(3t^2) - \left( t^3 \right)(3t|t|) $$ $$ W[f_1, f_2](t) = 3t^4|t| - 3t^4|t| \equiv 0 \quad \forall t \in \mathbb{R} $$
Phase 4: Interpretation & Epistemological Insight
Why Peano's Counterexample Does Not Contradict Abel's Theorem

If $f_1$ and $f_2$ were solutions of a regular second-order ODE $x'' + p_1(t)x' + p_2(t)x = 0$, $W \equiv 0$ would force linear dependence. However, constructing the ODE satisfied by $f_1$ and $f_2$ on $t > 0$ gives $t^2 x'' - 6x = 0 \iff x'' - \frac{6}{t^2}x = 0$. The coefficient $p_2(t) = -6/t^2$ is singular at $t = 0$. Because the coefficient blows up at $t = 0$, the ODE is not regular, Picard-Lindelöf existence-uniqueness breaks down, and Abel's theorem does not apply across $t = 0$.

Exercise 4

15 Second-Order Homogeneous Linear ODEs with Initial Conditions

Characteristic Polynomial • 15 IVPs Solved
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

Find the general solution of the following differential equations, and then the unique solution satisfying the specified initial conditions:

(i) $\ddot{x} - 3\dot{x} + 2x = 0, \; x(0)=2, \dot{x}(0)=6$
(ii) $y'' - 4y' + 4y = 0, \; y(0)=0, y'(0)=3$
(iii) $z'' - 4z' + 13z = 0, \; z(0)=7, z'(0)=42$
(iv) $\ddot{y} + \dot{y} - 6y = 0, \; y(0)=-1, y'(0)=8$
(v) $\ddot{y} - 4\dot{y} = 0, \; y(0)=13, \dot{y}(0)=0$
(vi) $\ddot{\theta} + 4\theta = 0, \; \theta(0)=0, \dot{\theta}(0)=10$
(vii) $\ddot{y} + 2\dot{y} + 10y = 0, \; y(0)=3, \dot{y}(0)=0$
(viii) $2\ddot{z} + 7\dot{z} - 4z = 0, \; z(0)=0, \dot{z}(0)=9$
(ix) $\ddot{y} + 2\dot{y} + y = 0, \; y(0)=0, \dot{y}(0)=-1$
(x) $\ddot{x} + 6\dot{x} + 10x = 0, \; x(0)=3, \dot{x}(0)=1$
(xi) $4\ddot{x} - 20\dot{x} + 21x = 0, \; x(0)=-4, \dot{x}(0)=-12$
(xii) $\ddot{y} + \dot{y} - 2y = 0, \; y(0)=4, \dot{y}(0)=-4$
(xiii) $\ddot{y} - 4y = 0, \; y(0)=10, \dot{y}(0)=0$
(xiv) $y'' + 4y' + 4y = 0, \; y(0)=27, y'(0)=-54$
(xv) $\ddot{y} + \omega^2 y = 0, \; y(0)=0, \dot{y}(0)=1$
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

For $a \ddot{u} + b \dot{u} + c u = 0$, the exponential ansatz $u(t) = e^{rt}$ yields the auxiliary equation: $$ a r^2 + b r + c = 0, \qquad \Delta = b^2 - 4ac $$ - $\Delta > 0$: $u(t) = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}$ (Distinct Real).
- $\Delta = 0$: $u(t) = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{rt}$ (Repeated Real).
- $\Delta < 0$: $u(t) = e^{\rho t}(c_1 \cos\omega t + c_2 \sin\omega t)$ (Complex Conjugate $r = \rho \pm i\omega$).
Constants $c_1, c_2$ are uniquely determined by Cauchy conditions $u(0) = u_0, \dot{u}(0) = v_0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation (All 15 Items)
(i) $\ddot{x} - 3\dot{x} + 2x = 0$ with $x(0) = 2, \; \dot{x}(0) = 6$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 - 3r + 2 = 0 \implies (r-1)(r-2) = 0 \implies r_1 = 1, r_2 = 2$.

General solution: $x(t) = c_1 e^t + c_2 e^{2t} \implies \dot{x}(t) = c_1 e^t + 2c_2 e^{2t}$.

Initial conditions: $c_1 + c_2 = 2$, $c_1 + 2c_2 = 6 \implies c_2 = 4, \; c_1 = -2$.

Solution: $\mathbf{x(t) = -2e^t + 4e^{2t}}$

(ii) $y'' - 4y' + 4y = 0$ with $y(0) = 0, \; y'(0) = 3$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 - 4r + 4 = 0 \implies (r-2)^2 = 0 \implies r = 2$ (mult. 2).

General solution: $y(t) = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{2t} \implies y'(t) = (2c_1 + c_2 + 2c_2 t)e^{2t}$.

Initial conditions: $y(0) = c_1 = 0$; $y'(0) = 2(0) + c_2 = 3 \implies c_2 = 3$.

Solution: $\mathbf{y(t) = 3t e^{2t}}$

(iii) $z'' - 4z' + 13z = 0$ with $z(0) = 7, \; z'(0) = 42$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 - 4r + 13 = 0 \implies (r-2)^2 + 9 = 0 \implies r = 2 \pm 3i$ ($\rho = 2, \omega = 3$).

General solution: $z(t) = e^{2t}(c_1 \cos 3t + c_2 \sin 3t)$.

Derivative: $z'(t) = e^{2t}\left[ (2c_1 + 3c_2)\cos 3t + (2c_2 - 3c_1)\sin 3t \right]$.

Initial conditions: $z(0) = c_1 = 7$; $z'(0) = 2(7) + 3c_2 = 42 \implies 3c_2 = 28 \implies c_2 = \frac{28}{3}$.

Solution: $\mathbf{z(t) = e^{2t}\left( 7\cos 3t + \frac{28}{3}\sin 3t \right)}$

(iv) $\ddot{y} + \dot{y} - 6y = 0$ with $y(0) = -1, \; y'(0) = 8$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + r - 6 = 0 \implies (r+3)(r-2) = 0 \implies r_1 = 2, r_2 = -3$.

General solution: $y(t) = c_1 e^{2t} + c_2 e^{-3t} \implies \dot{y}(t) = 2c_1 e^{2t} - 3c_2 e^{-3t}$.

Initial conditions: $c_1 + c_2 = -1$, $2c_1 - 3c_2 = 8 \implies 2c_1 - 3(-1-c_1) = 5c_1 + 3 = 8 \implies c_1 = 1, \; c_2 = -2$.

Solution: $\mathbf{y(t) = e^{2t} - 2e^{-3t}}$

(v) $\ddot{y} - 4\dot{y} = 0$ with $y(0) = 13, \; \dot{y}(0) = 0$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 - 4r = 0 \implies r(r-4) = 0 \implies r_1 = 0, r_2 = 4$.

General solution: $y(t) = c_1 + c_2 e^{4t} \implies \dot{y}(t) = 4c_2 e^{4t}$.

Initial conditions: $\dot{y}(0) = 4c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = 0$; $y(0) = c_1 = 13$.

Solution: $\mathbf{y(t) = 13}$ (Steady constant equilibrium)

(vi) $\ddot{\theta} + 4\theta = 0$ with $\theta(0) = 0, \; \dot{\theta}(0) = 10$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + 4 = 0 \implies r = \pm 2i$ ($\rho = 0, \omega = 2$).

General solution: $\theta(t) = c_1 \cos 2t + c_2 \sin 2t \implies \dot{\theta}(t) = -2c_1 \sin 2t + 2c_2 \cos 2t$.

Initial conditions: $\theta(0) = c_1 = 0$; $\dot{\theta}(0) = 2c_2 = 10 \implies c_2 = 5$.

Solution: $\mathbf{\theta(t) = 5\sin 2t}$

(vii) $\ddot{y} + 2\dot{y} + 10y = 0$ with $y(0) = 3, \; \dot{y}(0) = 0$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + 2r + 10 = 0 \implies (r+1)^2 + 9 = 0 \implies r = -1 \pm 3i$ ($\rho = -1, \omega = 3$).

General solution: $y(t) = e^{-t}(c_1 \cos 3t + c_2 \sin 3t)$.

Derivative: $\dot{y}(t) = e^{-t}\left[ (3c_2 - c_1)\cos 3t - (3c_1 + c_2)\sin 3t \right]$.

Initial conditions: $y(0) = c_1 = 3$; $\dot{y}(0) = 3c_2 - 3 = 0 \implies c_2 = 1$.

Solution: $\mathbf{y(t) = e^{-t}\left( 3\cos 3t + \sin 3t \right)}$

(viii) $2\ddot{z} + 7\dot{z} - 4z = 0$ with $z(0) = 0, \; \dot{z}(0) = 9$

Auxiliary eq: $2r^2 + 7r - 4 = 0 \implies (2r - 1)(r + 4) = 0 \implies r_1 = \frac{1}{2}, r_2 = -4$.

General solution: $z(t) = c_1 e^{t/2} + c_2 e^{-4t} \implies \dot{z}(t) = \frac{1}{2}c_1 e^{t/2} - 4c_2 e^{-4t}$.

Initial conditions: $z(0) = c_1 + c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = -c_1$; $\dot{z}(0) = \frac{1}{2}c_1 + 4c_1 = \frac{9}{2}c_1 = 9 \implies c_1 = 2, c_2 = -2$.

Solution: $\mathbf{z(t) = 2e^{t/2} - 2e^{-4t}}$

(ix) $\ddot{y} + 2\dot{y} + y = 0$ with $y(0) = 0, \; \dot{y}(0) = -1$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + 2r + 1 = 0 \implies (r+1)^2 = 0 \implies r = -1$ (mult. 2).

General solution: $y(t) = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-t} \implies \dot{y}(t) = (c_2 - c_1 - c_2 t)e^{-t}$.

Initial conditions: $y(0) = c_1 = 0$; $\dot{y}(0) = c_2 - 0 = -1 \implies c_2 = -1$.

Solution: $\mathbf{y(t) = -t e^{-t}}$

(x) $\ddot{x} + 6\dot{x} + 10x = 0$ with $x(0) = 3, \; \dot{x}(0) = 1$

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + 6r + 10 = 0 \implies (r+3)^2 + 1 = 0 \implies r = -3 \pm i$ ($\rho = -3, \omega = 1$).

General solution: $x(t) = e^{-3t}(c_1 \cos t + c_2 \sin t)$.

Derivative: $\dot{x}(t) = e^{-3t}\left[ (c_2 - 3c_1)\cos t - (c_1 + 3c_2)\sin t \right]$.

Initial conditions: $x(0) = c_1 = 3$; $\dot{x}(0) = c_2 - 3(3) = 1 \implies c_2 = 10$.

Solution: $\mathbf{x(t) = e^{-3t}\left( 3\cos t + 10\sin t \right)}$

(xi) $4\ddot{x} - 20\dot{x} + 21x = 0$ with $x(0) = -4, \; \dot{x}(0) = -12$

Auxiliary eq: $4r^2 - 20r + 21 = 0$. $\Delta = 400 - 336 = 64 = 8^2 \implies r = \frac{20 \pm 8}{8} \implies r_1 = \frac{7}{2}, r_2 = \frac{3}{2}$.

General solution: $x(t) = c_1 e^{3t/2} + c_2 e^{7t/2} \implies \dot{x}(t) = \frac{3}{2}c_1 e^{3t/2} + \frac{7}{2}c_2 e^{7t/2}$.

Initial conditions: $c_1 + c_2 = -4$, $\frac{3}{2}c_1 + \frac{7}{2}(-4-c_1) = -2c_1 - 14 = -12 \implies -2c_1 = 2 \implies c_1 = -1, \; c_2 = -3$.

Solution: $\mathbf{x(t) = -e^{3t/2} - 3e^{7t/2}}$

(xii) $\ddot{y} + \dot{y} - 2y = 0$ with $y(0) = 4, \; \dot{y}(0) = -4$

Saddle Point Invariant Manifold and Asymptotic Decay

Phase Plane • Stable Separatrix
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

If the roots of the auxiliary equation are $k_1 > 0$ and $-k_2 < 0$ then the solution is $x(t) = A e^{k_1 t} + B e^{-k_2 t}$. For most choices of initial conditions $x(0) = x_0, \; \dot{x}(0) = y_0$, we will have $x(t) \to \pm\infty$ as $t \to \infty$. However, there are some special initial conditions for which $x(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. Find the relationship between $x_0$ and $y_0$ that ensures this.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Given $k_1 > 0$ and $k_2 > 0$, the mode $e^{k_1 t}$ diverges exponentially to $+\infty$, while $e^{-k_2 t} \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. Therefore, $x(t) \to 0$ if and only if the unstable amplitude is strictly annihilated: $A = 0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Step 1: Differentiate the general solution:

$$ x(t) = A e^{k_1 t} + B e^{-k_2 t} \implies \dot{x}(t) = k_1 A e^{k_1 t} - k_2 B e^{-k_2 t} $$

Step 2: Impose initial Cauchy conditions at $t = 0$:

$$ x(0) = A + B = x_0 \tag{1} $$ $$ \dot{x}(0) = k_1 A - k_2 B = y_0 \tag{2} $$

Step 3: Solve for the amplitude $A$:

Multiply (1) by $k_2$ and add to (2):

$$ k_2(A + B) + (k_1 A - k_2 B) = k_2 x_0 + y_0 \implies (k_1 + k_2)A = y_0 + k_2 x_0 $$ $$ A = \frac{y_0 + k_2 x_0}{k_1 + k_2} \tag{3} $$

Step 4: Asymptotic Condition $x(t) \to 0$:

Because $\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{k_1 t} = \infty$ and $\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-k_2 t} = 0$:

$$ \lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = 0 \iff A = 0 $$ $$ \frac{y_0 + k_2 x_0}{k_1 + k_2} = 0 \implies \mathbf{y_0 = -k_2 x_0} $$
Phase 4: Interpretation & Dynamical Systems
✓ Asymptotic Decay Condition
$$ \boxed{y_0 = -k_2 x_0 \iff \dot{x}(0) = -k_2 x(0)} $$

When this relationship holds, $A = 0$ and $B = x_0$, giving the purely decaying trajectory $\mathbf{x(t) = x_0 e^{-k_2 t}}$.

Geometric Phase Plane Meaning: Saddle Separatrix
In the phase plane $(x, \dot{x})$, the origin is a saddle equilibrium. The line $\dot{x} = -k_2 x$ is the stable invariant manifold (separatrix). Any state starting off this 1D manifold is swept away by the unstable manifold $\dot{x} = k_1 x$, diverging exponentially to infinity.

Amplitude-Phase Transformation for Harmonic Oscillations

Polar Representation • Phasor Geometry
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

Given an oscillating function of the form $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t)$, show that it can be rewritten as a "single oscillation" $x(t) = M\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with amplitude $M = (A^2 + B^2)^{1/2}$ and phase $\phi = \arctan(B/A)$.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Apply the angle subtraction identity $\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$ with $\alpha = \omega t$ and $\beta = \phi$, and equate coefficients with the Cartesian basis $\{\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t\}$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Step 1: Expand the single oscillation form:

$$ x(t) = M\cos(\omega t - \phi) = M\left[ \cos(\omega t)\cos\phi + \sin(\omega t)\sin\phi \right] = (M\cos\phi)\cos(\omega t) + (M\sin\phi)\sin(\omega t) $$

Step 2: Equate with Cartesian components:

$$ A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t) = (M\cos\phi)\cos(\omega t) + (M\sin\phi)\sin(\omega t) $$ $$\begin{cases} A = M\cos\phi & (1) \\ B = M\sin\phi & (2) \end{cases}$$

Step 3: Solve for Amplitude $M$:

Square and add (1) and (2):

$$ A^2 + B^2 = M^2\cos^2\phi + M^2\sin^2\phi = M^2(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = M^2 $$ $$ \mathbf{M = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} = (A^2 + B^2)^{1/2}} $$

Step 4: Solve for Phase Lag $\phi$:

Divide (2) by (1):

$$ \frac{B}{A} = \frac{M\sin\phi}{M\cos\phi} = \tan\phi \implies \mathbf{\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{B}{A}\right)} $$
Phase 4: Interpretation & Engineering Relevance
✓ Polar Harmonic Formulas
$$ \boxed{x(t) = M\cos(\omega t - \phi), \qquad M = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}, \qquad \phi = \arctan(B/A)} $$
Structural Dynamics Application
In aircraft wing flutter and structural modal analysis, the amplitude-phase form immediately reveals the maximum physical peak deflection $x_{\max} = M$ and time lag $\Delta t = \phi/\omega$ relative to external periodic gusts.

Simple Pendulum Energy Conservation and Small Oscillations

Energy First Integral • Pendulum Isochronism
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

Consider a so-called simple pendulum whose shaft has length $L$ and negligible mass, and a suspended mass $m$ at its end. If the pivot is at the origin of coordinates, show that:

  1. The gravitational potential energy is $V(\theta) = mgL(1 - \cos\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle with the downward vertical.
  2. The total energy is $E = \frac{1}{2}mL^2\dot{\theta}^2 + mgL(1 - \cos\theta)$.
  3. The equation of motion reads $\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta$, obtained by requesting $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$.
  4. By considering small oscillations around the vertical, the equation becomes $\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\theta$. Show that the general solution reads $\theta(t) = M\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ and relate $\omega$ with system parameters. Does $\omega$ depend on mass $m$? Does it depend on amplitude?
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Let pivot be at $(0, 0)$ and positive $y$ upwards. Position of bob: $x = L\sin\theta, \; y = -L\cos\theta$. Velocity along arc: $v = L\dot{\theta}$. Total mechanical energy $E = T + V$. In the absence of dissipative friction, mechanical energy is conserved: $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Part (i): Potential Energy $V(\theta)$

Vertical height above the lowest point $\theta = 0$ ($y_{\min} = -L$):

$$ h(\theta) = y(\theta) - (-L) = -L\cos\theta + L = L(1 - \cos\theta) $$ $$ \mathbf{V(\theta) = mgh = mgL(1 - \cos\theta)} $$

Part (ii): Total Energy $E$

Kinetic energy of point mass: $T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(L\dot{\theta})^2 = \frac{1}{2}mL^2\dot{\theta}^2$.

$$ \mathbf{E = T + V(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}mL^2\dot{\theta}^2 + mgL(1 - \cos\theta)} $$

Part (iii): Equation of Motion via Energy Conservation

Differentiating $E(t)$ with respect to time using the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}mL^2(2\dot{\theta}\ddot{\theta}) + mgL(\sin\theta\cdot\dot{\theta}) = mL^2\dot{\theta}\ddot{\theta} + mgL\dot{\theta}\sin\theta = 0 $$

Factoring out $mL^2\dot{\theta} \neq 0$:

$$ mL^2\dot{\theta}\left( \ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta \right) = 0 \implies \mathbf{\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta} $$

Part (iv): Small Oscillations Linearization & Frequency Analysis

For $|\theta| \ll 1$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. The ODE simplifies to:

$$ \ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0 \iff \ddot{\theta} + \omega^2\theta = 0, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}} $$

The general solution is $\theta(t) = c_1\cos\omega t + c_2\sin\omega t$. In amplitude-phase form:

$$ \mathbf{\theta(t) = M\cos(\omega t - \phi)} $$
Phase 4: Conceptual Answers & Master Results
✓ Natural Frequency & Answers to Questions
$$ \boxed{\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \quad [\text{rad/s}], \qquad T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \quad [\text{s}]} $$

1. Does frequency depend on mass $m$? NO. Mass $m$ cancels identically from both terms in the equation of motion.

2. Does frequency depend on amplitude $M$? In the linear small-amplitude approximation, NO (Galileo's isochronism). In the exact nonlinear pendulum, the period lengthens with amplitude according to complete elliptic integrals: $T(M) \approx T_0(1 + \frac{1}{16}M^2 + \dots)$.

Physical Regimes of the Damped Harmonic Oscillator

Mechanical Damping • Under/Critical/Overdamped
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

Consider the equation $m\ddot{x} + \mu\dot{x} + kx = 0$ for the deviation $x(t)$ of a point particle with mass $m$ from its equilibrium position, subject to an elastic force (constant $k$) and drag force (parameter $\mu$). Discuss how the form of the solutions changes depending on the value of $\mu$ relative to the critical damping $\mu_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

The auxiliary polynomial is $m r^2 + \mu r + k = 0$. The discriminant is $\Delta = \mu^2 - 4mk = \mu^2 - \mu_c^2$, where $\mu_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$. Roots are $r_{1,2} = -\frac{\mu}{2m} \pm \frac{\sqrt{\mu^2 - \mu_c^2}}{2m} = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation
1. Underdamped Regime: $\mu < \mu_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$ ($\Delta < 0$)

The discriminant is negative: $\sqrt{\mu^2 - 4mk} = i\sqrt{4mk - \mu^2}$.

Roots are complex conjugates with negative real part: $r_{1,2} = -\gamma \pm i\omega_d$, where:

$$ \gamma = \frac{\mu}{2m}, \qquad \omega_d = \frac{\sqrt{4mk - \mu^2}}{2m} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2} $$

General solution:

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = e^{-\gamma t}\left( c_1\cos\omega_d t + c_2\sin\omega_d t \right) = M e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t - \phi)} $$

Behavior: Decaying oscillatory motion within an exponential envelope $\pm M e^{-\gamma t}$, crossing zero infinitely many times.

2. Critically Damped Regime: $\mu = \mu_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$ ($\Delta = 0$)

The discriminant vanishes identically. The characteristic equation has a single repeated real root:

$$ r_1 = r_2 = -\frac{\mu_c}{2m} = -\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = -\omega_0 $$

General solution:

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}} $$

Behavior: Non-oscillatory. Crosses equilibrium at most once. Represents the fastest monotonic return to equilibrium without overshoot.

3. Overdamped Regime: $\mu > \mu_c = 2\sqrt{mk}$ ($\Delta > 0$)

The discriminant is positive: $\sqrt{\mu^2 - 4mk} < \mu$. Both roots are distinct, real, and strictly negative:

$$ r_1 = -\gamma + \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} < 0, \qquad r_2 = -\gamma - \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} < 0 $$

General solution:

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}} $$

Behavior: Purely exponential sluggish decay. At long times, dominated by the slower decay mode $e^{r_1 t}$. Crosses zero at most once.

Falling Chain from Table and Hyperbolic Motion

Hyperbolic Motion • Variable Hanging Mass
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

A chain of length $\ell = 5\text{ m}$ and mass $m$, with uniform mass density, is sitting on a table. If the chain is initially at rest and $1\text{ m}$ of the chain hangs from the edge of the table, apply Newton’s second law to calculate the time $t_*$ when the whole chain falls off the table, neglecting any friction.

Hint: Formulate a second order ODE for the variable $x(t)$, which is the length of the portion of chain that is overhanging, as a function of time.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Let $x(t) \in [1, 5]$ be the overhanging length. Mass density is $\lambda = m/\ell$. The downward gravity force pulling the chain is $F_{\text{ext}} = m_{\text{hang}} g = (\lambda x) g = \frac{m}{\ell}gx$. Because the table is frictionless and the entire chain of constant total mass $m$ accelerates together along its contour: $$ m \ddot{x} = \frac{m}{\ell} g x \implies \ddot{x} - \frac{g}{\ell} x = 0 $$

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Step 1: Auxiliary Equation and General Solution

Let $\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}$. The ODE is $\ddot{x} - \alpha^2 x = 0$.

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 - \alpha^2 = 0 \implies r = \pm \alpha$.

Hyperbolic general solution:

$$ x(t) = c_1 \cosh(\alpha t) + c_2 \sinh(\alpha t) $$ $$ \dot{x}(t) = \alpha \left[ c_1 \sinh(\alpha t) + c_2 \cosh(\alpha t) \right] $$

Step 2: Initial Conditions

Released from rest: $\dot{x}(0) = \alpha c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = 0$.

Initial hanging length: $x(0) = c_1 = x_0 = 1\text{ m}$.

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = x_0 \cosh(\alpha t) = \cosh\left( \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}\,t \right)} $$

Step 3: Detachment Time $t_*$

The entire chain falls off when $x(t_*) = \ell = 5\text{ m}$:

$$ \cosh(\alpha t_*) = \frac{\ell}{x_0} = \frac{5}{1} = 5 $$ $$ \alpha t_* = \operatorname{arcosh}(5) = \ln\left( 5 + \sqrt{5^2 - 1} \right) = \ln(5 + \sqrt{24}) = \ln(5 + 2\sqrt{6}) $$

Step 4: Numerical Evaluation

Using standard gravity $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ and $\ell = 5\text{ m}$:

$$ \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{9.8}{5}} = \sqrt{1.96} = 1.4\text{ s}^{-1} $$ $$ \ln(5 + 2\sqrt{6}) \approx \ln(5 + 4.898979) = \ln(9.898979) \approx 2.292432 $$ $$ \mathbf{t_* = \frac{2.292432}{1.4} \approx 1.63745\text{ s} \approx 1.637\text{ s}} $$

(If taking $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$, $\alpha \approx 1.40071\text{ s}^{-1} \implies t_* \approx 1.6366\text{ s} \approx 1.637\text{ s}$)

Phase 4: Interpretation & Conservation Check
✓ Master Fall Time & Detachment Velocity
$$ \boxed{t_* = \frac{\operatorname{arcosh}(5)}{1.4} \approx 1.637\text{ s}} $$

Detachment velocity: $v_* = \dot{x}(t_*) = x_0 \alpha \sinh(\alpha t_*) = 1 \times 1.4 \times \sqrt{5^2 - 1} = 1.4\sqrt{24} \approx \mathbf{6.859\text{ m/s}}$.

Energy Check: $\Delta E_p = mg\frac{\ell}{2} - mg\frac{x_0^2}{2\ell} = \frac{mg}{2\ell}(\ell^2 - x_0^2) = \frac{1}{2}m v_*^2 \implies v_* = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}(\ell^2 - x_0^2)} = \sqrt{1.96(24)} \approx 6.859\text{ m/s}$. Exact match!

Phase 1: Statement & Setup

A parachutist of mass $m\text{ kg}$ jumps from a plane with initial velocity $0$. Write down an ODE to describe the motion of the parachutist, and determine the terminal speed (when $t \to \infty$). Compare this speed with that of free fall.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Taking positive axis downwards, Newton's 2nd law gives: $$ \sum F = m \frac{dv}{dt} = mg - cv \implies \frac{dv}{dt} + \frac{c}{m} v = g $$ With initial condition $v(0) = 0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation

Step 1: Integrating Factor Method

Integrating factor $\mu(t) = e^{(c/m)t}$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ v(t) e^{(c/m)t} \right] = g e^{(c/m)t} $$ $$ v(t) e^{(c/m)t} = \int g e^{(c/m)t}\,dt = \frac{mg}{c} e^{(c/m)t} + C $$ $$ v(t) = \frac{mg}{c} + C e^{-(c/m)t} $$

Step 2: Initial Condition $v(0) = 0$

$$ v(0) = \frac{mg}{c} + C = 0 \implies C = -\frac{mg}{c} $$ $$ \mathbf{v(t) = \frac{mg}{c}\left( 1 - e^{-(c/m)t} \right)} $$

Step 3: Terminal Velocity ($t \to \infty$)

$$ \mathbf{v_\infty = \lim_{t \to \infty} v(t) = \frac{mg}{c}} $$

Step 4: Comparison with Free Fall

  • Free fall ($c = 0$): $v_{\text{free}}(t) = gt$. Velocity increases linearly without bound ($v \to \infty$).
  • Parachutist with Drag ($c > 0$): Velocity saturates asymptotically to a safe finite constant $v_\infty = \frac{mg}{c}$.

✓ Terminal Velocity & Velocity Profile
$$ \boxed{\frac{dv}{dt} + \frac{c}{m}v = g \implies v(t) = \frac{mg}{c}\left(1 - e^{-(c/m)t}\right)} $$ $$ \boxed{v_\infty = \frac{mg}{c}} $$

Euler-Cauchy Equation via Exponential Transformation

Euler-Cauchy • Coordinate Mapping • Resonance
Phase 1: Statement & Setup

We consider the following second order ODE (Euler equation):

$$ x^2 y''(x) + p x y'(x) + q y(x) = g(x), \quad x > 0 $$
  1. By making the change of variable $x = e^z$ and using the chain rule, show that the ODE is transformed into one with constant coefficients in the variable $z$: $$ \frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} + (p - 1)\frac{dy}{dz} + q y = g(e^z) $$
  2. Take $p = 3, \; q = -3$ and $g(x) = 4x$, make the previous transformation and compute the general solution.
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Let $x = e^z \iff z = \ln x$ for $x > 0$. The derivative with respect to $x$ is $\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}$. By applying the chain and product rules to $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}$, the power pre-factors $x$ and $x^2$ cancel identically, transforming the variable-coefficient ODE into a linear ODE with constant coefficients.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Analytical Derivation
Part (i): Chain Rule Derivation of the Transformation

First Derivative:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dz}\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}\frac{dy}{dz} \implies x \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dz} \tag{1} $$

Second Derivative:

$$ \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left( \frac{1}{x}\frac{dy}{dz} \right) = -\frac{1}{x^2}\frac{dy}{dz} + \frac{1}{x}\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dz}\right) = -\frac{1}{x^2}\frac{dy}{dz} + \frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{d^2 y}{dz^2}\frac{dz}{dx}\right) $$ $$ \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}\frac{dy}{dz} + \frac{1}{x^2}\frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} = \frac{1}{x^2}\left( \frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} - \frac{dy}{dz} \right) \implies x^2 \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = \frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} - \frac{dy}{dz} \tag{2} $$

Substitution into the Euler-Cauchy Equation:

$$ x^2 y'' + p x y' + q y = \left( \frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} - \frac{dy}{dz} \right) + p\left( \frac{dy}{dz} \right) + q y = g(e^z) $$ $$ \mathbf{\frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} + (p - 1)\frac{dy}{dz} + q y = g(e^z)} $$
Part (ii): Specific Case $p = 3, \; q = -3, \; g(x) = 4x$

1. Transformed ODE in $z$:

With $p - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2$, $q = -3$, and $g(e^z) = 4e^z$:

$$ \frac{d^2 y}{dz^2} + 2\frac{dy}{dz} - 3y = 4e^z $$

2. Homogeneous Solution $y_h(z)$:

Auxiliary eq: $r^2 + 2r - 3 = 0 \implies (r+3)(r-1) = 0 \implies r_1 = 1, r_2 = -3$.

$$ y_h(z) = c_1 e^z + c_2 e^{-3z} $$

3. Resonant Particular Solution $y_p(z)$:

Since the forcing exponent $e^{1\cdot z}$ matches the characteristic root $r_1 = 1$, we test the resonant ansatz $y_p(z) = A z e^z$:

$$ y_p'(z) = A(1 + z)e^z, \qquad y_p''(z) = A(2 + z)e^z $$ $$ y_p'' + 2y_p' - 3y_p = A e^z\left[ (2 + z) + 2(1 + z) - 3z \right] = A e^z\left[ 4 + 3z - 3z \right] = 4A e^z $$

Equating to $4e^z$: $4A = 4 \implies A = 1 \implies \mathbf{y_p(z) = z e^z}$.

4. General Solution in $z$:

$$ y(z) = c_1 e^z + c_2 e^{-3z} + z e^z $$

5. Back-transformation to $x$ ($z = \ln x, \; e^z = x, \; e^{-3z} = x^{-3}$):

$$ \mathbf{y(x) = c_1 x + \frac{c_2}{x^3} + x \ln x, \quad x > 0} $$
Phase 4: Master Verification & Aerospace Application
✓ Master Solution & ODE Verification
$$ \boxed{y(x) = c_1 x + c_2 x^{-3} + x\ln x, \quad x > 0} $$

Direct Verification:

$y'(x) = c_1 - 3c_2 x^{-4} + \ln x + 1$

$y''(x) = 12c_2 x^{-5} + \frac{1}{x}$

$x^2 y'' + 3x y' - 3y = (12c_2 x^{-3} + x) + (3c_1 x - 9c_2 x^{-3} + 3x\ln x + 3x) - (3c_1 x + 3c_2 x^{-3} + 3x\ln x) = 4x \equiv g(x)$. Confirmed!
