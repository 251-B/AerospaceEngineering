Exercise 1

Linearized Centre vs Nonlinear Stability

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf
Linearized Centre • Polar Form • Hartman-Grobman Limit
Statement

The linearized system may not give a qualitatively correct picture when the linearized equation produces a center, as this example demonstrates. First show that the origin is a center for the linearized version of the system

$$ \dot x = -y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2), \qquad \dot y = x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2). $$

Write down the equation satisfied by $r$, where $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, and show that the stability of the origin depends actually on the sign of $\lambda$. Draw the phase portrait for $\lambda < 0$.

Setting up the system

We begin by writing the autonomous planar system in standard component form $\dot x = f(x,y)$, $\dot y = g(x,y)$ and noting the role of the real parameter $\lambda$.

$$ f(x,y) = -y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2), \qquad g(x,y) = x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2), \qquad \lambda \in \mathbb{R}. $$

Both $f$ and $g$ are polynomials in $(x,y)$, hence $C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^2)$, so global existence and uniqueness of local trajectories hold everywhere. Because the symbol $\lambda$ already denotes the real system parameter, we write $\mu_\pm$ for the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix to avoid any notation clash.

Step 1: Critical points

We find all stationary points $(x^*, y^*) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ by setting $f(x,y) = 0$ and $g(x,y) = 0$ simultaneously.

$$ -y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2) = 0, \qquad x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2) = 0. $$

To eliminate the cubic term $\lambda(x^2 + y^2)$ without dividing by a possibly zero quantity, multiply the first equation by $y$ and the second equation by $x$:

$$ -y^2 + \lambda xy(x^2 + y^2) = 0, \qquad x^2 + \lambda xy(x^2 + y^2) = 0. $$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives

$$ x^2 + y^2 = 0. $$

Since $x$ and $y$ are real numbers, $x^2 \ge 0$ and $y^2 \ge 0$, so $x^2 + y^2 = 0$ forces $x = 0$ and $y = 0$. Checking directly, $f(0,0) = 0$ and $g(0,0) = 0$. Thus the origin

$$ (x^*, y^*) = (0, 0) $$

is the unique real critical point of the system for every $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x,y)$ by differentiating $f(x,y)$ and $g(x,y)$ with respect to $x$ and $y$ using the product rule, and then evaluate it at $(0,0)$.

Expanding $f(x,y) = -y + \lambda x^3 + \lambda xy^2$ and $g(x,y) = x + \lambda x^2 y + \lambda y^3$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 3\lambda x^2 + \lambda y^2 = \lambda(3x^2 + y^2), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -1 + 2\lambda xy, $$

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 1 + 2\lambda xy, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = \lambda x^2 + 3\lambda y^2 = \lambda(x^2 + 3y^2). $$

Hence the general Jacobian matrix is

$$ J(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda(3x^2 + y^2) & -1 + 2\lambda xy \\ 1 + 2\lambda xy & \lambda(x^2 + 3y^2) \end{pmatrix}. $$

Evaluating at $(x^*, y^*) = (0, 0)$ yields

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, $$

which is completely independent of $\lambda$. The linearized system about the origin is therefore $\dot u = -v$, $\dot v = u$, where $(u,v) = (x,y)$.

Step 3: Local study

We determine the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $J(0,0)$ to classify the linearized equilibrium and check whether the Hartman–Grobman (Poincaré) linearization theorem applies.

Form the characteristic polynomial $\det(J(0,0) - \mu I) = 0$:

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} -\mu & -1 \\ 1 & -\mu \end{pmatrix} = (-\mu)(-\mu) - (-1)(1) = \mu^2 + 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu_\pm = \pm i. $$

To find the complex eigenvector $\vec\eta_+$ associated with $\mu_+ = i$, solve $(J(0,0) - iI)\vec\eta_+ = \vec 0$ row by row:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} -i & -1 \\ 1 & -i \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \eta_1 \\ \eta_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

  • Row 2 gives $\eta_1 - i\eta_2 = 0 \implies \eta_1 = i\eta_2$.
  • Row 1 gives $-i(i\eta_2) - \eta_2 = \eta_2 - \eta_2 = 0$ (consistent).

Choosing $\eta_2 = 1$ yields

$$ \vec\eta_+ = \begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec\eta_- = \begin{pmatrix} -i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Because the eigenvalues $\mu_\pm = \pm i$ are purely imaginary ($\operatorname{Re}(\mu_\pm) = 0$, $\operatorname{Im}(\mu_\pm) = \pm 1 \neq 0$), the origin is a centre for the linearized system, with circular trajectories rotating counter-clockwise (at $(1,0)$, $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 1 > 0$).

However, $(0,0)$ is non-hyperbolic ($\operatorname{Re}(\mu_\pm) = 0$). Consequently, the Hartman–Grobman / Poincaré linearization theorem does not apply: the cubic nonlinearities $\lambda x(x^2+y^2)$ and $\lambda y(x^2+y^2)$ can destroy the closed orbits of the linear centre and turn the origin into either an asymptotically stable spiral or an unstable spiral.

Step 4: Global picture

We transform the full nonlinear system into polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ to obtain exact equations for the radial distance and rotation angle across the entire plane.

Radial equation for $r$

From $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, differentiate both sides with respect to $t$ using the chain rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}(r^2) = 2r\dot r = 2x\dot x + 2y\dot y. $$

Substitute $\dot x = -y + \lambda x(x^2+y^2)$ and $\dot y = x + \lambda y(x^2+y^2)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2r\dot r &= 2x\bigl(-y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2)\bigr) + 2y\bigl(x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2)\bigr) \\ &= -2xy + 2\lambda x^2(x^2 + y^2) + 2xy + 2\lambda y^2(x^2 + y^2) \\ &= 2\lambda(x^2 + y^2)(x^2 + y^2) \\ &= 2\lambda r^4. \end{aligned} $$

For $r > 0$, dividing both sides by $2r$ yields the exact radial ODE:

$$ \dot r = \lambda r^3. $$

(This also holds at $r = 0$, since the origin is a stationary point with $\dot r = 0$.)

Angular equation for $\theta$

Differentiating $\tan\theta = y/x$ with respect to $t$ gives $\sec^2\theta\,\dot\theta = (x\dot y - y\dot x)/x^2$. Multiplying by $\cos^2\theta = x^2/r^2$ yields

$$ \dot\theta = \frac{x\dot y - y\dot x}{r^2}. $$

Compute the numerator explicitly:

$$ \begin{aligned} x\dot y - y\dot x &= x\bigl(x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2)\bigr) - y\bigl(-y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2)\bigr) \\ &= x^2 + \lambda xy(x^2 + y^2) + y^2 - \lambda xy(x^2 + y^2) \\ &= x^2 + y^2 = r^2. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore, for every $r > 0$,

$$ \dot\theta = \frac{r^2}{r^2} = 1 > 0. $$

Every non-trivial trajectory rotates counter-clockwise at constant unit angular speed $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + t$.

Stability dependence on the sign of $\lambda$

Separating variables in $\dot r = \lambda r^3$ for $r_0 = r(0) > 0$:

$$ \frac{dr}{r^3} = \lambda\,dt \;\Longrightarrow\; \int_{r_0}^{r(t)} r^{-3}\,dr = \int_0^t \lambda\,ds \;\Longrightarrow\; \left[-\frac{1}{2r^2}\right]_{r_0}^{r(t)} = \lambda t. $$

Simplifying gives

$$ \frac{1}{r(t)^2} = \frac{1}{r_0^2} - 2\lambda t \;\Longrightarrow\; r(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t}}. $$

Three distinct qualitative regimes emerge according to the sign of $\lambda$:

  1. $\lambda < 0$ (asymptotically stable spiral): Since $\dot r = \lambda r^3 < 0$ for all $r > 0$, the radius decreases strictly monotonically. Here $-2\lambda > 0$, so $r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t > 0$ for all $t \ge 0$, and $r(t) \to 0$ as $t \to +\infty$ (with slow algebraic decay $r(t) \sim (-2\lambda t)^{-1/2}$ rather than exponential decay). Combined with $\dot\theta = 1 > 0$, all trajectories spiral counter-clockwise into the origin.
  2. $\lambda = 0$ (true nonlinear centre): Here $\dot r = 0$, so $r(t) = r_0$ is constant and trajectories are exact concentric circles $x^2 + y^2 = r_0^2$ traversed counter-clockwise with period $2\pi$. The origin is Lyapunov stable, but not asymptotically stable.
  3. $\lambda > 0$ (unstable spiral with finite-time blow-up): Since $\dot r = \lambda r^3 > 0$ for all $r > 0$, every trajectory spirals counter-clockwise outward. Moreover, $r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t$ reaches zero at $t_* = \frac{1}{2\lambda r_0^2} < \infty$, so $r(t) \to +\infty$ in finite time.
Phase portrait of $\dot x = -y + \lambda x(x^2+y^2)$, $\dot y = x + \lambda y(x^2+y^2)$ for $\lambda < 0$ (drawn with $\lambda = -1$). Every non-trivial trajectory spirals counter-clockwise ($\dot\theta = 1 > 0$) into the asymptotically stable origin as $r(t) = (r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t)^{-1/2} \to 0$.

Answers and checks
  • Check against Robinson (Ex. 32.1): The radial equation $\dot r = \lambda r^3$ and the trichotomy ($\lambda < 0$ stable spiral, $\lambda = 0$ centre, $\lambda > 0$ unstable spiral) match Robinson, An Introduction to Ordinary Differential Equations, Exercise 32.1.
  • Limiting case $\lambda \to 0$: Setting $\lambda = 0$ in the exact radial formula $r(t) = (r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t)^{-1/2}$ recovers $r(t) = r_0$, matching the circular orbits of the linearized centre.
  • Dimensional / scaling consistency: Taking $x, y$ to have length dimension $[L]$ and $t$ to have time dimension $[T]$, the linear terms $\dot x \sim -y$ require unit frequency $[T^{-1}]$, while $\lambda x^3$ has dimension $[\lambda][L]^3 = [L][T]^{-1} \implies [\lambda] = [L]^{-2}[T]^{-1}$. Then $\dot r = \lambda r^3$ has dimension $[L]^{-2}[T]^{-1}\cdot [L]^3 = [L][T]^{-1}$, and $r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t$ has homogeneous dimension $[L]^{-2}$.
Key results
  • Linearization at $(0,0)$: $J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\mu_\pm = \pm i$ (linearized centre; Hartman–Grobman does not apply).
  • Exact polar form: $\dot r = \lambda r^3$, $\dot\theta = 1$, with radial solution $r(t) = \bigl(r_0^{-2} - 2\lambda t\bigr)^{-1/2}$.
  • Nonlinear stability of $(0,0)$: asymptotically stable spiral for $\lambda < 0$; true centre for $\lambda = 0$; unstable spiral (finite-time blow-up at $t_* = 1/(2\lambda r_0^2)$) for $\lambda > 0$.
Exercise 2

Seven Two-Species Population Models

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf
Competition • Predator-Prey • Seven Systems
Statement

For each of the following models of two population species, describe first the type of situation being modelled, then find the stationary points, determine their stability type and sketch the phase portrait for $x, y \ge 0$. Decide what the phase portrait means for the evolution of the two species.

(i) $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 2x)$,

(ii) $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - x/4)$,

(iii) $\dot x = x(2 - x - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 3x)$,

(iv) $\dot x = x(1 - 2y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + 3x)$,

(v) $\dot x = x(4 - x/2 - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$,

(vi) $\dot x = x(10 - x - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(1 + x - 10y)$,

(vii) $\dot x = x(3 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$.

Setting up the system

We begin by identifying the ecological interaction represented by each model from the signs of the intrinsic growth rates, self-limitation (logistic) terms, and cross-interaction terms. In all seven systems, $x = 0$ implies $\dot x = 0$ and $y = 0$ implies $\dot y = 0$, so the coordinate axes are invariant manifolds and the biologically meaningful first quadrant $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ is positively invariant.

(i) Asymmetric competition (species $x$ dominant)

In $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$ and $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 2x)$, both species grow logistically in isolation (carrying capacities $K_x = 2$ and $K_y = 1$), and each cross-term is negative ($-xy$ and $-2xy$). Hence this models interspecific competition for a shared resource.

(ii) Weak competition

In $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$ and $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - x/4)$, both species again have positive intrinsic growth rates and logistic self-regulation ($K_x = 2$, $K_y = 1$), with negative cross-terms ($-xy$ and $-xy/4$). This is a weak competition model in which interspecific inhibition is smaller than intraspecific self-limitation.

(iii) Strong competition

In $\dot x = x(2 - x - 3y)$ and $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 3x)$, both species grow logistically alone ($K_x = 2$, $K_y = 1$), but both cross-terms are large and negative ($-3xy$). This models strong competition, where each species inhibits its competitor more strongly than itself.

(iv) Classical Lotka–Volterra predator–prey

In $\dot x = x(1 - 2y)$ and $\dot y = y(-2 + 3x)$, species $x$ grows exponentially in the absence of $y$ ($\dot x = x$) and is harmed by $y$ ($-2xy$), whereas species $y$ decays exponentially without $x$ ($\dot y = -2y$) and benefits from $x$ ($+3xy$). Thus $x$ is the prey and $y$ is the predator in the classical Lotka–Volterra model without carrying capacity.

(v) Predator–prey with logistic prey ($K = 8$)

In $\dot x = x(4 - x/2 - 3y)$ and $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$, the prey $x$ obeys logistic growth with carrying capacity $K_x = 8$ in the absence of predators, while the predator $y$ dies out exponentially without prey and grows proportionally to $xy$. This is a predator–prey model with logistic prey.

(vi) Logistic prey and logistic predator with alternative food source

In $\dot x = x(10 - x - 3y)$ and $\dot y = y(1 + x - 10y)$, species $x$ is harmed by $y$ ($-3xy$) and has carrying capacity $K_x = 10$ alone, whereas species $y$ benefits from $x$ ($+xy$) and still has a positive intrinsic growth rate $+1$ with carrying capacity $K_y = 1/10$ when $x = 0$. This models a prey ($x$) and a generalist predator ($y$) that has an alternative food source and logistic self-regulation.

(vii) Predator–prey with logistic prey ($K = 3$)

In $\dot x = x(3 - x - y)$ and $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$, the prey $x$ grows logistically up to carrying capacity $K_x = 3$ alone, while the specialist predator $y$ decays without $x$ and benefits from $x$. This is again a predator–prey model with logistic prey.

Step 1: Critical points

We find all stationary points $(x^*, y^*)$ in the first quadrant $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ for each system by solving $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously.

(i) Critical points of $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 2x)$

Setting $x(2 - x - y) = 0$ and $y(2 - 2y - 2x) = 0$ yields four factor combinations:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $x = 0$, $2 - 2y = 0 \implies y = 1 \implies B(0, 1)$.
  3. $y = 0$, $2 - x = 0 \implies x = 2 \implies C(2, 0)$.
  4. $2 - x - y = 0$ and $2 - 2y - 2x = 0$: dividing the second equation by $2$ gives $x + y = 1$, which contradicts $x + y = 2$. These lines are parallel, so there is no fourth (interior) stationary point.
(ii) Critical points of $\dot x = x(2 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - x/4)$

Setting $x(2 - x - y) = 0$ and $y(2 - 2y - x/4) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $x = 0$, $2 - 2y = 0 \implies y = 1 \implies B(0, 1)$.
  3. $y = 0$, $2 - x = 0 \implies x = 2 \implies C(2, 0)$.
  4. Interior equilibrium: solve $x + y = 2$ and $\frac{1}{4}x + 2y = 2$. From the first, $x = 2 - y$; substituting into the second gives $$ \frac{2 - y}{4} + 2y = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2 - y + 8y = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; 7y = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^* = \frac{6}{7}, \qquad x^* = 2 - \frac{6}{7} = \frac{8}{7}. $$ Hence $D\bigl(\tfrac{8}{7}, \tfrac{6}{7}\bigr)$, which lies in the first quadrant.
(iii) Critical points of $\dot x = x(2 - x - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(2 - 2y - 3x)$

Setting $x(2 - x - 3y) = 0$ and $y(2 - 2y - 3x) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $x = 0$, $2 - 2y = 0 \implies y = 1 \implies B(0, 1)$.
  3. $y = 0$, $2 - x = 0 \implies x = 2 \implies C(2, 0)$.
  4. Interior equilibrium: solve $x + 3y = 2$ and $3x + 2y = 2$. From the first, $x = 2 - 3y$; substituting into the second yields $$ 3(2 - 3y) + 2y = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 6 - 7y = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^* = \frac{4}{7}, \qquad x^* = 2 - \frac{12}{7} = \frac{2}{7}. $$ Hence $D\bigl(\tfrac{2}{7}, \tfrac{4}{7}\bigr)$ in the first quadrant.
(iv) Critical points of $\dot x = x(1 - 2y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + 3x)$

Setting $x(1 - 2y) = 0$ and $y(-2 + 3x) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $x = 0$, $-2 + 3(0) = -2 \neq 0 \implies$ no other point on $x = 0$.
  3. $y = 0$, $1 - 2(0) = 1 \neq 0 \implies$ no other point on $y = 0$.
  4. $1 - 2y = 0$ and $-2 + 3x = 0 \implies x^* = \frac{2}{3}$, $y^* = \frac{1}{2} \implies B\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$.
(v) Critical points of $\dot x = x(4 - x/2 - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$

Setting $x(4 - x/2 - 3y) = 0$ and $y(-2 + x) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $y = 0$, $4 - x/2 = 0 \implies x = 8 \implies B(8, 0)$.
  3. $x = 0$ forces $-2 + 0 = -2 \neq 0$, so no non-zero equilibrium on $x = 0$.
  4. $-2 + x = 0 \implies x^* = 2$; substituting into $4 - x/2 - 3y = 0$ gives $4 - 1 - 3y = 0 \implies y^* = 1 \implies C(2, 1)$.
(vi) Critical points of $\dot x = x(10 - x - 3y)$, $\dot y = y(1 + x - 10y)$

Setting $x(10 - x - 3y) = 0$ and $y(1 + x - 10y) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $y = 0$, $10 - x = 0 \implies x = 10 \implies B(10, 0)$.
  3. $x = 0$, $1 - 10y = 0 \implies y = \frac{1}{10} \implies C\bigl(0, \tfrac{1}{10}\bigr)$.
  4. Interior equilibrium: solve $x + 3y = 10$ and $-x + 10y = 1$. Adding the two equations gives $$ 13y = 11 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^* = \frac{11}{13}, \qquad x^* = 10 - \frac{33}{13} = \frac{97}{13}. $$ Hence $D\bigl(\tfrac{97}{13}, \tfrac{11}{13}\bigr)$ in the first quadrant.
(vii) Critical points of $\dot x = x(3 - x - y)$, $\dot y = y(-2 + x)$

Setting $x(3 - x - y) = 0$ and $y(-2 + x) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $y = 0$, $3 - x = 0 \implies x = 3 \implies B(3, 0)$.
  3. $-2 + x = 0 \implies x^* = 2$; substituting into $3 - x - y = 0$ gives $3 - 2 - y = 0 \implies y^* = 1 \implies C(2, 1)$.
Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x & \partial f/\partial y \\ \partial g/\partial x & \partial g/\partial y \end{pmatrix}$ explicitly for each system and evaluate it at every stationary point.

(i) Jacobian matrices

With $f(x,y) = 2x - x^2 - xy$ and $g(x,y) = 2y - 2y^2 - 2xy$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2 - 2x - y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -2y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2 - 4y - 2x. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(0,1)$, and $C(2,0)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(0,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(2,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(ii) Jacobian matrices

With $f(x,y) = 2x - x^2 - xy$ and $g(x,y) = 2y - 2y^2 - \frac{1}{4}xy$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2 - 2x - y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -\frac{1}{4}y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2 - 4y - \frac{1}{4}x. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(0,1)$, $C(2,0)$, and $D\bigl(\tfrac{8}{7}, \tfrac{6}{7}\bigr)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(0,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{4} & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(2,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ 0 & \frac{3}{2} \end{pmatrix}, $$

$$ J\!\left(\frac{8}{7}, \frac{6}{7}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - \frac{16}{7} - \frac{6}{7} & -\frac{8}{7} \\ -\frac{6}{28} & 2 - \frac{24}{7} - \frac{2}{7} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{8}{7} & -\frac{8}{7} \\ -\frac{3}{14} & -\frac{12}{7} \end{pmatrix}. $$

(iii) Jacobian matrices

With $f(x,y) = 2x - x^2 - 3xy$ and $g(x,y) = 2y - 2y^2 - 3xy$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2 - 2x - 3y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -3x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -3y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2 - 4y - 3x. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(0,1)$, $C(2,0)$, and $D\bigl(\tfrac{2}{7}, \tfrac{4}{7}\bigr)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(0,1) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ -3 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(2,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -6 \\ 0 & -4 \end{pmatrix}, $$

$$ J\!\left(\frac{2}{7}, \frac{4}{7}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - \frac{4}{7} - \frac{12}{7} & -\frac{6}{7} \\ -\frac{12}{7} & 2 - \frac{16}{7} - \frac{6}{7} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{2}{7} & -\frac{6}{7} \\ -\frac{12}{7} & -\frac{8}{7} \end{pmatrix}. $$

(iv) Jacobian matrices

With $f(x,y) = x - 2xy$ and $g(x,y) = -2y + 3xy$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 1 - 2y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -2x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 3y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -2 + 3x. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$ and $B\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J\!\left(\frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\frac{4}{3} \\ \frac{3}{2} & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(v) Jacobian matrices

With $f(x,y) = 4x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 - 3xy$ and $g(x,y) = -2y + xy$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 4 - x - 3y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -3x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -2 + x. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(8,0)$, and $C(2,1)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(8,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & -24 \\ 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(2,1) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -6 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(vi) Jacobian matrices

Step 3: Local study

We compute the characteristic polynomial $\det(J - \lambda I) = 0$ and solve $(J - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0$ row by row at every stationary point to classify each equilibrium.

(i) Local classification
  • $A(0,0)$: $J(0,0) = 2I$, so $(2 - \lambda)^2 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 2 > 0$ with two linearly independent eigenvectors $\vec v_1 = (1,0)^T$, $\vec v_2 = (0,1)^T$. Unstable star.
  • $B(0,1)$: Lower triangular, so $(1 - \lambda)(-2 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -2 < 0$. Saddle point.
  • For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies -2v_1 - 3v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • For $\lambda_2 = -2$: $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies v_1 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • $C(2,0)$: Upper triangular, so $(-2 - \lambda)^2 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = -2 < 0$. Solving $(J(2,0) + 2I)\vec v = \vec 0$: $$ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; -2v_2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; v_2 = 0. $$ There is only one independent eigenvector $\vec v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $C(2,0)$ is a stable improper (degenerate) node, tangent to the $x$-axis.
Discrepancy with the official solution

In Solved_exer_nonlinear_system.pdf (p. 2), the handwritten solution writes $J(2,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ and classifies $C(2,0)$ as a "stable star". The correct matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} -2 & -2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$, making $C(2,0)$ a stable improper node with single eigenvector $(1,0)^T$. Because both eigenvalues are still $-2 < 0$, the local asymptotic stability and the global biological outcome ($x \to 2$, $y \to 0$) are completely unaffected.

(i) Competitive exclusion of $y$ by $x$

The non-trivial nullclines are the parallel lines $y = 2 - x$ ($\dot x = 0$) and $y = 1 - x$ ($\dot y = 0$). Every initial state with $x(0) > 0$ converges to $(2,0)$: species $x$ always outcompetes species $y$ and drives it to extinction, reaching its own carrying capacity $x = 2$.

(iv) Periodic predator–prey oscillations (nonlinear centre proof)

The nullclines $y = 1/2$ ($\dot x = 0$) and $x = 2/3$ ($\dot y = 0$) divide the interior into four quadrants with counter-clockwise circulation around $B\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$. To prove that $B$ is a true nonlinear centre, separate variables in the orbit equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y(-2+3x)}{x(1-2y)}$:

$$ \frac{1 - 2y}{y}\,dy = \frac{-2 + 3x}{x}\,dx \;\Longrightarrow\; \int \left(\frac{1}{y} - 2\right)dy = \int \left(-\frac{2}{x} + 3\right)dx \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln y - 2y = -2\ln x + 3x - C. $$

Define $H(x,y) = 3x - 2\ln x + 2y - \ln y$ on $x > 0, y > 0$. Differentiating along any trajectory by the chain rule verifies that $H$ is strictly conserved:

$$ \dot H = \left(3 - \frac{2}{x}\right)\dot x + \left(2 - \frac{1}{y}\right)\dot y = \frac{3x - 2}{x}\,x(1 - 2y) + \frac{2y - 1}{y}\,y(-2 + 3x) = (3x - 2)(1 - 2y) - (1 - 2y)(3x - 2) = 0. $$

Moreover, $H_{xx} = 2/x^2 > 0$, $H_{yy} = 1/y^2 > 0$, and $H_{xy} = 0$, so $H$ is strictly convex on $x, y > 0$ with a unique global minimum at $B\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$ and $H \to +\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$, $y \to 0^+$, or $x + y \to \infty$. Therefore every level curve $H(x,y) = C$ is a closed orbit around $B$: prey and predator populations undergo sustained, periodic counter-clockwise oscillations whose amplitude is fixed by the initial condition.

Two Cooperative Species Models

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Cooperation • Bounded vs Unbounded Growth
Statement

The situation in which two species cooperate, so that the presence of one enhances the environment for the other, can be modelled by a coupled pair of equations of the form

$$ \dot x = x(A - ax + by), \qquad \dot y = y(B + cx - dy), $$

where all the parameters are positive. Sketch the phase portraits for the following cooperative equations:

(i) $\dot x = x(1 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(1 + x - 2y)$,

(ii) $\dot x = x(2 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(4 + 2x - y)$.

Setting up the system

We begin by identifying the positive parameters $(A, a, b, B, c, d)$ in each model and noting the invariant coordinate axes. In both models, $x = 0 \implies \dot x = 0$ and $y = 0 \implies \dot y = 0$, so the first quadrant $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ is positively invariant. Both species grow logistically in isolation (with carrying capacities $A/a$ and $B/d$), while the positive cross-terms $+bxy$ and $+cxy$ represent mutual benefit (cooperation). The qualitative outcome depends on the balance between self-limitation ($ad$) and mutual enhancement ($bc$).

(i) Weak cooperation ($ad > bc$)

Here $\dot x = x(1 - x + y)$ and $\dot y = y(1 + x - 2y)$, so $A = 1$, $a = 1$, $b = 1$, $B = 1$, $c = 1$, $d = 2$. Self-limitation dominates cooperation because $ad = 1 \cdot 2 = 2 > bc = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$.

(ii) Strong cooperation ($ad < bc$)

Here $\dot x = x(2 - x + y)$ and $\dot y = y(4 + 2x - y)$, so $A = 2$, $a = 1$, $b = 1$, $B = 4$, $c = 2$, $d = 1$. Mutual enhancement overwhelms self-limitation because $ad = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 < bc = 1 \cdot 2 = 2$.

Step 1: Critical points

We find all stationary points $(x^*, y^*)$ in the biologically meaningful region $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ by setting $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously.

(i) Critical points of $\dot x = x(1 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(1 + x - 2y)$

Solving $x(1 - x + y) = 0$ and $y(1 + x - 2y) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $y = 0$, $1 - x = 0 \implies x = 1 \implies B(1, 0)$.
  3. $x = 0$, $1 - 2y = 0 \implies y = \frac{1}{2} \implies C\bigl(0, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$.
  4. Interior equilibrium: solve $1 - x + y = 0$ (i.e. $x - y = 1$) and $1 + x - 2y = 0$ (i.e. $-x + 2y = 1$). Adding the two equations eliminates $x$: $$ (x - y) + (-x + 2y) = 1 + 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^* = 2, \qquad x^* = 1 + y^* = 3. $$ Thus $D(3, 2)$ lies in the first quadrant.
(ii) Critical points of $\dot x = x(2 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(4 + 2x - y)$

Solving $x(2 - x + y) = 0$ and $y(4 + 2x - y) = 0$:

  1. $x = 0$, $y = 0 \implies A(0, 0)$.
  2. $y = 0$, $2 - x = 0 \implies x = 2 \implies B(2, 0)$.
  3. $x = 0$, $4 - y = 0 \implies y = 4 \implies C(0, 4)$.
  4. Fourth system: solve $2 - x + y = 0 \implies y = x - 2$ and $4 + 2x - y = 0 \implies y = 2x + 4$. Equating the two expressions for $y$: $$ x - 2 = 2x + 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = -6, \qquad y = -6 - 2 = -8. $$ Because $(-6, -8)$ lies in the third quadrant, there is no fourth (interior) stationary point in $x, y \ge 0$.
Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x,y)$ from the partial derivatives of $f(x,y)$ and $g(x,y)$ and evaluate it at every stationary point in $x, y \ge 0$.

(i) Jacobian matrices

Expanding $f(x,y) = x - x^2 + xy$ and $g(x,y) = y + xy - 2y^2$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 1 - 2x + y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 1 + x - 4y. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(1,0)$, $C\bigl(0, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$, and $D(3,2)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(1,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J\!\left(0, \frac{1}{2}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{3}{2} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & -1 \end{pmatrix}, $$

$$ J(3,2) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 6 + 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 + 3 - 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 3 \\ 2 & -4 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(ii) Jacobian matrices

Expanding $f(x,y) = 2x - x^2 + xy$ and $g(x,y) = 4y + 2xy - y^2$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2 - 2x + y, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 2y, \quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 4 + 2x - 2y. $$

Evaluating at $A(0,0)$, $B(2,0)$, and $C(0,4)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(2,0) = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(0,4) = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 8 & -4 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We solve the characteristic equation $\det(J - \lambda I) = 0$ and find the eigenvectors row by row at each critical point to classify the local phase portrait.

(i) Local classification
  • $A(0,0)$: $J(0,0) = I$, so $(1 - \lambda)^2 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 1 > 0$ with two independent eigenvectors $(1,0)^T, (0,1)^T$. Unstable star.
  • $B(1,0)$: Upper triangular, so $(-1 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = -1 < 0$, $\lambda_2 = 2 > 0$. Saddle point.
  • For $\lambda_1 = -1$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $\begin{pmatrix} -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies -3v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • $C\bigl(0, \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)$: Lower triangular, so $\bigl(\frac{3}{2} - \lambda\bigr)(-1 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = \frac{3}{2} > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -1 < 0$. Saddle point.
  • For $\lambda_1 = \frac{3}{2}$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1/2 & -5/2 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}v_1 - \frac{5}{2}v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • For $\lambda_2 = -1$: $\begin{pmatrix} 5/2 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies v_1 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • $D(3,2)$: Expanding $\det(J(3,2) - \lambda I) = 0$: $$ (-3 - \lambda)(-4 - \lambda) - (3)(2) = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 12 - 6 = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 6) = 0. $$ Hence $\lambda_1 = -1 < 0$ and $\lambda_2 = -6 < 0$: stable node.
  • For $\lambda_1 = -1$: $\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 3 \\ 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies -2v_1 + 3v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ (slow direction).
  • For $\lambda_2 = -6$: $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (fast direction).
(ii) Local classification
  • $A(0,0)$: Diagonal, so $(2 - \lambda)(4 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 2 > 0$ with $\vec v_1 = (1,0)^T$, $\lambda_2 = 4 > 0$ with $\vec v_2 = (0,1)^T$. Unstable node (trajectories leave tangent to the $x$-axis).
  • $B(2,0)$: Upper triangular, so $(-2 - \lambda)(8 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = -2 < 0$, $\lambda_2 = 8 > 0$. Saddle point.
  • For $\lambda_1 = -2$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • For $\lambda_2 = 8$: $\begin{pmatrix} -10 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies -10v_1 + 2v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • $C(0,4)$: Lower triangular, so $(6 - \lambda)(-4 - \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 6 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -4 < 0$. Saddle point.
  • For $\lambda_1 = 6$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 8 & -10 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_1 = \vec 0 \implies 8v_1 - 10v_2 = 0 \implies \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
  • For $\lambda_2 = -4$: $\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 8 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec v_2 = \vec 0 \implies v_1 = 0 \implies \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Step 4: Global picture

We sketch the non-trivial nullclines and vector-field signs in $x, y \ge 0$ to contrast the two cooperation regimes.

(i) Bounded mutualistic coexistence ($ad > bc$)

The non-trivial nullclines are $y = x - 1$ ($\dot x = 0$, slope $1$) and $y = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{2}$ ($\dot y = 0$, slope $\frac{1}{2}$). Because the $\dot x = 0$ nullcline is steeper than the $\dot y = 0$ nullcline ($a/b = 1 > c/d = 1/2 \iff ad > bc$), they intersect in the first quadrant at $D(3, 2)$. In all four regions around $D$, the vector field points toward $D$, and the unstable manifolds of both boundary saddles $B(1,0)$ and $C(0, 1/2)$ flow directly into $D$. Consequently, every initial state with $x(0) > 0$ and $y(0) > 0$ converges to $D(3, 2)$: both species coexist stably at populations strictly larger than their isolated carrying capacities ($x^* = 3 > 1$, $y^* = 2 > 1/2$).

(ii) Unbounded mutualistic growth ($ad < bc$)

The non-trivial nullclines are $y = x - 2$ ($\dot x = 0$, slope $1$) and $y = 2x + 4$ ($\dot y = 0$, slope $2$). Because $a/b = 1 < c/d = 2$ ($ad < bc$), the two lines diverge in the first quadrant rather than intersecting. Inside the open wedge $x - 2 < y < 2x + 4$ between them, both $\dot x > 0$ and $\dot y > 0$; trajectories starting above $y = 2x + 4$ move right and down ($\dot x > 0, \dot y < 0$) into the wedge, while trajectories starting below $y = x - 2$ move left and up ($\dot x < 0, \dot y > 0$) into the wedge. Once inside the wedge, trajectories can never leave and both populations grow without bound: $x(t) \to +\infty$ and $y(t) \to +\infty$ (a classic modelling pathology of unconstrained linear mutualism, showing that realistic strong cooperation requires saturation terms).

Conservative One-Dimensional Potentials

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Conservative Systems • Energy Curves • Double Well
Statement

For the following choices of potential functions $V(x)$, write down the total energy for a particle of unit mass, and assuming that this is conserved write down a coupled system of equations for $x$ and $y = \dot x$. Sketch the phase portrait and interpret the dynamics in each case.

(i) $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$,

(ii) $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$,

(iii) $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$.

Setting up the system

We derive the coupled first-order system from conservation of mechanical energy for a particle of unit mass ($m = 1$) moving in a one-dimensional potential $V(x)$, and then write out the explicit energy and equations for each of the three potentials.

The total mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}\dot x^2$ and potential energy $V(x)$:

$$ E(x, \dot x) = \frac{1}{2}\dot x^2 + V(x). $$

Assuming $E$ is conserved along every trajectory ($\dot E = 0$), differentiate with respect to $t$ using the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}(2\dot x\ddot x) + V'(x)\dot x = \dot x\bigl(\ddot x + V'(x)\bigr) = 0. $$

For any non-stationary motion ($\dot x \not\equiv 0$), this forces Newton's equation $\ddot x = -V'(x)$ (which also holds at equilibria where $\dot x = 0$ and $V'(x) = 0$). Introducing the velocity variable $y = \dot x$ converts this second-order ODE into the planar conservative system

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -V'(x), $$

whose trajectories lie on the constant-energy level curves $\frac{1}{2}y^2 + V(x) = E$, i.e. $y = \pm\sqrt{2\bigl(E - V(x)\bigr)}$. Every phase portrait is therefore symmetric about the $x$-axis, with motion to the right in the upper half-plane ($y > 0 \implies \dot x > 0$) and to the left in the lower half-plane ($y < 0 \implies \dot x < 0$).

(i) Harmonic potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$ ($\omega > 0$)

Here $V'(x) = \omega^2 x$, so the conserved energy and coupled system are

$$ E(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2, \qquad \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -\omega^2 x. $$

(ii) Double-well potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$

Here $V'(x) = 2x^3 - 2x = 2x(x^2 - 1)$, so the conserved energy and coupled system are

$$ E(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2, \qquad \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -2x^3 + 2x = -2x(x^2 - 1). $$

(iii) Sextic potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$

Here $V'(x) = 3x^5 - 5x^3 + 4x = x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4)$, so the conserved energy and coupled system are

$$ E(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2, \qquad \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -3x^5 + 5x^3 - 4x = -x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4). $$

Step 1: Critical points

We find all stationary points $(x^*, y^*) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ by setting $\dot x = y = 0$ and $\dot y = -V'(x) = 0$. All critical points therefore lie on the $x$-axis at the stationary points of the potential $V(x)$.

(i) Harmonic potential

Setting $y = 0$ and $-\omega^2 x = 0$ (with $\omega \neq 0$) gives a single stationary point:

$$ (x^*, y^*) = (0, 0). $$

(ii) Double-well potential

Setting $y = 0$ and $-2x(x^2 - 1) = -2x(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0$ gives three stationary points:

$$ (0, 0), \qquad (1, 0), \qquad (-1, 0). $$

(iii) Sextic potential

Setting $y = 0$ and $-x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4) = 0$ gives either $x = 0$ or $3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0$. Letting $u = x^2 \ge 0$, the quadratic equation $3u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0$ has discriminant

$$ \Delta = (-5)^2 - 4(3)(4) = 25 - 48 = -23 < 0. $$

Hence $3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 > 0$ has no real roots, and the system has only one real critical point:

$$ (x^*, y^*) = (0, 0). $$

Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x,y)$ for any conservative system $\dot x = y$, $\dot y = -V'(x)$ and evaluate it at every critical point.

Since $f(x,y) = y$ and $g(x,y) = -V'(x)$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -V''(x), \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 0, $$

so

$$ J(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x) & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(i) Harmonic potential

Here $V''(x) = \omega^2$, so at $(0,0)$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(ii) Double-well potential

Here $V''(x) = 6x^2 - 2$. Evaluating at $(0,0)$ and $(\pm 1, 0)$ (following Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, pp. 15–16):

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(\pm 1, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

(iii) Sextic potential

Here $V''(x) = 15x^4 - 15x^2 + 4$. Evaluating at $(0,0)$ gives $V''(0) = 4$:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $J(x^*, 0)$ row by row at each stationary point. Note that for a conservative system, a linearized centre at a strict local minimum of $V(x)$ is automatically a true nonlinear centre, because $E(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + V(x)$ has a strict local minimum there and its level sets form closed loops around $(x^*, 0)$.

(i) Harmonic potential

Characteristic equation at $(0,0)$:

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + \omega^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_\pm = \pm i\omega. $$

For $\lambda_+ = i\omega$, solve $(J(0,0) - i\omega I)\vec\eta_+ = \vec 0$:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} -i\omega & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -i\omega \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \eta_1 \\ \eta_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Row 1 gives $-i\omega\eta_1 + \eta_2 = 0 \implies \eta_2 = i\omega\eta_1$ (Row 2 gives $-\omega^2\eta_1 - i\omega(i\omega\eta_1) = 0$, consistent). Choosing $\eta_1 = 1$ gives $\vec\eta_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i\omega \end{pmatrix}$. Since the system is already linear, $(0,0)$ is an exact centre.

(ii) Double-well potential
  • At $(0,0)$: Characteristic equation: $$ \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 2 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_\pm = \pm\sqrt{2}. $$ Solving $(J(0,0) - \lambda_\pm I)\vec v_\pm = \vec 0$: $$ \begin{pmatrix} \mp\sqrt{2} & 1 \\ 2 & \mp\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$ Row 1 gives $\mp\sqrt{2}v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies v_2 = \pm\sqrt{2}v_1$ (Row 2 gives $2v_1 \mp \sqrt{2}(\pm\sqrt{2}v_1) = 0$, consistent). Choosing $v_1 = \pm\sqrt{2}$ yields $\vec v_\pm = \begin{pmatrix} \pm\sqrt{2} \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Because $\lambda_+ > 0 > \lambda_-$, $(0,0)$ is a saddle point.
  • At $(\pm 1, 0)$: Characteristic equation: $$ \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -4 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_\pm = \pm 2i. $$ Solving $(J(\pm 1, 0) - (\pm 2i)I)\vec\eta_\pm = \vec 0$: $$ \begin{pmatrix} \mp 2i & 1 \\ -4 & \mp 2i \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \eta_1 \\ \eta_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$ Row 1 gives $\mp 2i\eta_1 + \eta_2 = 0 \implies \eta_2 = \pm 2i\eta_1$ (Row 2 consistent). Choosing $\eta_1 = \pm i$ gives $\eta_2 = -2$, so $$ \vec\eta_\pm = \begin{pmatrix} \pm i \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \pm i\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$ Both $(\pm 1, 0)$ are linearized centres; because $V(\pm 1) = -\frac{1}{2}$ are strict local minima of $V(x)$ ($V''(\pm 1) = 4 > 0$), the closed energy contours confirm that both are true nonlinear centres.
(iii) Sextic potential

Characteristic equation at $(0,0)$:

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -4 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda_\pm = \pm 2i. $$

As in part (ii), the complex eigenvectors are $\vec\eta_\pm = \begin{pmatrix} \pm i \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$. Because $V'(x) = x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4)$ has the same sign as $x$ everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, $V(x)$ has a strict global minimum at $x = 0$ with $V(0) = 0$. Therefore $(0,0)$ is a true nonlinear centre.

Step 4: Global picture

We trace the constant-energy curves $y = \pm\sqrt{2(E - V(x))}$ across all energy levels $E$ and interpret the physical motion of the particle.

(i) Simple harmonic oscillations

For every $E > 0$, the level curve $\frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2 = E$ is the ellipse

$$ \frac{x^2}{2E/\omega^2} + \frac{y^2}{2E} = 1, $$

traversed clockwise (since $\dot x = y > 0$ in the upper half-plane). Physically, the particle executes periodic simple harmonic oscillations about $x = 0$ with amplitude $A = \sqrt{2E}/\omega$ and constant period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ independent of energy.

(ii) Double-well dynamics and homoclinic separatrix

The potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$ has two symmetric minima at $x = \pm 1$ with depth $V(\pm 1) = -\frac{1}{2}$, separated by a central barrier of height $V(0) = 0$:

  • $E = -\frac{1}{2}$: The particle sits at rest at the bottom of either potential well, $(-1, 0)$ or $(1, 0)$.
  • $-\frac{1}{2} < E < 0$ (low-energy trapped oscillations): The particle lacks enough energy to cross the central barrier at $x = 0$; trajectories form two disjoint families of closed clockwise orbits oscillating inside either the left well ($x < 0$) or the right well ($x > 0$).
  • $E = 0$ (figure-eight separatrix): The level curve $\frac{1}{2}y^2 + \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2 = 0$ simplifies to $y = \pm x\sqrt{2 - x^2}$, forming two homoclinic loops that leave and return to the saddle $(0,0)$ in infinite time, with turning points at $x = \pm\sqrt{2}$.
  • $E > 0$ (high-energy oscillations): The particle overcomes the central barrier and executes large periodic clockwise oscillations enclosing both wells and the saddle.

(iii) Single-well anharmonic oscillations

Consequently, every energy level $E > 0$ produces a single closed clockwise periodic orbit enclosing $(0,0)$: every non-stationary trajectory is a bounded, periodic anharmonic oscillation about the origin.

Exercise 5

Particle on a Curved Wire

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Bead on Wire • Arc-Length Kinetic Energy
Statement

For the functions $V(x)$ in parts (i) and (ii) of the previous exercise, write down the kinetic energy of a particle of unit mass moving on a wire whose height as a function of $x$ is $V(x)$. Working in physical units for which the acceleration of gravity is $g = 1$, write down the total energy, and hence derive the second order differential equation satisfied by $x$. Write down a coupled system of equations for $x$ and $y = \dot x$, and sketch the phase portrait.

Setting up the system

First, we express the speed of the bead along the wire in terms of $x$ and $\dot x$, build the total mechanical energy $E$, and differentiate $E(t) = \text{const}$ to obtain the equation of motion and its equivalent first-order system.

A particle constrained to move without friction on a planar wire of height $z = V(x)$ has position vector $\mathbf r(t) = \bigl(x(t),\, V(x(t))\bigr)$. Differentiating the vertical coordinate $z(t) = V(x(t))$ with respect to time $t$ by the chain rule gives

$$ \dot z = \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[V(x(t))\bigr] = V'(x)\,\dot x. $$

The squared speed along the wire is therefore

$$ v^2 = \dot x^2 + \dot z^2 = \dot x^2 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2 \dot x^2 = \Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\dot x^2. $$

For a particle of unit mass ($m = 1$) in units where $g = 1$, the kinetic energy $T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ and gravitational potential energy $U = mgz$ are

$$ T = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\dot x^2, \qquad U = V(x), $$

so the total mechanical energy is

$$ E(x, \dot x) = T + U = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\dot x^2 + V(x). $$

Since the wire is frictionless and the constraint force is perpendicular to the velocity, $E$ is conserved along every trajectory: $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$. We differentiate $E(x(t), \dot x(t))$ term by term using the product rule and the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\cdot \dot x^2 + \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\cdot \frac{d}{dt}(\dot x^2) + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[V(x)\bigr]. $$

Evaluating each derivative explicitly:

  • $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(1 + [V'(x)]^2\bigr) = 2V'(x)\,V''(x)\,\dot x$, so the first term is $\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2V'(x)V''(x)\dot x \cdot \dot x^2 = V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^3$;
  • $\frac{d}{dt}(\dot x^2) = 2\dot x\ddot x$, so the second term is $\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + [V'(x)]^2\bigr)\cdot 2\dot x\ddot x = \bigl(1 + [V'(x)]^2\bigr)\dot x\ddot x$;
  • $\frac{d}{dt}[V(x)] = V'(x)\dot x$.

Combining the three terms and factoring out $\dot x$:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \dot x\left[\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + V'(x)\right] = \dot x\left[\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)\dot x^2\bigr)\right] = 0. $$

On any time interval where the particle moves ($\dot x \neq 0$), the factor in brackets must vanish (and by continuity of $\ddot x$ it also vanishes at isolated turning points where $\dot x = 0$). Since $1 + [V'(x)]^2 \ge 1 > 0$ everywhere, we divide by $1 + [V'(x)]^2$ to obtain the general second-order ODE:

$$ \ddot x = -\frac{V'(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)\dot x^2\bigr)}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

Introducing the phase-plane variable $y = \dot x$ converts this into the autonomous first-order system

$$ \dot x = f(x, y) = y, \qquad \dot y = g(x, y) = -\frac{V'(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)y^2\bigr)}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

For the two potentials from Exercise 5.4:

  • Part (i): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$ ($\omega > 0$), so $V'(x) = \omega^2 x$ and $V''(x) = \omega^2$. The total energy and coupled system are

$$ E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + \omega^4 x^2\bigr)y^2 + \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2, $$

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -\frac{\omega^2 x\bigl(1 + \omega^2 y^2\bigr)}{1 + \omega^4 x^2}. $$

  • Part (ii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$, so $V'(x) = 2x^3 - 2x = 2x(x^2 - 1)$ and $V''(x) = 6x^2 - 2$. The total energy and coupled system are

$$ E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 + 4x^2(x^2 - 1)^2\Bigr)y^2 + \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2, $$

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -\frac{2x(x^2 - 1)\bigl(1 + (6x^2 - 2)y^2\bigr)}{1 + 4x^2(x^2 - 1)^2}. $$

Step 1: Critical points

Next, we find all equilibrium points by setting $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously, which identifies the stationary positions of the bead on the wire.

Setting $f(x, y) = 0$ gives $y = 0$. Substituting $y = 0$ into $g(x, y) = 0$ yields

$$ -\frac{V'(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)\cdot 0\bigr)}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2} = -\frac{V'(x)}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2} = 0 \iff V'(x) = 0. $$

Thus the equilibria are located at $(x^*, 0)$ where $x^*$ is a stationary point of the wire height $V(x)$ — the exact same critical points as in Exercise 5.4:

  • Part (i): $V'(x) = \omega^2 x = 0 \implies x^* = 0$. Unique critical point: $(0, 0)$.
  • Part (ii): $V'(x) = 2x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \implies x^* \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$. Three critical points: $(-1, 0)$, $(0, 0)$, and $(1, 0)$.
Step 2: Linearization

We now compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x, y)$ by differentiating $f(x, y)$ and $g(x, y)$ explicitly and evaluate it at any equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$ where $V'(x^*) = 0$.

For $f(x, y) = y$, the partial derivatives are $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1$.

For $g(x, y) = -\frac{N(x, y)}{D(x)}$ with numerator $N(x, y) = V'(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)y^2\bigr)$ and denominator $D(x) = 1 + [V'(x)]^2$:

  • Partial derivative with respect to $x$ by the quotient rule:

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -\frac{\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,D(x) - N(x, y)\,D'(x)}{\bigl[D(x)\bigr]^2}, $$

where

$$ \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = V''(x)\bigl(1 + V''(x)y^2\bigr) + V'(x)V'''(x)y^2, \qquad D'(x) = 2V'(x)V''(x). $$

Evaluating at any equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$, where $V'(x^*) = 0$ and $y = 0$: $N(x^*, 0) = 0$, $D(x^*) = 1 + 0 = 1$, $D'(x^*) = 0$, and $\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}(x^*, 0) = V''(x^*)$. Hence

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x^*, 0) = -\frac{V''(x^*)\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0}{1^2} = -V''(x^*). $$

  • Partial derivative with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -\frac{2V'(x)V''(x)y}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

Evaluating at $(x^*, 0)$ gives $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(x^*, 0) = 0$.

Therefore, at every equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$ of the curved-wire system, the Jacobian matrix is

$$ J(x^*, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

This coincides identically with the Jacobian of the planar potential system $\ddot x = -V'(x)$ in Exercise 5.4, because the geometric factor $[V'(x)]^2$ and the curvature term $V'(x)V''(x)y^2$ are both quadratic or higher order in the perturbations $(x - x^*, y)$.

Step 3: Local study

We now find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $J(x^*, 0)$ at each critical point and determine whether the linear classification persists in the full nonlinear system.

Part (i): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$ at $(0, 0)$

Here $V''(0) = \omega^2$, so

$$ J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Characteristic polynomial:

$$ \det\bigl(J(0, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + \omega^2 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \pm i\omega. $$

Linearization predicts a centre (non-hyperbolic, $\operatorname{Re}\lambda = 0$). To confirm it is a true nonlinear centre, observe that $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + \omega^4 x^2)y^2 + \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$ is conserved ($\dot E = 0$), satisfies $E(0, 0) = 0$, and satisfies $E(x, y) > 0$ for all $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$. Hence every level curve $E(x, y) = c > 0$ is a closed loop enclosing $(0, 0)$, proving that $(0, 0)$ is a global nonlinear centre.

Part (ii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$
  • At $(0, 0)$: $V''(0) = -2$, so

$$ J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Characteristic polynomial:

$$ \det\bigl(J(0, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 2 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 2 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = \sqrt{2}, \quad \lambda_2 = -\sqrt{2}. $$

Row-by-row eigenvectors:

  • For $\lambda_1 = \sqrt{2}$: $(J - \sqrt{2}I)\mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -\sqrt{2} & 1 \\ 2 & -\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies -\sqrt{2}\,v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}$ (unstable direction, slope $+\sqrt{2}$).
  • For $\lambda_2 = -\sqrt{2}$: $(J + \sqrt{2}I)\mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 1 \\ 2 & \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies \sqrt{2}\,v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -\sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}$ (stable direction, slope $-\sqrt{2}$).

Since $\lambda_1 > 0 > \lambda_2$, $(0, 0)$ is a hyperbolic saddle; by the Hartman–Grobman theorem, the nonlinear trajectories near $(0, 0)$ are tangent to $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$.

  • At $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$: $V''(\pm 1) = 6(\pm 1)^2 - 2 = 4$, so

$$ J(\pm 1, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Characteristic polynomial:

$$ \det\bigl(J(\pm 1, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -4 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \pm 2i. $$

Because $V(\pm 1) = -\frac{1}{2}$ is a strict local minimum of $V(x)$ and $\frac{1}{2}(1 + [V'(x)]^2)y^2 \ge 0$, the conserved energy $E(x, y)$ has a strict local minimum $E = -\frac{1}{2}$ at both $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$. Consequently, the level curves $E(x, y) = c$ for $-\frac{1}{2} < c < 0$ are nested closed loops around each minimum, proving that both $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ are nonlinear centres.

Step 4: Global picture

We now trace the global energy contours $E(x, y) = c$ and compare the geometry of the wire trajectories against the flat-potential orbits of Exercise 5.4.

In both parts, solving $E(x, y) = c$ for $y$ yields the exact orbit equation

$$ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2\bigl(c - V(x)\bigr)}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}}. $$

Since $\dot x = y$, every trajectory moves to the right in the upper half-plane ($y > 0$) and to the left in the lower half-plane ($y < 0$), so closed orbits run clockwise. The denominator $\sqrt{1 + [V'(x)]^2} \ge 1$ compresses $|y|$ relative to the flat-potential curve $y_{\text{flat}} = \pm\sqrt{2(c - V(x))}$ wherever the wire is steep ($|V'(x)|$ large), because part of the kinetic energy goes into the vertical velocity $\dot z = V'(x)y$.

  • Part (i) ($V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\omega^2 x^2$): Every energy level $c > 0$ gives a closed periodic orbit oscillating between $x = -\frac{\sqrt{2c}}{\omega}$ and $x = +\frac{\sqrt{2c}}{\omega}$:

$$ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2c - \omega^2 x^2}{1 + \omega^4 x^2}}. $$

Near the origin ($|x| \ll \omega^{-2}$), the contours are ellipses, just like the harmonic oscillator. At large amplitudes ($c \gg \omega^{-2}$), the denominator $1 + \omega^4 x^2$ pinches the contours vertically along the flanks, flattening the maximum horizontal velocity $|y|$ compared to the ellipses of Exercise 5.4(i).

  • Part (ii) ($V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$): The qualitative topology is a double-well figure-eight, controlled by the saddle energy $E(0, 0) = 0$:
  • $-\frac{1}{2} < E < 0$: two disjoint families of closed clockwise orbits trapped inside the left valley around $(-1, 0)$ and the right valley around $(1, 0)$.
  • $E = 0$: the homoclinic separatrix forming a figure-eight through the saddle $(0, 0)$, given explicitly by

$$ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2x^2 - x^4}{1 + 4x^2(x^2 - 1)^2}}, \qquad x \in [-\sqrt{2},\, \sqrt{2}], $$

crossing $(0, 0)$ with slopes $\pm\sqrt{2}$ and turning vertically at $x = \pm\sqrt{2}$ where $V(\pm\sqrt{2}) = 0$.

  • $E > 0$: large clockwise periodic orbits enclosing all three equilibria, flattened vertically where $|V'(x)| = |2x(x^2 - 1)|$ is large.
Phase portrait of the bead on the double-well wire $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$ (Exercise 5.5(ii)). Centres at $(\pm 1, 0)$, saddle at $(0, 0)$ with slopes $\pm\sqrt{2}$, homoclinic separatrix $E = 0$ reaching $x = \pm\sqrt{2}$, and large periodic orbits for $E > 0$ pinched vertically where the wire slope $|V'(x)|$ is steep.

Exercise 6

Damped Oscillators in One-Dimensional Potentials

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Damped Oscillators • Basins of Attraction
Statement

Write down the equation of motion for a particle of unit mass moving in each of the potentials in Exercise 4 above, when there is an additional damping force $-\dot x$ (in part (i) take $\omega = 1$). Writing down a coupled system of equations for $x$ and $y = \dot x$, sketch the phase portrait for each case.

Setting up the system

First, we write Newton's second law with both the conservative force $-V'(x)$ and the linear damping force $-\dot x$, convert it to a first-order system, and compute the rate of change of the mechanical energy $E$.

For a particle of unit mass ($m = 1$) in a potential $V(x)$ subject to a linear damping force $-\dot x$, Newton's second law gives

$$ \ddot x = -V'(x) - \dot x. $$

Setting $y = \dot x$ produces the coupled first-order system

$$ \dot x = f(x, y) = y, \qquad \dot y = g(x, y) = -V'(x) - y. $$

The mechanical energy of the undamped system is $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + V(x)$. Differentiating $E(x(t), y(t))$ along any solution by the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\dot y = V'(x)\cdot y + y\cdot\bigl(-V'(x) - y\bigr) = V'(x)y - V'(x)y - y^2 = -y^2 \le 0. $$

Thus $E$ is non-increasing along every trajectory, and $\dot E = 0$ only on the $x$-axis ($y = 0$). Whenever $y = 0$ and $V'(x) \neq 0$, we have $\dot y = -V'(x) \neq 0$, so trajectories cross the $x$-axis vertically without remaining on it. Consequently, $E(t)$ decreases strictly along every non-equilibrium orbit: no closed orbits (periodic solutions) can exist in any of the three cases.

Writing out the explicit system for each potential of Exercise 5.4:

  • Part (i): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^2$ (taking $\omega = 1$), $V'(x) = x$, $V''(x) = 1$:

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -x - y. $$

  • Part (ii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$, $V'(x) = 2x^3 - 2x = 2x(x^2 - 1)$, $V''(x) = 6x^2 - 2$:

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -2x^3 + 2x - y. $$

  • Part (iii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$ (the potential printed on the official sheet), $V'(x) = 3x^5 - 5x^3 + 4x = x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4)$, $V''(x) = 15x^4 - 15x^2 + 4$:

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -3x^5 + 5x^3 - 4x - y. $$

Step 1: Critical points

Next, we find all equilibria by setting $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously.

Setting $\dot x = y = 0$ and substituting into $\dot y = -V'(x) - y = 0$ gives $V'(x) = 0$. Damping does not shift the positions of the critical points:

  • Part (i): $x = 0 \implies$ unique equilibrium $(0, 0)$.
  • Part (ii): $2x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \implies$ three equilibria: $(-1, 0)$, $(0, 0)$, and $(1, 0)$.
  • Part (iii): $x(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 4) = 0$. Setting $u = x^2 \ge 0$, the quadratic $3u^2 - 5u + 4 = 0$ has discriminant $\Delta = (-5)^2 - 4(3)(4) = 25 - 48 = -23 < 0$, so it has no real roots. Thus the unique equilibrium is $(0, 0)$.

(Note: in Robinson's textbook, Exercise 34.1(iii) uses $V(x) = \frac{1}{6}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$, whose derivative $x(x^2 - 1)(x^2 - 4)$ gives five equilibria at $x \in \{0, \pm 1, \pm 2\}$. Here we solve the official sheet potential $\frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$ and comment on Robinson's triple-well variant at the end.)

Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x, y)$ in general and evaluate it at every equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$.

Differentiating $f(x, y) = y$ and $g(x, y) = -V'(x) - y$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -V''(x), \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -1. $$

Hence at any critical point $(x^*, 0)$,

$$ J(x^*, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Its characteristic polynomial is

$$ \det\bigl(J(x^*, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -1 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (-\lambda)(-1 - \lambda) - (-V''(x^*)) = \lambda^2 + \lambda + V''(x^*) = 0, $$

with roots

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4V''(x^*)}}{2}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We now evaluate $\lambda_\pm$ (and eigenvectors where real) at every critical point of parts (i), (ii), and (iii).

Part (i): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^2$ at $(0, 0)$

Here $V''(0) = 1$, so $J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and

$$ \lambda^2 + \lambda + 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4}}{2} = \frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}. $$

Since $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm = -\frac{1}{2} < 0$ and $\operatorname{Im}\lambda_\pm \neq 0$, $(0, 0)$ is a stable spiral (underdamped linear harmonic oscillator). At $(0, 1)$, $\dot x = 1 > 0$, so trajectories wind clockwise into the origin.

Part (ii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^4 - x^2$
  • At $(0, 0)$: $V''(0) = -2$, so $J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Characteristic equation:

$$ \lambda^2 + \lambda - 2 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 2) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = -2. $$

Row-by-row eigenvectors:

  • For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $(J - I)\mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies -v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (unstable manifold, slope $+1$).
  • For $\lambda_2 = -2$: $(J + 2I)\mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies 2v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$ (stable manifold, slope $-2$).

Since $\lambda_1 = 1 > 0 > -2 = \lambda_2$, $(0, 0)$ is a hyperbolic saddle.

  • At $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$: $V''(\pm 1) = 6(\pm 1)^2 - 2 = 4$, so $J(\pm 1, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Characteristic equation:

$$ \lambda^2 + \lambda + 4 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 16}}{2} = \frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt{15}}{2}. $$

Since $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm = -\frac{1}{2} < 0$, both $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ are hyperbolic stable spirals (clockwise, since $\dot x = y > 0$ above the $x$-axis).

Part (iii): $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^6 - \frac{5}{4}x^4 + 2x^2$ at $(0, 0)$

Here $V''(0) = 4$, so $J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and

Thus $(0, 0)$ is a hyperbolic stable spiral (clockwise).

Damped Particle on a Curved Wire

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Damped Wire • Qualitative Equivalence
Statement

A particle of unit mass moves on a wire whose height as a function of $x$ is $V(x)$, and is subject to an additional damping force $-k\dot x$. Write down the equation of motion, and show that the behavior of this system is qualitatively the same as that of $\ddot x = -V'(x) - k\dot x$.

Setting up the system

First, we use the work-energy balance for a unit-mass particle ($m = 1, g = 1$) on the wire $z = V(x)$ under the horizontal damping force $-k\dot x$ ($k > 0$) to derive the equation of motion and its coupled first-order system.

From Exercise 5.5, the total mechanical energy of the bead on the wire $z = V(x)$ is

$$ E(x, \dot x) = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\dot x^2 + V(x). $$

The rate of change of mechanical energy equals the power delivered by the non-conservative damping force $F_d = -k\dot x$:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = F_d\,\dot x = -k\dot x^2. $$

Differentiating $E(x(t), \dot x(t))$ explicitly by the product and chain rules (exactly as in Exercise 5.5):

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\dot x\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^3 + V'(x)\dot x. $$

Equating the two expressions for $\frac{dE}{dt}$:

$$ \dot x\left[\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + V'(x)\right] = -k\dot x^2. $$

Rearranging all terms to the left-hand side and factoring out $\dot x$:

$$ \dot x\left[\Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + V'(x) + k\dot x\right] = 0. $$

On any interval where $\dot x \neq 0$ (and by continuity at isolated points where $\dot x = 0$), the bracket vanishes, giving the second-order equation of motion:

$$ \Bigl(1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2\Bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + k\dot x + V'(x) = 0, $$

or, dividing by $1 + [V'(x)]^2 \ge 1 > 0$,

$$ \ddot x = -\frac{V'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + k\dot x}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

Setting $y = \dot x$ yields the coupled first-order system (which we call System W for the wire):

$$ \text{(W)} \qquad \dot x = f(x, y) = y, \qquad \dot y = g(x, y) = -\frac{V'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)y^2 + ky}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

We are asked to prove that System (W) is qualitatively equivalent to the flat damped-potential system (System F):

$$ \text{(F)} \qquad \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -V'(x) - ky. $$

Step 1: Critical points

First, we show that Systems (W) and (F) have the exact same critical points in the phase plane.

For System (W), $\dot x = 0 \implies y = 0$. Substituting $y = 0$ into $\dot y = 0$ gives

$$ -\frac{V'(x) + 0 + 0}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2} = 0 \iff V'(x) = 0. $$

For System (F), $\dot x = 0 \implies y = 0$, and $\dot y = 0 \implies -V'(x) = 0 \iff V'(x) = 0$.

Therefore, both systems share the identical set of equilibria: $(x^*, 0)$ where $V'(x^*) = 0$.

Step 2: Linearization

Next, we compute the Jacobian matrix $J_W(x, y)$ of System (W) explicitly using the quotient rule and evaluate it at every equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$, showing that it coincides identically with the Jacobian $J_F(x^*, 0)$ of System (F).

Write $g(x, y) = -\frac{N(x, y)}{D(x)}$ where

$$ N(x, y) = V'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)y^2 + ky, \qquad D(x) = 1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2. $$

We compute each partial derivative explicitly:

  1. For $f(x, y) = y$: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1$.
  2. For $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$, apply the quotient rule:

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -\frac{\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,D(x) - N(x, y)\,D'(x)}{\bigl[D(x)\bigr]^2}, $$

where

$$ \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = V''(x) + \Bigl(\bigl[V''(x)\bigr]^2 + V'(x)V'''(x)\Bigr)y^2, \qquad D'(x) = 2V'(x)V''(x). $$

Evaluating at any critical point $(x^*, 0)$ (where $V'(x^*) = 0$ and $y = 0$):

  • $N(x^*, 0) = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$,
  • $D(x^*) = 1 + 0 = 1$,
  • $D'(x^*) = 0$,
  • $\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}(x^*, 0) = V''(x^*) + 0 = V''(x^*)$.

Hence

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x^*, 0) = -\frac{V''(x^*)\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0}{1^2} = -V''(x^*). $$

  1. For $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}$, note that $D(x)$ does not depend on $y$:

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -\frac{2V'(x)V''(x)y + k}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

Evaluating at $(x^*, 0)$ (where $V'(x^*) = 0$ and $y = 0$):

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}(x^*, 0) = -\frac{0 + k}{1 + 0} = -k. $$

Assembling the Jacobian of System (W) at $(x^*, 0)$:

$$ J_W(x^*, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -k \end{pmatrix}. $$

Meanwhile, for System (F), $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-V'(x) - ky) = -V''(x)$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-V'(x) - ky) = -k$, so

$$ J_F(x^*, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -k \end{pmatrix} = J_W(x^*, 0). $$

The two linearizations are identical at every equilibrium point.

Step 3: Local study

Because $J_W(x^*, 0) = J_F(x^*, 0)$, both systems have the exact same characteristic equation, eigenvalues, and eigenvectors at every equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$:

$$ \det\bigl(J(x^*, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -k - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + k\lambda + V''(x^*) = 0, $$

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - 4V''(x^*)}}{2}. $$

For any $k > 0$ and any non-degenerate stationary point ($V''(x^*) \neq 0$), every equilibrium is hyperbolic ($\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm \neq 0$):

  • Local maximum of $V$ ($V''(x^*) < 0$): $\sqrt{k^2 - 4V''(x^*)} > k$, so $\lambda_+ > 0 > \lambda_-$ — a saddle in both systems. Row 1 of $(J - \lambda_\pm I)\mathbf v_\pm = \mathbf 0$ reads $-\lambda_\pm v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_\pm = (1, \lambda_\pm)^T$, giving identical stable and unstable tangent slopes $\lambda_-$ and $\lambda_+$.
  • Local minimum of $V$ ($V''(x^*) > 0$): both eigenvalues have strictly negative real part ($\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm < 0$):
  • $0 < k < 2\sqrt{V''(x^*)}$: stable spiral (underdamped);
  • $k = 2\sqrt{V''(x^*)}$: stable improper node (critically damped, $\lambda = -k/2$);
  • $k > 2\sqrt{V''(x^*)}$: stable node (overdamped).

By the Hartman–Grobman theorem, the local phase portraits of (W) and (F) around every non-degenerate equilibrium are topologically conjugate (identical type, stability, and tangent directions).

Step 4: Global picture

To extend the equivalence from local neighbourhoods to the entire phase plane, we compare the global energy/Lyapunov structure and direction of flow of both systems.

  1. Identical energy dissipation law: For System (W), $E_W(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + [V'(x)]^2)y^2 + V(x)$ satisfies $\dot E_W = -ky^2 \le 0$. For System (F), $E_F(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + V(x)$ satisfies $\dot E_F = -ky^2 \le 0$. Both energy functions have the exact same critical points and strict local minima (the minima of $V(x)$ on $y = 0$), and both satisfy $E \to +\infty$ whenever $|y| \to \infty$ or $V(x) \to +\infty$.
  2. No periodic orbits and identical LaSalle invariance: In both systems, $\dot E = 0$ if and only if $y = 0$. On the $x$-axis $y = 0$, the vertical acceleration is - in (W): $\dot y = -\frac{V'(x)}{1 + [V'(x)]^2}$, - in (F): $\dot y = -V'(x)$, which have the exact same sign everywhere (since $1 + [V'(x)]^2 \ge 1 > 0$) and vanish only at equilibria $V'(x^*) = 0$. Therefore, no non-equilibrium trajectory can stay on $y = 0$; $E(t)$ decreases strictly along every non-equilibrium orbit, ruling out limit cycles and homoclinic/heteroclinic loops in both systems.
  3. Same flow direction and basin structure: In both systems $\dot x = y$ (motion to the right for $y > 0$, to the left for $y < 0$), and every bounded trajectory must converge to an equilibrium point (generically a local minimum of $V$, with the one-dimensional stable manifolds of the saddles separating the basins of attraction). The factor $1 + [V'(x)]^2$ in System (W) only compresses the horizontal speed $|y|$ geometrically where the wire is steep, without altering the number, type, stability, or connectivity of the equilibria.

Hence the global phase portrait of the damped bead on the wire (W) is qualitatively identical to that of the flat damped-potential system (F).

Answers and checks

We verify the limiting cases and dimensional consistency of the equation of motion.

  • Undamped limit ($k \to 0^+$): Setting $k = 0$ in System (W) gives $\dot y = -\frac{V'(x)(1 + V''(x)y^2)}{1 + [V'(x)]^2}$ and $\dot E_W = 0$, recovering Exercise 5.5 identically.
  • Small-slope limit ($|V'(x)| \ll 1$, $|V''(x)y^2| \ll 1$): Setting $1 + [V'(x)]^2 \approx 1$ and neglecting $V'(x)V''(x)y^2$ reduces System (W) directly to System (F), $\ddot x = -V'(x) - k\dot x$.
  • Dimensional check (SI units): Restoring $m = 1\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $g$ ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$), the damping constant $k$ has units of $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (since $F_d = -mk\dot x$ has units $\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}$) and the restored ODE is

$$ \ddot x = -\frac{gV'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + k\dot x}{1 + \bigl[V'(x)\bigr]^2}. $$

Every term in the numerator has units of $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ ($[gV'] = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $[V'V''\dot x^2] = 1\cdot\mathrm{m}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $[k\dot x] = \mathrm{s}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$), and the denominator is dimensionless, matching $[\ddot x] = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

Key results
  • Equation of motion (damped bead on wire): $\ddot x = -\dfrac{V'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)\dot x^2 + k\dot x}{1 + [V'(x)]^2}$, equivalent to $\dot x = y$, $\dot y = -\dfrac{V'(x) + V'(x)V''(x)y^2 + ky}{1 + [V'(x)]^2}$.
  • Proof of qualitative equivalence with $\ddot x = -V'(x) - k\dot x$: 1. Identical equilibria: $(x^*, 0)$ with $V'(x^*) = 0$. 2. Identical Jacobian at every equilibrium: $J_W(x^*, 0) = J_F(x^*, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -k \end{pmatrix}$, giving the same eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and local stability/type. 3. Identical global Lyapunov dissipation: $\dot E_W = -ky^2 \le 0$ and $\dot E_F = -ky^2 \le 0$, with $\dot y$ having the same sign on $y = 0$, ruling out periodic orbits and preserving the basin-of-attraction topology.

Simple Undamped Pendulum

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Simple Pendulum • Separatrix • Librations & Rotations
Statement

Consider the equation for a simple pendulum of unit frequency $\omega = 1$ that was derived and studied in Chapter 3, $\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\sin\theta$. Convert this single second order equation into a first order system and sketch the phase portrait. Discuss the physical behavior of the system taking into account that the total energy is conserved.

Setting up the system

First, we convert the second-order pendulum ODE into an autonomous first-order system using the angular position $\theta$ and angular velocity $v = \dot\theta$ (matching the notation of Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, pp. 10–12), and we verify explicitly that the total mechanical energy $E$ is conserved.

Defining $v = \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \dot\theta$, we have $\dot v = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\sin\theta$, so the equivalent coupled first-order system is

$$ \dot\theta = f(\theta, v) = v, \qquad \dot v = g(\theta, v) = -\sin\theta. $$

Since the right-hand side is $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$, the entire phase portrait is $2\pi$-periodic along the $\theta$-axis.

In dimensionless units ($\omega^2 = g/L = 1$, $m = 1$, $L = 1$), the kinetic energy is $T = \frac{1}{2}v^2$ and the gravitational potential energy (measured from the horizontal level of the pivot) is $V(\theta) = -\cos\theta$, so the total mechanical energy is

$$ E(\theta, v) = \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \cos\theta. $$

Differentiating $E(\theta(t), v(t))$ along any trajectory by the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial\theta}\dot\theta + \frac{\partial E}{\partial v}\dot v = (\sin\theta)\cdot v + v\cdot(-\sin\theta) = v\sin\theta - v\sin\theta = 0. $$

Therefore $E(\theta(t), v(t)) = E_0 = \text{const}$ along every solution: every trajectory lies on a level curve $\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \cos\theta = E_0$.

Step 1: Critical points

Next, we locate all equilibrium points by setting $\dot\theta = 0$ and $\dot v = 0$ simultaneously.

$$ \begin{cases} \dot\theta = v = 0, \\ \dot v = -\sin\theta = 0 \implies \theta = m\pi, \quad m \in \mathbb{Z}. \end{cases} $$

There is an infinite periodic array of critical points along the $\theta$-axis:

$$ (\theta^*, v^*) = (m\pi,\, 0), \qquad m \in \mathbb{Z}. $$

Physically, the even points $(2n\pi, 0)$ represent the pendulum hanging straight downward at rest, while the odd points $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ represent the pendulum balanced straight upward at rest.

Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(\theta, v)$ by taking all four partial derivatives of $f(\theta, v) = v$ and $g(\theta, v) = -\sin\theta$.

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial v} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial\theta} = -\cos\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial v} = 0. $$

Thus

$$ J(\theta, v) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

At $(m\pi, 0)$ we have $\cos(m\pi) = (-1)^m$, so the Jacobian alternates between two matrices depending on the parity of $m$:

$$ J(2n\pi,\, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J\bigl((2n+1)\pi,\, 0\bigr) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We now classify the two families of equilibria via their eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and energy contours.

Even equilibria: $(2n\pi, 0)$ (hanging downward)

Characteristic polynomial of $J(2n\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$:

$$ \det\bigl(J(2n\pi, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \pm i. $$

The linearized system predicts a centre (purely imaginary eigenvalues, non-hyperbolic, so Hartman–Grobman alone is inconclusive). To confirm that $(2n\pi, 0)$ is a true nonlinear centre, note that the conserved energy $E(\theta, v) = \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \cos\theta$ attains a strict local minimum $E(2n\pi, 0) = -1$ at each even equilibrium (since $V(2n\pi) = -1$ and $V''(2n\pi) = \cos(2n\pi) = 1 > 0$). Hence for $-1 < E < 1$ the level curves $E(\theta, v) = \text{const}$ are nested closed loops surrounding $(2n\pi, 0)$, proving that every $(2n\pi, 0)$ is a nonlinear centre (Lyapunov stable).

Odd equilibria: $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ (balanced upright)

Characteristic polynomial of $J((2n+1)\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$:

$$ \det\Bigl(J\bigl((2n+1)\pi, 0\bigr) - \lambda I\Bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = -1. $$

Row-by-row eigenvectors:

  • For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $(J - I)\mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_\theta \\ v_v \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies -v_\theta + v_v = 0 \implies \mathbf v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (unstable direction, slope $+1$).
  • For $\lambda_2 = -1$: $(J + I)\mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_\theta \\ v_v \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies v_\theta + v_v = 0 \implies \mathbf v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ (stable direction, slope $-1$).

Since $\lambda_1 = 1 > 0 > -1 = \lambda_2$, every odd equilibrium $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ is a hyperbolic saddle (unstable).

Step 4: Global picture

We solve the energy conservation equation $\frac{1}{2}v^2 - \cos\theta = E$ for $v$ to classify all global trajectories and their physical meaning.

Solving for $v$:

$$ v = \pm\sqrt{2(E + \cos\theta)}. $$

Since $\dot\theta = v$, trajectories move to the right in the upper half-plane ($v > 0$) and to the left in the lower half-plane ($v < 0$). Because $\cos\theta \in [-1, 1]$, real motion requires $E \ge -1$, and the global phase portrait splits into three distinct physical regimes separated by the saddle energy $E((2n+1)\pi, 0) = 0 - (-1) = 1$:

  1. Librations (oscillations), $-1 < E < 1$: Here $E + \cos\theta \ge 0$ only on bounded intervals $|\theta - 2n\pi| \le \theta_{\max}$ where $\cos\theta_{\max} = -E$. Each energy level gives a closed, clockwise periodic orbit around $(2n\pi, 0)$: the pendulum swings back and forth between $-\theta_{\max}$ and $+\theta_{\max}$ without ever reaching the top. Near $(2n\pi, 0)$ ($E \approx -1$), $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{1}{2}\theta^2$, so the orbits are nearly circles $v^2 + (\theta - 2n\pi)^2 \approx 2(E + 1)$.
  2. Heteroclinic separatrix, $E = 1$: Substituting $E = 1$ and using the half-angle identity $1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$:

$$ v = \pm\sqrt{2(1 + \cos\theta)} = \pm\sqrt{4\cos^2(\theta/2)} = \pm 2\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \quad \text{(with sign alternating across each odd } m\pi\text{)}. $$

These curves are heteroclinic orbits connecting adjacent saddles $((2n-1)\pi, 0)$ and $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$, entering and leaving each saddle with slopes $\pm 1$. Physically, the pendulum has the exact energy needed to swing up and approach the upright position asymptotically as $t \to +\infty$ (taking infinite time to reach it, since an equilibrium cannot be reached in finite time by uniqueness of solutions).

  1. Rotations (whirling motion), $E > 1$: Here $E + \cos\theta \ge E - 1 > 0$ for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, so $v$ never vanishes. The phase curves are wavy, unbounded $2\pi$-periodic graphs running purely to the right ($v > 0$, continuous rotation in one direction) or purely to the left ($v < 0$, continuous rotation in the opposite direction). The speed $|v|$ is largest at the bottom ($\theta = 2n\pi$, $|v| = \sqrt{2(E + 1)}$) and smallest at the top ($\theta = (2n+1)\pi$, $|v| = \sqrt{2(E - 1)}$).

Exercise 9

Damped Pendulum

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Damped Pendulum • Energy Dissipation • Bifurcation at k = 2
Statement

The first order system

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -\sin x - ky $$

describes a more realistic situation in which the simple pendulum considered in the previous exercise is subject to a damping force with constant $k > 0$. Sketch the phase portrait and discuss the physical behavior of the system taking into account that the total energy is not conserved.

Setting up the system

First, we relate the variables $(x, y)$ to the physical pendulum coordinates and compute the time derivative of the mechanical energy $E(x, y)$ to quantify energy dissipation.

Here $x = \theta$ is the angular displacement and $y = \dot\theta = v$ is the angular velocity (matching Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, pp. 12–14). The system is

$$ \dot x = f(x, y) = y, \qquad \dot y = g(x, y) = -\sin x - ky, \qquad k > 0, $$

which is $2\pi$-periodic in $x$. Taking the undamped mechanical energy $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x$ and differentiating along any solution by the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\dot y = (\sin x)\cdot y + y\cdot(-\sin x - ky) = y\sin x - y\sin x - ky^2 = -ky^2 \le 0. $$

Because $k > 0$, $\dot E \le 0$ everywhere, and $\dot E = 0$ if and only if $y = 0$. On the $x$-axis ($y = 0$), $\dot y = -\sin x$, which is non-zero except at the equilibria $x = m\pi$. Therefore no non-equilibrium trajectory can remain on $y = 0$, so $E(t)$ decreases strictly along every non-equilibrium orbit: neither closed librations nor perpetual rotations can exist.

Step 1: Critical points

Next, we find all equilibria by setting $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously.

$$ \begin{cases} \dot x = y = 0, \\ \dot y = -\sin x - k\cdot 0 = -\sin x = 0 \implies x = m\pi, \quad m \in \mathbb{Z}. \end{cases} $$

Damping leaves the equilibrium positions unchanged: $(m\pi, 0)$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ (even $m = 2n$ hanging downward; odd $m = 2n+1$ balanced upright).

Step 2: Linearization

We compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x, y)$ explicitly and evaluate it at the even and odd equilibria.

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -\cos x, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -k, $$

so

$$ J(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x & -k \end{pmatrix}. $$

Evaluating at $x = m\pi$ where $\cos(m\pi) = (-1)^m$:

$$ J(2n\pi,\, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -k \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J\bigl((2n+1)\pi,\, 0\bigr) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -k \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We now find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of both matrices and identify the bifurcation in local type at $k = 2$.

Even equilibria: $(2n\pi, 0)$ (hanging downward)

Characteristic polynomial of $J(2n\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -k \end{pmatrix}$:

$$ \det\bigl(J(2n\pi, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -1 & -k - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (-\lambda)(-k - \lambda) - (-1) = \lambda^2 + k\lambda + 1 = 0. $$

Applying the quadratic formula:

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - 4}}{2}. $$

Every root has strictly negative real part for all $k > 0$, so every $(2n\pi, 0)$ is hyperbolic and asymptotically stable, with a qualitative transition (bifurcation) at the critical damping value $k = 2$:

  • Underdamped ($0 < k < 2$): $k^2 - 4 < 0$, giving a complex conjugate pair with negative real part:

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm i\sqrt{4 - k^2}}{2}. $$

Each $(2n\pi, 0)$ is a stable spiral (clockwise, since $\dot x = y > 0$ for $y > 0$). For instance, in the lecturer's example $k = \sqrt{2}$ (Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, p. 13), $\lambda_\pm = \frac{-\sqrt{2} \pm i\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Physically, the pendulum oscillates back and forth around the bottom with exponentially decaying amplitude.

  • Critically damped ($k = 2$): $k^2 - 4 = 0$, giving a repeated negative eigenvalue $\lambda = -1$ (algebraic multiplicity $2$). Checking the eigenspace:

$$ (J + I)\mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

There is only a one-dimensional eigenspace (geometric multiplicity $1$), so each $(2n\pi, 0)$ is a stable improper node (degenerate node) with unique tangent slope $-1$.

  • Overdamped ($k > 2$): $k^2 - 4 > 0$ and $\sqrt{k^2 - 4} < k$, giving two distinct negative real eigenvalues $\lambda_- < \lambda_+ < 0$ with eigenvectors

$$ (J - \lambda_\pm I)\mathbf v_\pm = \begin{pmatrix} -\lambda_\pm & 1 \\ -1 & -k - \lambda_\pm \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf 0 \implies \mathbf v_\pm = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \lambda_\pm \end{pmatrix}. $$

Each $(2n\pi, 0)$ is a stable node: the pendulum returns to the bottom without oscillating, approaching tangent to the slower eigendirection $\mathbf v_+ = (1, \lambda_+)^T$.

Odd equilibria: $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ (balanced upright)

Characteristic polynomial of $J((2n+1)\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -k \end{pmatrix}$:

$$ \det\Bigl(J\bigl((2n+1)\pi, 0\bigr) - \lambda I\Bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -k - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + k\lambda - 1 = 0, $$

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 + 4}}{2}. $$

Because $\sqrt{k^2 + 4} > \sqrt{k^2} = k > 0$, we have $\lambda_+ > 0$ and $\lambda_- < 0$ for every $k > 0$ (with $|\lambda_-| > \lambda_+$, since $\lambda_+ + \lambda_- = -k < 0$). Row-by-row eigenvectors from $(J - \lambda_\pm I)\mathbf v_\pm = \mathbf 0$:

$$ -\lambda_\pm v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_+ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \lambda_+ \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{unstable, slope } \lambda_+ > 0), \qquad \mathbf v_- = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \lambda_- \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{stable, slope } \lambda_- < 0). $$

Hence every odd equilibrium $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ is a hyperbolic saddle for all $k > 0$.

Step 4: Global picture

Combining the local classification with the strict energy decay $\dot E = -ky^2 \le 0$ determines the global dynamics completely:

  • Breaking of the heteroclinic separatrix: In the undamped pendulum ($k = 0$), a trajectory leaving $((2n-1)\pi, 0)$ along its unstable manifold had $E = 1$ and arrived at the next saddle $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$. With $k > 0$, energy decreases strictly along the unstable manifold, so it drops below $E = 1$ and cannot reach the next hilltop; instead, it falls into the basin of $(2n\pi, 0)$ (for small $k$, spiralling into $(2n\pi, 0)$).
  • Basins of attraction as diagonal strips: The two branches of the stable manifold of each saddle $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ extend backward in time toward $|y| \to \infty$ without intersecting any other trajectory, forming parallel curved boundaries that partition the entire $(x, y)$-plane into an infinite sequence of $2\pi$-shifted basins of attraction, one for each downward equilibrium $(2n\pi, 0)$.
  • Physical interpretation of large initial velocities: A pendulum launched with large angular speed ($E \gg 1$) initially rotates over the top several times, losing energy $\Delta E = -k\int y^2\,dt$ on every revolution. Eventually $E$ drops below $1$, the pendulum can no longer pass the upright position, and it gets trapped in one of the basins, settling asymptotically to $(2n\pi, 0)$ (where $n$ is the integer number of full revolutions completed before stopping). Only a measure-zero set of initial conditions — those lying exactly on the stable manifold of a saddle $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ — comes to rest balanced upright as $t \to +\infty$.

Answers and checks

We check the undamped limit, the lecturer's numerical case, and dimensional consistency.

  • Undamped limit ($k \to 0^+$): As $k \to 0^+$, $\dot E = -ky^2 \to 0$, $\lambda_\pm(2n\pi, 0) = \frac{-k \pm i\sqrt{4-k^2}}{2} \to \pm i$, and $\lambda_\pm((2n+1)\pi, 0) = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2+4}}{2} \to \pm 1$, recovering Exercise 5.8 smoothly.
  • Cross-check with class notes (Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, pp. 12–14): For $k = \sqrt{2} < 2$, the even equilibria have $\lambda_\pm = \frac{-\sqrt{2} \pm i\sqrt{2}}{2}$ (stable spirals) and the odd equilibria have $\lambda_\pm = \frac{-\sqrt{2} \pm \sqrt{6}}{2}$ (saddles), matching the notes identically.
  • Dimensional check (SI units): Restoring $\omega^2 = g/L$ ($\mathrm{s}^{-2}$) and damping rate $\gamma$ ($\mathrm{s}^{-1}$), the equation is $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\sin\theta - \gamma\dot\theta$. Every term has units of $\mathrm{s}^{-2}$ ($[\gamma\dot\theta] = \mathrm{s}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{s}^{-1} = \mathrm{s}^{-2}$). The dimensionless damping ratio is $k = \gamma/\omega$, so the critical damping condition $k = 2$ corresponds to $\gamma = 2\omega$.
Key results
  • Energy dissipation: $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x \implies \dot E = -ky^2 \le 0$ (strictly decreasing off equilibria; no periodic orbits).
  • Even equilibria $(2n\pi, 0)$ ($\lambda^2 + k\lambda + 1 = 0$, $\lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - 4}}{2}$):
  • $0 < k < 2$: stable spiral (underdamped);
  • $k = 2$: stable improper node ($\lambda = -1$, single eigenvector $(1, -1)^T$, critically damped);
  • $k > 2$: stable node (overdamped).
  • Odd equilibria $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ ($\lambda^2 + k\lambda - 1 = 0$, $\lambda_\pm = \frac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 + 4}}{2}$): hyperbolic saddles for all $k > 0$.
  • Global dynamics: Every rotating trajectory loses energy until captured by a basin of attraction of some $(2n\pi, 0)$; the stable manifolds of the saddles partition the phase plane into these basins.
Exercise 10

Pendulum with Quadratic Damping

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Quadratic Drag • Nonlinear Spiral • Lyapunov Energy
Statement

Consider the equation for a pendulum with a quadratic damping term

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -\sin x - ky|y|. $$

Show that if $E = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x$, then

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = -ky^2|y|, $$

and hence that, while the point $(0, 0)$ is a center for the linearized equation, it behaves like a stable spiral for the nonlinear equation. Sketch the phase diagram for the latter.

Setting up the system

First, we examine the quadratic aerodynamic drag term $-ky|y|$ ($k > 0$) and derive the exact energy dissipation rate $\frac{dE}{dt}$ term by term using the chain rule.

The autonomous first-order system is

$$ \dot x = f(x, y) = y, \qquad \dot y = g(x, y) = -\sin x - ky|y|, \qquad k > 0. $$

Note that $y|y|$ always has the same sign as $y$ (since $|y| \ge 0$), so $-ky|y|$ always opposes the angular velocity $y$, modelling turbulent aerodynamic drag proportional to the square of the speed.

For the mechanical energy $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x$, we compute the partial derivatives explicitly:

$$ \frac{\partial E}{\partial x} = \sin x, \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial y} = y. $$

Differentiating $E(x(t), y(t))$ along any trajectory by the chain rule:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\dot y = (\sin x)\cdot y + y\cdot\bigl(-\sin x - ky|y|\bigr). $$

Expanding the product and cancelling the opposite terms $y\sin x$ and $-y\sin x$:

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = y\sin x - y\sin x - ky^2|y| = -ky^2|y| \le 0. $$

This proves the required energy dissipation identity.

Step 1: Critical points

Next, we find all equilibrium points by setting $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$ simultaneously.

$$ \begin{cases} \dot x = y = 0, \\ \dot y = -\sin x - k\cdot 0\cdot |0| = -\sin x = 0 \implies x = m\pi, \quad m \in \mathbb{Z}. \end{cases} $$

As in Exercises 5.8 and 5.9, the equilibria are $(m\pi, 0)$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ (even $m = 2n$ hanging downward; odd $m = 2n+1$ balanced upright).

Step 2: Linearization

To compute the Jacobian matrix $J(x, y)$ rigorously, we first differentiate the non-polynomial function $\phi(y) = y|y|$ explicitly on all three domains ($y > 0$, $y < 0$, and $y = 0$).

Writing $\phi(y) = y|y|$ piecewise:

  • For $y > 0$: $\phi(y) = y^2$, so $\phi'(y) = 2y = 2|y|$.
  • For $y < 0$: $\phi(y) = -y^2$, so $\phi'(y) = -2y = 2|y|$.
  • At $y = 0$: from the definition of the derivative,

$$ \phi'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\phi(h) - \phi(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{h|h| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}|h| = 0 = 2|0|. $$

Therefore $\phi(y) = y|y|$ is continuously differentiable ($C^1$) everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, with $\frac{d}{dy}(y|y|) = 2|y|$ for all $y \in \mathbb{R}$.

Computing all four partial derivatives of $f(x, y) = y$ and $g(x, y) = -\sin x - ky|y|$:

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = -\cos x, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = -2k|y|. $$

Thus the Jacobian matrix at any point $(x, y)$ is

$$ J(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x & -2k|y| \end{pmatrix}. $$

At every equilibrium $(m\pi, 0)$, we have $y = 0$ so $-2k|0| = 0$: the quadratic damping vanishes completely from the linearization! Hence

$$ J(2n\pi,\, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J\bigl((2n+1)\pi,\, 0\bigr) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3: Local study

We now classify the linearized equilibria and then use the Lyapunov/energy function $E(x, y)$ to resolve the non-hyperbolic even equilibria.

Odd equilibria: $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ (balanced upright)

For $J((2n+1)\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, expanding the characteristic determinant gives

$$ \det\Bigl(J\bigl((2n+1)\pi, 0\bigr) - \lambda I\Bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (-\lambda)(-\lambda) - 1\cdot 1 = \lambda^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 1,\ \lambda_2 = -1. $$

Solving $(J - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ row by row: for $\lambda_1 = 1$, Row 1 gives $-v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_1 = (1, 1)^T$; for $\lambda_2 = -1$, Row 1 gives $v_x + v_y = 0 \implies \mathbf v_2 = (1, -1)^T$. Because $\lambda_1 > 0 > \lambda_2$, every odd equilibrium is hyperbolic, so the Hartman–Grobman theorem guarantees that each $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ is a saddle in the nonlinear system, with local stable and unstable manifolds tangent to slopes $-1$ and $+1$.

Even equilibria: $(2n\pi, 0)$ (in particular $(0, 0)$)

For $J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic polynomial is

$$ \det\bigl(J(0, 0) - \lambda I\bigr) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_\pm = \pm i. $$

Hence $(0, 0)$ is a centre for the linearized system $\dot x = y, \dot y = -x$. Because $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm = 0$, $(0, 0)$ is non-hyperbolic: the Hartman–Grobman theorem does not apply, and nonlinear terms decide the true stability.

We now prove using $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x$ (equivalently, the positive-definite Lyapunov function $V_L(x, y) = E(x, y) + 1 = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + (1 - \cos x)$) that $(0, 0)$ behaves like a nonlinear stable spiral:

  1. Strict local minimum: On the open neighbourhood $|x| < \pi$, $V_L(0, 0) = 0$ and $V_L(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 + (1 - \cos x) > 0$ for all $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$. Its level sets $E(x, y) = c$ ($-1 < c < 1$) are nested closed ovals surrounding $(0, 0)$.
  2. Strict energy decay along orbits: Along any solution, $\dot V_L = \dot E = -ky^2|y| \le 0$, with $\dot E = 0$ if and only if $y = 0$. On the segment $y = 0$ with $0 < |x| < \pi$, the system gives $\dot y = -\sin x \neq 0$, so trajectories cross the $x$-axis vertically and can never remain on $y = 0$ except at $(0, 0)$. Therefore $E(t)$ decreases strictly along every non-equilibrium trajectory inside the well $-1 < E < 1$.
  3. Inward winding (nonlinear spiral): Whenever $y \neq 0$, the trajectory crosses the closed $E$-contours strictly from outside to inside. Meanwhile, the vector field winds clockwise around $(0, 0)$ ($\dot x = y > 0$ for $y > 0$, $\dot x < 0$ for $y < 0$, $\dot y < 0$ for $x \in (0, \pi)$ and $y = 0$, $\dot y > 0$ for $x \in (-\pi, 0)$ and $y = 0$). By LaSalle's invariance principle, every trajectory starting with $E < 1$ and $|x| < \pi$ winds clockwise infinitely many times while approaching $E = -1$, i.e. $(x(t), y(t)) \to (0, 0)$ as $t \to +\infty$.

By $2\pi$-periodicity, every even equilibrium $(2n\pi, 0)$ is an asymptotically stable nonlinear spiral (also called a weak focus).

Step 4: Global picture

Globally, the phase portrait shares the same topological structure as the underdamped linearly damped pendulum of Exercise 5.9, with two notable quantitative differences driven by the quadratic drag law $-ky|y|$:

  • Strong braking at high speeds: For $|y| \gg 1$, $\bigl|y|y|\bigr| = y^2 \gg |y|$, so rotating trajectories lose energy much more rapidly than under linear damping and quickly drop below the saddle energy $E = 1$.
  • Very slow decay near $(2n\pi, 0)$: As a trajectory approaches $(2n\pi, 0)$, $|y| \to 0$ and the drag force $ky^2$ becomes negligibly small compared to the linear restoring force $-x$. Consequently, the spiral turns become tightly packed near $(2n\pi, 0)$, with amplitude decaying algebraically ($O(1/t)$) rather than exponentially ($O(e^{-kt/2})$).
  • Basins of attraction: Just as in Exercise 5.9, the stable manifolds of the saddles $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ (now entering each saddle with exact slopes $\pm 1$, since $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ at $y = 0$) partition the entire phase plane into $2\pi$-periodic basins of attraction, one for each nonlinear stable spiral $(2n\pi, 0)$.

Answers and checks

We verify the undamped limit and dimensional consistency.

  • Undamped limit ($k \to 0^+$): Setting $k = 0$ gives $\dot E = 0$, turning the tightly wound nonlinear spirals at $(2n\pi, 0)$ back into the true closed centres of Exercise 5.8 while leaving the saddle Jacobians at $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$ unchanged.
  • Dimensional check (SI units): Restoring $\omega^2 = g/L$ ($\mathrm{s}^{-2}$) and the dimensionless quadratic drag constant $c_q$, the equation reads $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\sin\theta - c_q\dot\theta|\dot\theta|$. Here $[\dot\theta|\dot\theta|] = (\mathrm{s}^{-1})^2 = \mathrm{s}^{-2} = [\ddot\theta]$, so $c_q$ (and therefore $k$ in $\omega = 1$ units) is dimensionless (proportional to $\frac{\rho_{\text{air}} C_D A L}{2m}$). Key results
  • Energy dissipation: For $E(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \cos x$, $\dfrac{dE}{dt} = (\sin x)y + y(-\sin x - ky|y|) = -ky^2|y| \le 0$.
  • Linearization: Since $\frac{d}{dy}(y|y|) = 2|y|$, $J(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x & -2k|y| \end{pmatrix}$. At $(2n\pi, 0)$, $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ ($\lambda = \pm i$, linear centre — non-hyperbolic). At $((2n+1)\pi, 0)$, $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ ($\lambda = \pm 1$, $\mathbf v_\pm = (1, \pm 1)^T$, saddles).
  • Nonlinear behaviour at $(0, 0)$: $E(x, y)$ has a strict local minimum $E = -1$ at $(0, 0)$ and $\dot E = -ky^2|y| \le 0$ vanishes only on $y = 0$, where $\dot y = -\sin x \neq 0$ for $0 < |x| < \pi$. Hence $E$ decreases strictly along every non-equilibrium orbit, forcing trajectories to spiral clockwise into $(0, 0)$ (nonlinear stable spiral with algebraic decay as $y \to 0$).
