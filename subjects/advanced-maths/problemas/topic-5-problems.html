Exercise 1

Linearisation Failure at a Centre

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf
Linearisation failure at a centre
Statement

The linearized system may not give a qualitatively correct picture when the linearized equation produces a center, as this example demonstrates. First show that the origin is a center for the linearized version of the system $\dot x = -y + \lambda x(x^2 + y^2)$, $\dot y = x + \lambda y(x^2 + y^2)$. Write down the equation satisfied by $r$, where $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, and show that the stability of the origin depends actually on the sign of $\lambda$. Draw the phase portrait for $\lambda < 0$.

Model Classification and Stationary Points

The system is autonomous, two-dimensional and nonlinear (cubic terms). Write $\dot x = f(x,y)$, $\dot y = g(x,y)$ with

$$ f(x,y) = -y + \lambda x(x^2+y^2), \qquad g(x,y) = x + \lambda y(x^2+y^2). $$

Stationary points

A stationary point solves $f = g = 0$. Multiply $f=0$ by $x$ and $g=0$ by $y$ and add:

$$ x f + y g = -xy + \lambda x^2(x^2+y^2) + xy + \lambda y^2(x^2+y^2) = \lambda (x^2+y^2)^2 . $$

For $\lambda \neq 0$ this vanishes only at $x=y=0$. Multiply $f=0$ by $y$ and $g=0$ by $x$ and subtract:

$$ x g - y f = x^2 + \lambda xy(x^2+y^2) + y^2 - \lambda xy(x^2+y^2) = x^2+y^2 , $$

which vanishes only at the origin for every $\lambda$. Hence $(0,0)$ is the only stationary point, for any value of $\lambda$.

Linearisation and Local Eigenvalues

Method: Jacobian at the stationary point, then trace and determinant (Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, linearisation and classification; Robinson, Sec. 33).

Jacobian entries

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lambda (x^2+y^2) + 2\lambda x^2 = \lambda(3x^2+y^2), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -1 + 2\lambda xy, $$

$$ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 1 + 2\lambda xy, \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = \lambda (x^2+y^2) + 2\lambda y^2 = \lambda(x^2+3y^2). $$

At $(0,0)$ every term containing $\lambda$ vanishes:

$$ J(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Trace, determinant, eigenvalues

$$ \tau = \operatorname{tr}J = 0, \qquad \Delta = \det J = 0\cdot 0 - (-1)(1) = 1 > 0, $$

$$ \mu^2 - \tau\mu + \Delta = \mu^2 + 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu = \pm i . $$

Purely imaginary eigenvalues ($\tau = 0$, $\Delta > 0$) give a centre of the linearised system $\dot u = -v$, $\dot v = u$: circles $u^2+v^2=\text{const}$ traversed counter-clockwise (at $(u,v)=(1,0)$ the velocity is $(0,1)$). This is the borderline case of the linearisation theorem, so it cannot decide the stability of the nonlinear system: the cubic terms must be examined.

Global Phase Portrait and Interpretation

Equation for $r$

Differentiate $r^2 = x^2+y^2$ with respect to $t$ using the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dt}(r^2) = 2r\dot r$:

$$ 2r\dot r = 2x\dot x + 2y\dot y = 2x\left[-y + \lambda x r^2\right] + 2y\left[x + \lambda y r^2\right]. $$

Expand: $-2xy + 2\lambda x^2 r^2 + 2xy + 2\lambda y^2 r^2 = 2\lambda r^2 (x^2+y^2) = 2\lambda r^4$. Hence $2r\dot r = 2\lambda r^4$ and, for $r>0$,

$$ \boxed{\dot r = \lambda r^3 .} $$

Equation for the angle

With $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$, $\dot\theta = \dfrac{x\dot y - y\dot x}{x^2+y^2}$. From the stationary-point computation $x\dot y - y\dot x = x g - y f = r^2$, so

$$ \boxed{\dot\theta = 1,} $$

independent of $\lambda$: every orbit turns counter-clockwise at unit angular speed.

Solving $\dot r = \lambda r^3$

Separate variables, with $r(0)=r_0>0$:

$$ \int_{r_0}^{r}\frac{d\rho}{\rho^3} = \lambda\int_0^t ds \;\Longrightarrow\; \left[-\frac{1}{2\rho^2}\right]_{r_0}^{r} = -\frac{1}{2r^2} + \frac{1}{2r_0^2} = \lambda t $$

(Barrow's rule with the limits written). Solving for $r$:

$$ r(t) = \frac{r_0}{\sqrt{1 - 2\lambda r_0^2\, t}} . $$

(i) $\lambda < 0$

Put $\lambda = -|\lambda|$: $r(t) = r_0/\sqrt{1 + 2|\lambda| r_0^2 t}$ is decreasing, and $r(t)\to 0$ like $(2|\lambda| t)^{-1/2}$ as $t\to\infty$ (algebraic, not exponential, decay: the linearisation has $\operatorname{Re}\mu=0$). With $\dot\theta=1$ the orbits are inward counter-clockwise spirals: the origin is an asymptotically stable spiral (a stable focus), not a centre.

(ii) $\lambda > 0$

$r(t) = r_0/\sqrt{1 - 2\lambda r_0^2 t}$ grows and becomes infinite at the finite time $t^\ast = 1/(2\lambda r_0^2)$. Orbits are outward counter-clockwise spirals; the origin is an unstable spiral.

(iii) $\lambda = 0$

$\dot r = 0$, $\dot\theta=1$: circles $r=r_0$, a genuine centre, stable but not asymptotically stable.

Conclusion

The linearisation is identical ($\mu=\pm i$) for all $\lambda$, but the nonlinear origin is asymptotically stable for $\lambda<0$, unstable for $\lambda>0$ and a centre only for $\lambda=0$. The sign of the cubic coefficient decides.

Dimensional and consistency check

$\lambda r^3$ has the units of $\dot r$, so $[\lambda] = [\mathrm{L}^{-2}\mathrm{T}^{-1}]$ if $r$ is a length and $t$ a time; the unit angular speed in $\dot\theta=1$ is $[\mathrm{T}^{-1}]$. Limit $\lambda\to0$: $r(t)\to r_0$, recovering the circles.

Phase portrait for $\lambda<0$: counter-clockwise spirals winding into the origin (asymptotically stable focus), with $\dot r=\lambda r^3<0$ and $\dot\theta=1$.

Seven Two-Species Population Models

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf
Seven two-species models
Statement

For each of the following models of two population species, describe first the type of situation being modelled, then find the stationary points, determine their stability type and sketch the phase portrait for $x, y \ge 0$. Decide what the phase portrait means for the evolution of the two species.

(i) $\dot x = x(2 - x - y),\ \dot y = y(2 - 2y - 2x)$ (ii) $\dot x = x(2 - x - y),\ \dot y = y(2 - 2y - x/4)$ (iii) $\dot x = x(2 - x - 3y),\ \dot y = y(2 - 2y - 3x)$ (iv) $\dot x = x(1 - 2y),\ \dot y = y(-2 + 3x)$ (v) $\dot x = x(4 - x/2 - 3y),\ \dot y = y(-2 + x)$ (vi) $\dot x = x(10 - x - 3y),\ \dot y = y(1 + x - 10y)$ (vii) $\dot x = x(3 - x - y),\ \dot y = y(-2 + x)$

Related worked material

Models (i) and (ii) are also worked in Solved_exer_nonlinear_system.pdf and in Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf (competition models and their classification by trace and determinant). The computations below are repeated in full here.

Model Classification and Stationary Points

General method

Each system has the form $\dot x=x\,P(x,y)$, $\dot y=y\,Q(x,y)$ with $P,Q$ affine. Both axes are invariant ($x=0\Rightarrow\dot x=0$), so orbits starting in $x,y>0$ never leave the open quadrant. Stationary points come in four types: $(0,0)$; $P=0$ on $y=0$; $Q=0$ on $x=0$; and $P=Q=0$ in the interior. The Jacobian is

$$ J=\begin{pmatrix}P+xP_x & xP_y\\ yQ_x & Q+yQ_y\end{pmatrix}. $$

At a stationary point on an axis one of $P,Q$ may be non-zero and $J$ is triangular, at the interior point $P=Q=0$ and $J=\begin{pmatrix}xP_x&xP_y\\yQ_x&yQ_y\end{pmatrix}$. Classification by $\tau=\operatorname{tr}J$, $\Delta=\det J$ and $\tau^2-4\Delta$ (Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf).

(i) Competition without coexistence

$x$ and $y$ each have a positive growth rate and each is reduced by itself and by the other (negative cross terms): competition. Nullclines $\dot x=0$: $x+y=2$; $\dot y=0$: $x+y=1$ (parallel). Stationary points: $(0,0)$, $y=0\Rightarrow(2,0)$, $x=0\Rightarrow 2-2y=0\Rightarrow(0,1)$; interior: $x+y=2$ and $x+y=1$ are incompatible, none.

(ii) Competition with coexistence

Same signs of coefficients. Nullclines: $x+y=2$ and $2y+x/4=2$. Stationary points $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,1)$; interior: $x=2-y$ in $2y+\tfrac14(2-y)=2$ gives $8y+2-y=8$, $y=\tfrac67$, $x=\tfrac87$: $(\tfrac87,\tfrac67)$.

(iii) Strong competition

Nullclines: $x+3y=2$ and $3x+2y=2$. Stationary points $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,1)$; interior: $x=2-3y$ in $3x+2y=2$ gives $6-9y+2y=2$, $y=\tfrac47$, $x=2-\tfrac{12}7=\tfrac27$: $(\tfrac27,\tfrac47)$.

Check of the interior point

Verification: $x+3y=\tfrac27+\tfrac{12}7=2$ and $3x+2y=\tfrac67+\tfrac87=2$. The interior point is $(\tfrac27,\tfrac47)$ (the coordinates $(\tfrac47,\tfrac27)$ fail the second equation: $\tfrac{12}7+\tfrac47\ne2$).

(iv) Predator-prey without limits (Lotka-Volterra)

$x$ is the prey ($\dot x=x(1-2y)$: grows alone at rate 1, killed by $y$), $y$ the predator ($\dot y=y(-2+3x)$: dies alone at rate 2, fed by $x$). No self-limitation. Nullclines $y=\tfrac12$ and $x=\tfrac23$. Stationary points: $(0,0)$ and $(\tfrac23,\tfrac12)$ (on the axes $\dot x=0$, $y=0$ gives only the origin).

(v) Predator-prey, logistic prey

$\dot x=x(4-\tfrac x2-3y)$: prey with carrying capacity $8$ ($4-\tfrac x2=0$), eaten by $y$; $\dot y=y(-2+x)$: predator, no alternative food, needs $x>2$ to grow. Stationary points: $(0,0)$, $(8,0)$; $x=0\Rightarrow\dot y=-2y=0$ gives no new point; interior: $x=2$, $4-1-3y=0$, $y=1$: $(2,1)$.

(vi) Predator-prey, predator with alternative food

$\dot y=y(1+x-10y)$: the predator grows at rate 1 even without prey and is self-limited (carrying capacity $\tfrac1{10}$ on the axis), increased by $x$. Stationary points: $(0,0)$; $(10,0)$ ($10-x=0$); $(0,\tfrac1{10})$ ($1-10y=0$); interior: $x=10-3y$ in $1+x-10y=0$: $11-13y=0$, $y=\tfrac{11}{13}$, $x=10-\tfrac{33}{13}=\tfrac{97}{13}$: $(\tfrac{97}{13},\tfrac{11}{13})$.

(vii) Predator-prey, logistic prey (carrying capacity 3)

$\dot x=x(3-x-y)$, $\dot y=y(-2+x)$. Stationary points: $(0,0)$, $(3,0)$, interior $x=2$, $3-2-y=0$, $y=1$: $(2,1)$.

Exercise 3

Two Cooperative Species Models

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Two cooperative species
Statement

The situation in which two species cooperate, so that the presence of one enhances the environment for the other, can be modelled by a coupled pair of equations of the form $\dot x = x(A - ax + by)$, $\dot y = y(B + cx - dy)$, where all the parameters are positive. Sketch the phase portraits for the following cooperative equations: (i) $\dot x = x(1 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(1 + x - 2y)$; (ii) $\dot x = x(2 - x + y)$, $\dot y = y(4 + 2x - y)$.

Model Classification and Stationary Points

General model

Write $\dot x = x(A-ax+by)$, $\dot y = y(B+cx-dy)$ with $A,B,a,b,c,d>0$. Each species grows alone ($A,B>0$), limits itself ($a,d>0$) and is helped by the other ($b,c>0$: $\partial\dot x/\partial y = bx>0$, $\partial\dot y/\partial x = cy>0$): mutualism. Only $x,y\ge 0$ is meaningful; both axes are invariant ($x=0\Rightarrow\dot x=0$, $y=0\Rightarrow\dot y=0$).

Stationary points

Four cases: $(0,0)$; $x$-axis point $(A/a,0)$; $y$-axis point $(0,B/d)$; interior point from the nullclines

$$ ax - by = A,\qquad -cx + dy = B . $$

Cramer's rule gives $x^\ast = \dfrac{Ad+bB}{ad-bc}$, $y^\ast = \dfrac{aB+cA}{ad-bc}$, with denominator $ad-bc$. For positive parameters the numerators are positive, so the interior point lies in the first quadrant iff $ad-bc>0$ (self-limitation stronger than mutual help).

Jacobian in general

$$ J = \begin{pmatrix} A-2ax+by & bx\\ cy & B+cx-2dy\end{pmatrix}. $$

Linearisation and Local Eigenvalues
(i) $\dot x = x(1-x+y)$, $\dot y = y(1+x-2y)$

Here $A=a=b=B=c=1$, $d=2$, so $ad-bc = 2-1 = 1>0$.

Statement

Model Classification and Stationary Points

Energy and the first-order system

For unit mass the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot x^2$, so $E=\tfrac12 y^2+V(x)$ with $y=\dot x$. Conservation, with the chain rule $\frac{d}{dt}V(x(t))=V'(x)\dot x$:

$$ \dot x=y,\qquad \dot y=-V'(x). $$

General classification

Stationary points: $y=0$ and $V'(x)=0$. The Jacobian is

$$ J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-V''(x)&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \tau=0,\ \Delta=V''(x). $$

Statement

Working in physical units for which the acceleration of gravity is $g = 1$, write down the total energy, and hence derive the second order differential equation satisfied by $x$. Write down a coupled system of equations for $x$ and $y = \dot x$, and sketch the phase portrait.

Model Classification and Stationary Points

Kinetic and total energy

The particle sits at $(x,\,V(x))$ and is constrained to the wire, so one degree of freedom, $x$. Velocity: $(\dot x,\ \tfrac{d}{dt}V(x))=(\dot x,\ V'(x)\dot x)$ by the chain rule. For unit mass

$$ T=\tfrac12\left(\dot x^2+V'(x)^2\dot x^2\right)=\tfrac12\big(1+[V'(x)]^2\big)\dot x^2 . $$

Potential energy $mgh$ with $m=g=1$ and $h=V(x)$: $E=T+V(x)$.

Equation of motion from $dE/dt=0$

Differentiate $T$ with the product and chain rules, $\frac{d}{dt}[V'(x)^2]=2V'V''\dot x$:

$$ \frac{dT}{dt}=\tfrac12\cdot2V'V''\dot x\cdot\dot x^2+\big(1+V'^2\big)\dot x\ddot x=V'V''\dot x^3+\big(1+V'^2\big)\dot x\ddot x,\qquad \frac{dV}{dt}=V'\dot x. $$

Add and set to zero:

$$ 0=\dot x\Big[\big(1+V'^2\big)\ddot x+V'V''\dot x^2+V'\Big]. $$

Dividing by $\dot x\ne0$ (the bracket must vanish, also by continuity when $\dot x=0$):

$$ \boxed{\ddot x=-\frac{V'(x)\big(1+V''(x)\dot x^2\big)}{1+[V'(x)]^2}.} $$

Coupled system, $y=\dot x$:

$$ \dot x=y,\qquad \dot y=-\frac{V'(x)\,\big(1+V''(x)\,y^2\big)}{1+[V'(x)]^2}. $$

Stationary points

$y=0$ and $V'(x)\big(1+V''\cdot0\big)=V'(x)=0$ (the denominator is $\ge1$). The stationary points are exactly those of Exercise 4, as expected: the wire is in equilibrium where it is horizontal.

Write $\dot y=F(x,y)=-V'N/D$ with $N=1+V''y^2$, $D=1+V'^2$. At a point with $V'=0$, $y=0$ (so $N=D=1$):

$$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=-V''\frac ND-V'\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\Big(\frac ND\Big)=-V'',\qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=-V'\frac{2V''y}{D}=0. $$

Hence

$$ J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-V''(x_0)&0\end{pmatrix}, $$

identical to Exercise 4: eigenvalues $\pm i\sqrt{V''}$ (centre) when $V''>0$, $\pm\sqrt{-V''}$ (saddle) when $V''<0$. Because $E$ has a strict minimum at the centres, they remain true nonlinear centres.

Damped Motion in Three Potential Wells

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Damped motion in three potential wells
Statement

Write down the equation of motion for a particle of unit mass moving in each of the potentials in Exercise 4 above, when there is an additional damping force $-\dot x$ (in part (i) take $\omega = 1$). Writing down a coupled system of equations for $x$ and $y = \dot x$, sketch the phase portrait for each case.

Equation of Motion and Energy Dissipation

For a unit mass in a potential $V(x)$ with the extra force $-\dot x$, Newton's second law gives

$$ \ddot x = -V'(x) - \dot x . $$

Setting $y = \dot x$ gives $\dot y = \ddot x$, so the coupled first order system is

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -V'(x) - y . $$

The total energy is $E(x,y) = \tfrac12 y^2 + V(x)$. Its derivative along a trajectory follows from the chain rule, $\dot E = \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\dot y$:

$$ \dot E = V'(x)\,y + y\,\bigl(-V'(x) - y\bigr) = -y^2 \le 0 . $$

Dissipation and the LaSalle argument

$\dot E = -y^2$ vanishes only on the line $y = 0$. On that line the second equation gives $\dot y = -V'(x)$, so a trajectory can stay on $y=0$ only where $V'(x) = 0$, i.e. at an equilibrium. By LaSalle's invariance principle, every bounded trajectory therefore tends to an equilibrium. No periodic orbit exists, since one would need $\dot E \equiv 0$ along a closed curve, which forces $y \equiv 0$. Energy is lost, so the closed level curves of the conservative Exercise 4 open into spirals.

The Jacobian of the system at an equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$ with $V'(x^*) = 0$ is

$$ J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr} J = -1, \quad \det J = V''(x^*), $$

with characteristic equation $\lambda^2 + \lambda + V''(x^*) = 0$ and roots $\lambda = \dfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4V''(x^*)}}{2}$. Source: Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, linearisation about an equilibrium; Robinson, Sec. 34.

(i) $V(x) = \tfrac12 x^2$ (with $\omega = 1$)

Here $V'(x) = x$ and $V''(x) = 1$, so $\ddot x = -x - \dot x$ and

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = -x - y . $$

The only equilibrium is $(0,0)$. With $V''(0) = 1$,

$$ J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda^2 + \lambda + 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1-4}}{2} = -\frac12 \pm i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}. $$

Complex roots with negative real part: $(0,0)$ is a stable spiral, and the system is linear, so the picture is global.

Dimensional check (unit mass, unit damping rate $c/m = 1\,\mathrm{T^{-1}}$): $[E] = [y^2] = \mathrm{L^2 T^{-2}}$ (energy per unit mass) and $[\dot E] = [(c/m)y^2] = \mathrm{L^2 T^{-3}}$ in the nondimensional units of the sheet, consistent with $\dot E = -y^2$.

Exercise 7

Damped Particle on a Wire, Qualitative Equivalence

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 2
Damped particle on a wire
Statement

A particle of unit mass moves on a wire whose height as a function of $x$ is $V(x)$, and is subject to an additional damping force $-k\dot x$. Write down the equation of motion, and show that the behavior of this system is qualitatively the same as that of $\ddot x = -V'(x) - k\dot x$.

Equation of Motion and Energy Dissipation

Kinematics. The particle sits at $\mathbf r = (x, V(x))$ (unit gravity $g = 1$, height equals potential energy per unit mass). By the chain rule $\dot{\mathbf r} = (\dot x,\, V'(x)\dot x)$, so

$$ |\dot{\mathbf r}|^2 = \bigl(1 + [V'(x)]^2\bigr)\dot x^2 . $$

Lagrangian. $L = T - U = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + [V']^2\bigr)\dot x^2 - V(x)$. The pieces of the Euler-Lagrange equation, using the product rule and the chain rule $\frac{d}{dt}[V'(x)]^2 = 2V'V''\dot x$:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x} = (1+[V']^2)\dot x, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x} = (1+[V']^2)\ddot x + 2V'V''\dot x^2, $$

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = V'V''\dot x^2 - V' . $$

Damping. The generalised force of a force $\mathbf F$ is $Q = \mathbf F\cdot\partial\mathbf r/\partial x$, with $\partial\mathbf r/\partial x = (1, V')$.

  • Horizontal drag $\mathbf F = (-k\dot x, 0)$ gives $Q = -k\dot x$ (the form of the statement).
  • Tangential drag $\mathbf F = -k\dot{\mathbf r}$ gives $Q = -k\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\partial\mathbf r/\partial x = -k(1+[V']^2)\dot x$.

Equation $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = Q$ then reads, for horizontal drag,

$$ \boxed{\bigl(1 + [V'(x)]^2\bigr)\ddot x + V'(x)V''(x)\,\dot x^2 = -V'(x) - k\dot x} $$

(for tangential drag the right-hand side is $-V' - k(1+[V']^2)\dot x$; the conclusion below is identical).

Energy. With $E = \tfrac12(1+[V']^2)\dot x^2 + V(x)$, the Euler-Lagrange equation multiplied by $\dot x$ gives

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = \dot x\,Q = -k\dot x^2 \le 0 \quad\text{(horizontal drag)}, \qquad -k(1+[V']^2)\dot x^2 \le 0 \quad\text{(tangential drag)}. $$

Source: Robinson, Sec. 34; Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, conservative systems with damping.

Stationary Points and Linearisation

With $y = \dot x$, solve the equation for $\dot y$:

$$ \dot x = y, \qquad \dot y = \frac{-V'(x) - V'(x)V''(x)\,y^2 - ky}{1 + [V'(x)]^2} . $$

Equilibria. $y = 0$ and then $-V'(x)/(1+[V']^2) = 0$, i.e. $V'(x^*) = 0$. These are exactly the equilibria $(x^*, 0)$ of the reference system $\dot x = y,\ \dot y = -V'(x) - ky$.

Jacobian at $(x^*, 0)$. Write $\dot y = N(x,y)/D(x)$ with $N = -V' - V'V''y^2 - ky$ and $D = 1+[V']^2$. At the equilibrium $N = 0$, so the quotient rule $\partial_\bullet(N/D) = (\partial_\bullet N)/D - N\,\partial_\bullet D/D^2$ loses its second term, and

$$ \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\Big|_{(x^*,0)} = -V''(x^*) - \bigl(V'V''\bigr)'\,y^2\big|_{y=0} = -V''(x^*), \qquad \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\Big|_{(x^*,0)} = -2V'V''y - k = -k, \qquad D(x^*) = 1 . $$

Why $V'(x^*) = 0$ matters

Away from equilibria, differentiating $\dot y$ keeps the $y^2$ and quotient-rule terms in $D(x) = 1 + [V'(x)]^2$. At an equilibrium $(x^*, 0)$, however, $V'(x^*) = 0$ and $y = 0$, so $N = 0$, $D(x^*) = 1$, and all extra terms drop out:

$$ J_{\text{wire}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -V''(x^*) & -k \end{pmatrix} = J_{\ddot x = -V' - k\dot x}. $$

The Jacobians coincide, so the eigenvalues $\lambda = \dfrac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - 4V''(x^*)}}{2}$ coincide, and so does the type of each equilibrium: $V''(x^*) < 0$ saddle; $V''(x^*) > 0$ stable spiral for $k^2 < 4V''$, stable node for $k^2 > 4V''$. By the Hartman-Grobman theorem (hyperbolic cases) the local portraits are topologically equivalent. For tangential drag the lower-right entry is $-k(1+[V']^2)/(1+[V']^2) = -k$ for every $x$, giving the same matrix.

Phase Portrait and Physical Interpretation

Both systems dissipate energy, $\dot E \le 0$, with $\dot E = 0$ only on $y = 0$, where $\dot y \ne 0$ unless $V' = 0$. By LaSalle's principle trajectories tend to equilibria, i.e. to the minima of $V$ (stable) while maxima of $V$ are saddles; the same wells, hills and basins appear in both. The only difference is the metric factor $1+[V']^2$ that distorts the time scale away from equilibria (the particle has to climb along an arc longer than $\Delta x$), which changes the shape of the orbits but not the topology of the portrait.

Conclusion

Both systems share: the same equilibria, the same linearisation at every equilibrium, the same sign of $\dot E$ and the same Lyapunov decay, hence the same qualitative phase portrait as $\ddot x = -V'(x) - k\dot x$.

Exercise 8

Simple Pendulum, Energy Conservation and Phase Portrait

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Simple pendulum phase portrait
Statement

Consider the equation for a simple pendulum of unit frequency $\omega = 1$ that was derived and studied in Chapter 3, $\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\sin\theta$. Convert this single second order equation into a first order system and sketch the phase portrait. Discuss the physical behavior of the system taking into account that the total energy is conserved.

Equation of Motion and Energy Dissipation

With $y = \dot\theta$:

$$ \dot\theta = y, \qquad \dot y = -\sin\theta . $$

The pendulum is a particle in the potential $V(\theta) = -\cos\theta$ (since $V' = \sin\theta$), so $E(\theta, y) = \tfrac12 y^2 - \cos\theta$. Along solutions, by the chain rule,

$$ \dot E = y\,\dot y + \sin\theta\,\dot\theta = y(-\sin\theta) + \sin\theta\,y = 0 . $$

Consequence

$E$ is constant on trajectories (no dissipation, so no attractors and no spirals): each orbit lies on a level curve $\tfrac12 y^2 - \cos\theta = E$, i.e. $y = \pm\sqrt{2(E + \cos\theta)}$, defined where $E + \cos\theta \ge 0$. The portrait is $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$ (the angle $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi$ are the same physical state).

Source: Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, conserved quantities of Hamiltonian systems; Robinson, Sec. 34.

Stationary Points and Linearisation

Equilibria: $y = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 0$, so $(k\pi, 0)$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. The Jacobian of $(y, -\sin\theta)$ is $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos\theta & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

(i) $(2k\pi, 0)$: pendulum hanging down

$\cos(2k\pi) = 1$, so $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, $\lambda = \pm i$: a linear centre. It is non-hyperbolic, so linearisation alone cannot decide. The conserved $E$ decides it: $E = \tfrac12 y^2 - \cos\theta$ has a strict local minimum $E = -1$ at $(2k\pi, 0)$ (Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(\cos\theta, 1) = I$, positive definite), so the level curves $E < 1$ near it are closed curves. Therefore $(2k\pi, 0)$ is a nonlinear centre.

(ii) $((2k+1)\pi, 0)$: pendulum inverted

$\cos((2k+1)\pi) = -1$, so $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda^2 - 1 = 0$, $\lambda = \pm1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)^T$ (unstable) and $(1,-1)^T$ (stable): a saddle.

Phase Portrait and Physical Interpretation

The saddle sits at $E = -\cos\pi = 1$. Its level set $E = 1$ gives $\tfrac12y^2 = 1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$ (half-angle identity), so

$$ E = 1 \iff y = \pm 2\cos\frac{\theta}{2} . $$

This is the separatrix: the curves joining adjacent saddles (heteroclinic orbits).

Damped Simple Pendulum, Energy Dissipation and Regimes

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Damped pendulum regimes
Statement

The first order system $\dot x = y$, $\dot y = -\sin x - ky$ describes a more realistic situation in which the simple pendulum considered in the previous exercise is subject to a damping force with constant $k > 0$. Sketch the phase portrait and discuss the physical behavior of the system taking into account that the total energy is not conserved.

Equation of Motion and Energy Dissipation

With $E = \tfrac12 y^2 - \cos x$ (the energy of Problem - Ch5-P8 Simple Pendulum Energy Conservation and Phase Portrait), the chain rule gives

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = y\,\dot y + \sin x\,\dot x = y(-\sin x - ky) + y\sin x = -ky^2 \le 0 . $$

LaSalle argument

$\dot E = 0$ only on $y = 0$. There $\dot y = -\sin x$, so a trajectory can remain on $y=0$ only if $\sin x = 0$, i.e. at an equilibrium. Hence the largest invariant subset of $\{\dot E = 0\}$ consists of the equilibria. The set $\{E < 1\}$ is a union of bounded lens-shaped pockets, $y^2 < 2(1+\cos x)$ (it forces $|x - 2m\pi| < \pi$), each containing one point $(2m\pi, 0)$. Since $E$ decreases, a trajectory that enters a pocket never leaves it, so it converges to the equilibrium inside it.

Source: Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, damped systems and Lyapunov functions; Robinson, Sec. 34.

Stationary Points and Linearisation

Equilibria: $y=0$, $\sin x=0$, so $(m\pi, 0)$. The Jacobian is $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x & -k \end{pmatrix}$.

(i) $(2m\pi, 0)$: hanging position

$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -k \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda^2 + k\lambda + 1 = 0$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - 4}}{2}$ (trace $-k<0$, determinant $1>0$, so always stable).

Exercise 10

Quadratically Damped Pendulum and Lyapunov Stability

Original statement · probls_ch5_2627.pdf, p. 3
Quadratic damping and Lyapunov stability
Statement

Consider the equation for a pendulum with a quadratic damping term $\dot x = y$, $\dot y = -\sin x - ky|y|$. Show that if $E = \tfrac12 y^2 - \cos x$, then $\dfrac{dE}{dt} = -ky^2|y|$, and hence that, while the point $(0,0)$ is a center for the linearized equation, it behaves like a stable spiral for the nonlinear equation. Sketch the phase diagram for the latter.

Equation of Motion and Energy Dissipation

By the chain rule,

$$ \dot E = \frac{\partial E}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial E}{\partial y}\dot y = \sin x\;y + y\bigl(-\sin x - ky|y|\bigr) = -ky^2|y| = -k|y|^3 \le 0 . $$

Here $y^2|y| = |y|^3$, so $\dot E = 0$ exactly when $y = 0$. Source: Notes03-05-Nov_v2.pdf, Lyapunov functions; Robinson, Sec. 34.

Stationary Points and Linearisation

Equilibria: $y = 0$, $\sin x = 0$, so $(m\pi, 0)$, as in Problem - Ch5-P9 Damped Simple Pendulum Energy Dissipation and Regimes.

Differentiability of $y|y|$

$y|y| = y^2$ for $y\ge0$ and $-y^2$ for $y<0$, so $\frac{d}{dy}(y|y|) = 2y$ for $y>0$ and $-2y$ for $y<0$, i.e. $2|y|$. This is continuous and equals $0$ at $y=0$ (one-sided limits agree), so $y|y|$ is $C^1$ and the Jacobian exists.

$$ J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos x & -2k|y| \end{pmatrix}. $$

At $(0,0)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda = \pm i$: a centre of the linearised equation (non-hyperbolic, so the linearisation does not decide the nonlinear behaviour). At $(\pi, 0)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda=\pm1$, a saddle, as in the undamped and linearly damped pendulum.

Asymptotic stability of $(0,0)$

$E$ is positive definite near the origin up to the constant: $E + 1 = \tfrac12y^2 + (1-\cos x) \ge 0$, vanishing only at $(0,0)$ in the pocket $\{E<1\}$, and $\dot E = -k|y|^3 \le 0$, so $(0,0)$ is Lyapunov stable. If $\dot E = 0$ along a whole trajectory, then $y\equiv0$, so $\dot y = 0$ and $\sin x = 0$, which in the pocket means $x=0$. By LaSalle, every trajectory starting in $\{E<1\}$ (bounded and invariant, since $\dot E\le0$) tends to $(0,0)$: it is asymptotically stable.

Spiral behaviour and its rate

Since $\dot x = y$, orbits turn clockwise (they move right in the upper half plane, left in the lower). They cannot be stopped on $y=0$ (there $\dot y=-\sin x\ne0$ for $x\ne0$), so they cross the $x$-axis infinitely many times while $E$ decreases monotonically: an inward spiral.

Not an exponential spiral

The decay is algebraic, not exponential. Near the origin take $x\approx A\cos t$, $y\approx-A\sin t$ with $E+1\approx A^2/2$. Averaging $\dot E=-k|y|^3$ over one period with $\langle|\sin t|^3\rangle=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin t|^3dt=\frac1{2\pi}\cdot\frac83=\frac4{3\pi}$ gives $A\dot A=-\frac{4k}{3\pi}A^3$, so $\dot A=-\frac{4k}{3\pi}A^2$ and $A(t)=\dfrac{A_0}{1+\frac{4k}{3\pi}A_0t}\sim\dfrac1t$. A linearly damped stable spiral decays like $e^{-kt/2}$; the quadratic drag is too weak at small $|y|$ for that (hence the linearisation sees a centre).

Phase Portrait and Physical Interpretation

Orbits from the pocket around $(0,0)$ spiral clockwise inward, closer together at large $|y|$ (strong drag) and almost circular near the origin (weak drag). Orbits with large energy rotate over the top, lose energy at each turn and are eventually captured in one of the wells $(2m\pi,0)$; the stable manifolds of the saddles $((2m+1)\pi,0)$ separate the basins.
