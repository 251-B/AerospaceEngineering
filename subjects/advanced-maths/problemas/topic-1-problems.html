Problem 1.1

Classification of Ten Fundamental Differential Equations

Taxonomy • Order • Linearity
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: Classify the following equations as ordinary or partial; give their order; and state whether they are linear or nonlinear. In each case identify the dependent and independent variables:

  1. (i) Bessel's equation ($\nu$ is a parameter): $x^2 y'' + x y' + (x^2 - \nu^2)y = 0$
  2. (ii) Burgers' equation ($\nu$ is a parameter): $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$
  3. (iii) Duffing's equation ($m, k, a, b$ are parameters): $m \ddot{x} + k x = a x - b x^3$
  4. (iv) $\frac{dy}{dt} = t - y^2$
  5. (v) The wave equation ($c$ is a parameter): $\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$
  6. (vi) Newton's law of cooling ($k$ is a parameter and $T_A(t)$ is a specified function): $\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_A(t))$
  7. (vii) The logistic population model ($k$ is a parameter): $\frac{dp}{dt} = k p (1 - p)$
  8. (viii) Newton's second law for a particle of mass $m$ moving in a potential $V(x)$: $m \ddot{x} = -V'(x)$
  9. (ix) The coupled equations: $\dot{x} = x(4 - 2x - y), \quad \dot{y} = y(9 - 3x - 3y)$
  10. (x) $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}$, where $\mathbf{x}$ is an $n$-component vector and $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix.
Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation & Criteria

We apply the four foundational mathematical tests established by the UC3M Department of Mathematics:

  • Type (ODE vs PDE): An ODE depends on exactly 1 independent variable; a PDE depends on 2 or more independent variables.
  • Order: The highest derivative index present in the equation.
  • Dependent vs Independent: Independent is the differentiation variable(s); dependent is the unknown state function(s).
  • Linearity (Cátedra Standard): An equation is linear if and only if it is of degree 1 in the dependent variable and all its derivatives, with no mutual products or transcendental compositions: $\sum_{j=0}^n a_j(x) y^{(j)} = b(x)$.
Phase 3: Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

(i) Bessel's equation: $x^2 y'' + x y' + (x^2 - \nu^2)y = 0$
Independent variable: $x$ (single $\implies$ ODE). Dependent variable: $y(x)$.
Derivatives present: $y'$ and $y''$. Highest derivative is order 2 $\implies$ Order 2.
Linearity: Matches $a_2(x)y'' + a_1(x)y' + a_0(x)y = b(x)$ with $a_2(x)=x^2, a_1(x)=x, a_0(x)=x^2-\nu^2, b(x)=0$. All $y, y', y''$ appear linearly $\implies$ Linear.

(ii) Burgers' equation: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0$
Independent variables: $t, x$ (two $\implies$ PDE). Dependent variable: $u(x, t)$.
Highest derivative: $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ $\implies$ Order 2.
Linearity: The convective acceleration term $u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ is a product of the dependent variable and its derivative $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(iii) Duffing's equation: $m \ddot{x} + k x = a x - b x^3$
Overdot signifies time derivative: $\ddot{x} = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}$. Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ ODE. Dependent variable: $x(t)$.
Highest derivative: $\ddot{x}$ $\implies$ Order 2.
Linearity: Rearranging gives $m \ddot{x} + (k - a)x + b x^3 = 0$. If $b \neq 0$, the cubic power $x^3$ violates linearity $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(iv) Riccati Type: $\frac{dy}{dt} = t - y^2$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ ODE. Dependent variable: $y(t)$. Highest derivative: $\frac{dy}{dt}$ $\implies$ Order 1.
Linearity: The term $y^2$ is quadratic in the dependent variable $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(v) Wave Equation: $\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$
Independent variables: $t, x$ $\implies$ PDE. Dependent variable: $y(x, t)$ (displacement).
Highest derivative: $\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}, \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$ $\implies$ Order 2.
Linearity: $\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} - c^2 \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = 0$ is a homogeneous linear differential operator $\implies$ Linear.

(vi) Newton's Law of Cooling: $\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_A(t))$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ ODE. Dependent variable: $T(t)$. Highest derivative: $\frac{dT}{dt}$ $\implies$ Order 1.
Linearity: Rearranges to $\frac{dT}{dt} + kT = k T_A(t)$, matching $a_1(t)\dot{T} + a_0(t)T = b(t)$ $\implies$ Linear.

(vii) Logistic Model: $\frac{dp}{dt} = k p (1 - p)$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ ODE. Dependent variable: $p(t)$. Highest derivative: $\frac{dp}{dt}$ $\implies$ Order 1.
Linearity: Expanding yields $\frac{dp}{dt} - kp + kp^2 = 0$. The term $kp^2$ is nonlinear in $p$ $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(viii) Particle in Potential: $m \ddot{x} = -V'(x)$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ ODE. Dependent variable: $x(t)$. Highest derivative: $\ddot{x}$ $\implies$ Order 2.
Linearity: The force $-V'(x)$ is in general a nonlinear function of $x$ (linear only if $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\kappa x^2$) $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(ix) Coupled Competing Species: $\dot{x} = x(4 - 2x - y), \quad \dot{y} = y(9 - 3x - 3y)$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ System of ODEs. Dependent variables: $x(t), y(t)$. Highest derivative: 1st order $\implies$ Order 1.
Linearity: Expanding gives $\dot{x} = 4x - 2x^2 - xy$ and $\dot{y} = 9y - 3xy - 3y^2$, containing quadratic terms ($x^2, y^2$) and cross product ($xy$) $\implies$ Nonlinear.

(x) Vector Matrix System: $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}$
Independent variable: $t$ $\implies$ System of ODEs. Dependent variables: $\mathbf{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Highest derivative: 1st order $\implies$ Order 1.
Linearity: Matrix multiplication is a linear operator: $A(c_1 \mathbf{x}_1 + c_2 \mathbf{x}_2) = c_1 A\mathbf{x}_1 + c_2 A\mathbf{x}_2$ $\implies$ Linear.

Problem 1.2

Malthusian Population Dynamics & Doubling Time

Separable ODE • Asymptotics • Doubling Time
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: Let $x(t)$ be the population of a certain species as a function of time, and suppose that the rate of change of this population is proportional to the existing population at each time, with constant of proportionality $k \in \mathbb{R}$.

(i) Write a differential equation for $x(t)$.
(ii) Solve it, determine the behavior of $x(t)$ as $t \to \infty$ and interpret it depending on the sign of $k$.
(iii) If the initial population is $x(0) = x_0$ and $k > 0$, determine the time needed for the population to double.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

The rate of change is the time derivative $\frac{dx}{dt}$. Proportionality to $x(t)$ with constant $k$ yields:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = k x(t), \quad x(0) = x_0 > 0 $$

This is a first-order linear separable homogeneous ODE.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Part (i): By definition, the ODE is:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dx}{dt} = k x} \tag{1.2.1} $$

Part (ii): Separate variables assuming $x > 0$:

$$ \frac{1}{x} \, dx = k \, dt $$

Integrate both sides from initial state $(0, x_0)$ to arbitrary state $(t, x(t))$:

$$ \int_{x_0}^{x(t)} \frac{1}{\xi} \, d\xi = \int_0^t k \, ds \implies [\ln\xi]_{x_0}^{x(t)} = k [s]_0^t \implies \ln\left(\frac{x(t)}{x_0}\right) = k t $$

Exponentiate both sides:

$$ \frac{x(t)}{x_0} = e^{kt} \implies \mathbf{x(t) = x_0 e^{kt}} \tag{1.2.2} $$

Asymptotic limits as $t \to \infty$:

  • If $k > 0$: $\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} x_0 e^{kt} = +\infty$ (Exponential Malthusian explosion; natality exceeds mortality).
  • If $k = 0$: $x(t) = x_0 e^0 = x_0$ (Static equilibrium; births perfectly balance deaths).
  • If $k < 0$: $\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} x_0 e^{-|k|t} = 0$ (Exponential extinction; mortality exceeds natality).

Part (iii): Doubling Time $t_d$:

Set $x(t_d) = 2x_0$ with $k > 0$:

$$ x_0 e^{k t_d} = 2 x_0 \implies e^{k t_d} = 2 $$

Take the natural logarithm:

$$ k t_d = \ln 2 \implies \mathbf{t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k}} \tag{1.2.3} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Dimensional Verification
Boxed Result
$$ x(t) = x_0 e^{kt}, \quad t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{k} $$

Dimensional Verification: $[k] = \text{time}^{-1} \implies [t_d] = \frac{1}{[k]} = \text{time}$, which is dimensionally homogeneous.

Problem 1.3

Plutonium-239 Radioactive Decay & Decay Constant

Half-Life • Nuclear Decay • Space RTG
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: Plutonium 239, virtually nonexistent in nature, is one of the radioactive materials used in the production of nuclear weapons, and is a byproduct of the generation of power in a nuclear reactor. Its half-life (i.e., the time it takes for half of the radioactive isotope to decay) is approximately 24,000 years.

Using $\frac{dN}{dt} = -kN$ as an appropriate model for radioactive decay, where $N = N(t)$ is the number of isotopes at time $t$, what is the value of $k$ that should be used for this isotope?

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Radioactive disintegration follows the first-order linear ODE $\frac{dN}{dt} = -kN$ ($k > 0$). By definition of half-life $t_{1/2} = 24{,}000\text{ yr}$, the isotope quantity at $t = t_{1/2}$ satisfies $N(t_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{2} N_0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Separate variables and integrate from $t = 0$ ($N = N_0$) to $t$:

$$ \int_{N_0}^{N(t)} \frac{d\xi}{\xi} = \int_0^t -k \, ds \implies \ln\left(\frac{N(t)}{N_0}\right) = -k t \implies N(t) = N_0 e^{-kt} $$

Substitute $t = t_{1/2}$ and $N(t_{1/2}) = \frac{1}{2}N_0$:

$$ N_0 e^{-k t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2} N_0 \implies e^{-k t_{1/2}} = 2^{-1} $$

Apply the natural logarithm to both sides:

$$ \ln\left(e^{-k t_{1/2}}\right) = \ln\left(2^{-1}\right) \implies -k t_{1/2} = -\ln 2 \implies \mathbf{k = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}} $$

Numerical evaluation with $t_{1/2} = 24{,}000\text{ years}$ and $\ln 2 \approx 0.69314718$:

$$ k = \frac{0.69314718}{24{,}000}\text{ yr}^{-1} \approx \mathbf{2.8881 \times 10^{-5}\text{ yr}^{-1}} $$

In SI units ($1\text{ yr} \approx 365.25 \times 86400\text{ s} = 3.15576 \times 10^7\text{ s}$):

$$ k = \frac{2.8881 \times 10^{-5}}{3.15576 \times 10^7}\text{ s}^{-1} \approx \mathbf{9.1519 \times 10^{-13}\text{ s}^{-1}} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Analysis
Boxed Result
$$ k = \frac{\ln 2}{24{,}000} \approx 2.8881 \times 10^{-5}\text{ yr}^{-1} \approx 9.1519 \times 10^{-13}\text{ s}^{-1} $$

Physical Interpretation: The extremely minute decay constant explains why Plutonium-239 provides sustained, quasi-constant thermal power over centuries in space probes.

Problem 1.4

Newton's Law of Cooling Modeling

Lumped Thermal Model • Variable Identification
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: A cup of hot chocolate is initially at temperature $T(0)$ and is left in the room with an ambient temperature $T_A(t)$. The rate of cooling of the hot chocolate is proportional to the difference between the current temperature $T(t)$ and the ambient temperature.

(i) Which are the dependent variable, the independent variable, and the parameters?
(ii) Write a linear differential equation for the temperature of the hot chocolate that takes into account the heat exchange with the room. Such differential equation is known as Newton's law of cooling.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Lumped capacitance thermal balance: Internal thermal energy rate $m c_p \frac{dT}{dt}$ balances convective heat loss $\dot{Q}_{\text{loss}} = h A_s (T - T_A(t))$. Rate of cooling is $-\frac{dT}{dt}$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Part (i): Identification of Variables and Parameters:

  • Independent variable: Time $t$ $[s]$ (or $[\text{min}], [\text{h}]$).
  • Dependent variable: Temperature of the hot chocolate $T = T(t)$ $[{}^\circ\text{C}]$ or $[\text{K}]$.
  • Parameters:
    • Cooling constant: $k = \frac{h A_s}{m c_p} > 0$ $[t^{-1}]$.
    • Initial condition: $T(0) = T_0$ $[{}^\circ\text{C}]$.
    • Ambient temperature: $T_A(t)$ (specified external driving function; acts as a parameter if constant).

Part (ii): Formulation of Newton's Law of Cooling:

The verbal statement dictates that the cooling rate $-\frac{dT}{dt}$ is proportional to $(T(t) - T_A(t))$ with constant $k > 0$:

$$ -\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T(t) - T_A(t)) $$

Multiplying by $-1$ and rearranging to standard linear form:

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dT}{dt} + k T(t) = k T_A(t)} \tag{1.4.1} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Linearity Check
Boxed Result
$$ \frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_A(t)) \iff \frac{dT}{dt} + k T = k T_A(t) $$

Linearity: In form $a_1(t)T' + a_0(t)T = b(t)$ with $a_1(t)=1, a_0(t)=k, b(t)=k T_A(t)$. Strictly 1st-order linear ODE.

Problem 1.5

Forensic Time of Death Estimation (Algor Mortis)

Algor Mortis • Thermal Inversion • Newton Cooling
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: A body is found in a cold room ($T_A = 5^\circ\text{C}$) at 3 pm, and its temperature then is $19^\circ\text{C}$. An hour later its temperature has dropped to $15^\circ\text{C}$. Use Newton's law of cooling to estimate the time of death, assuming that the living body temperature is $37^\circ\text{C}$ and measuring time in hours.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Let $t = 0\text{ h}$ correspond to 3:00 pm. Known data: $T_A = 5^\circ\text{C}$, $T(0) = 19^\circ\text{C}$, and $T(1) = 15^\circ\text{C}$ (at 4:00 pm). We seek the negative time $t_d < 0$ when $T(t_d) = 37^\circ\text{C}$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Step 1: General Solution:

$$ \frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - 5) \implies \frac{d(T - 5)}{T - 5} = -k \, dt \implies T(t) - 5 = [T(0) - 5] e^{-kt} $$

With $T(0) = 19^\circ\text{C}$, the temperature difference is $19 - 5 = 14^\circ\text{C}$:

$$ T(t) = 5 + 14 e^{-kt} \tag{1.5.1} $$

Step 2: Determination of $k$:

At $t = 1\text{ h}$, $T(1) = 15^\circ\text{C}$:

$$ 15 = 5 + 14 e^{-k} \implies 10 = 14 e^{-k} \implies e^{-k} = \frac{10}{14} = \frac{5}{7} $$

Inverting both sides:

$$ e^k = \frac{7}{5} = 1.4 \implies \mathbf{k = \ln(1.4) \approx 0.336472\text{ h}^{-1}} $$

Step 3: Calculating Time of Death $t_d$:

Enforce the living core body temperature $T(t_d) = 37^\circ\text{C}$:

$$ 37 = 5 + 14 e^{-k t_d} \implies 32 = 14 e^{-k t_d} \implies e^{-k t_d} = \frac{32}{14} = \frac{16}{7} $$

Take the natural logarithm:

$$ -k t_d = \ln\left(\frac{16}{7}\right) \implies t_d = -\frac{\ln(16/7)}{k} = -\frac{\ln(16/7)}{\ln(1.4)} $$

Evaluating numerically with $\ln(16/7) \approx 0.8266786$ and $\ln(1.4) \approx 0.3364722$:

$$ t_d = -\frac{0.8266786}{0.3364722}\text{ h} \approx \mathbf{-2.4569\text{ hours}} $$

Step 4: Clock Time Conversion:

Multiply the fractional part by 60 minutes:

$$ 2.4569\text{ hours} = 2\text{ h} + (0.4569 \times 60)\text{ min} = 2\text{ h } 27.41\text{ min} \approx 2\text{ hours and } 27\text{ minutes} $$

Subtracting 2 hours and 27 minutes from 3:00 pm (15:00):

$$ 15:00 - 2\text{ h } 27\text{ min} = \mathbf{12:33\text{ pm}} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Analysis
Boxed Result
$$ t_d \approx -2.457\text{ hours} \implies \text{Time of Death} \approx \mathbf{12:33\text{ pm}} $$

Forensic Consistency: Algor mortis cooling is monotonic and deterministic under steady ambient conditions.

Problem 1.6

Saline Mixing Tank Dynamics (CSTR)

CSTR Mass Balance • Washout • Integrating Factor
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: A tank contains 100 liters of water with 1 kg of salt dissolved in it (uniformly). At time $t_0 = 0$, water without salt flows into the tank at a rate of 1 liter per minute, while the mixture flows out at the same rate.

(i) Write a differential equation for $x(t)$, which is the number of kg of salt dissolved in the tank at time $t \ge 0$.
(ii) How much salt is dissolved in the tank as $t \to \infty$?
(iii) What happens if water with $s$ kg of salt per liter flows into the tank at a given rate while the mixture (that is kept uniform) flows out at the same rate?

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Species conservation of dissolved salt mass: $\frac{dx}{dt} = \dot{m}_{\text{in}} - \dot{m}_{\text{out}} = Q_{\text{in}} C_{\text{in}} - Q_{\text{out}} \frac{x(t)}{V(t)}$. Since $Q_{\text{in}} = Q_{\text{out}} = Q$, $V(t) \equiv V_0 = 100\text{ L}$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Part (i): Pure Water Flushing ($C_{\text{in}} = 0$):

$$ \dot{m}_{\text{in}} = 1 \times 0 = 0\text{ kg/min}, \quad \dot{m}_{\text{out}} = 1 \times \frac{x(t)}{100} = \frac{x(t)}{100}\text{ kg/min} $$ $$ \mathbf{\frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{x(t)}{100}}, \quad x(0) = 1\text{ kg} \tag{1.6.1} $$

Part (ii): Analytical Solution and Limit as $t \to \infty$:

$$ \int_1^{x(t)} \frac{d\xi}{\xi} = \int_0^t -\frac{1}{100} ds \implies \ln(x(t)) = -\frac{t}{100} \implies \mathbf{x(t) = e^{-t/100}\text{ kg}} $$

Taking the limit as $t \to \infty$:

$$ \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} e^{-t/100} = \mathbf{0\text{ kg}} $$

Part (iii): Saline Inflow at Concentration $s\text{ kg/L}$:

Now $\dot{m}_{\text{in}} = Q \cdot s = 1 \cdot s = s\text{ kg/min}$, leading to the inhomogeneous linear ODE:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{1}{100} x(t) = s \tag{1.6.2} $$

Multiplying by integrating factor $\mu(t) = \exp\left(\int \frac{1}{100} dt\right) = e^{t/100}$:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ x(t) e^{t/100} \right] = s e^{t/100} $$

Integrating both sides from $0$ to $t$:

$$ x(t) e^{t/100} - x(0) = s \int_0^t e^{\tau/100} d\tau = 100 s \left[ e^{t/100} - 1 \right] $$

With $x(0) = 1\text{ kg}$:

$$ x(t) e^{t/100} = 1 + 100s e^{t/100} - 100s = 100s e^{t/100} + (1 - 100s) $$

Dividing by $e^{t/100}$:

$$ \mathbf{x(t) = 100 s + (1 - 100 s) e^{-t/100}\text{ kg}} \tag{1.6.3} $$

As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-t/100} \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = \mathbf{100 s\text{ kg}} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Analysis
Boxed Result
$$ \text{Washout: } x(t) = e^{-t/100}, \; \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 0 $$ $$ \text{Saline Inflow: } x(t) = 100s + (1 - 100s)e^{-t/100}, \; \lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) = 100s\text{ kg} $$

Physical Interpretation: The asymptotic equilibrium mass $100s\text{ kg}$ corresponds exactly to the tank volume $100\text{ L}$ multiplied by the inlet concentration $s\text{ kg/L}$.

Problem 1.7

Free Fall Motion under Gravity

Newton II • Successive Integration • Kinematics
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: Consider an object in free fall from an initial point $y(0) = y_0$ and with initial velocity $0$, under the action of gravity and neglecting air friction. Write a differential equation for the position of the object $y(t)$ and solve it.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

Coordinate $y$ oriented vertically upward. Forces: $\vec{F}_g = -m g \hat{j}$. Newton's second law: $m \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -mg \implies \frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} = -g$. Initial data: $y(0) = y_0$, $y'(0) = 0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Stage 1: Velocity Integration:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right) = -g \implies \int_0^t \frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{dy}{ds}\right) ds = \int_0^t -g \, ds $$ $$ \frac{dy}{dt}(t) - \frac{dy}{dt}(0) = -gt \implies v(t) = \frac{dy}{dt} = -gt \tag{1.7.1} $$

Stage 2: Position Integration:

$$ \int_0^t \frac{dy}{ds} \, ds = \int_0^t -gs \, ds \implies y(t) - y(0) = \left[ -\frac{1}{2}gs^2 \right]_0^t $$ $$ y(t) - y_0 = -\frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies \mathbf{y(t) = y_0 - \frac{1}{2} g t^2} \tag{1.7.2} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Energy Conservation
Boxed Result
$$ y(t) = y_0 - \frac{1}{2} g t^2, \quad v(t) = -gt $$

Energy Check: $E = \frac{1}{2}m v^2 + mgy = \frac{1}{2}m (-gt)^2 + mg(y_0 - \frac{1}{2}gt^2) = mgy_0 = \text{constant}$. Mechanical energy is strictly conserved.

Problem 1.8

Simple Pendulum Equation of Motion

Newtonian Polar Frame • Mechanical Energy • Nonlinear ODE
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: Consider a simple pendulum consisting of a bob of mass $m$ at the end of a string of length $l$ and negligible mass, and denote by $\theta(t)$ the angle with respect to the vertical as a function of time $t$. Neglecting friction, apply Newton's second law to write a differential equation for $\theta(t)$.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

We present two independent methods: (1) Direct force projection in polar coordinates $(\hat{e}_r, \hat{e}_\theta)$ using Newton's second law; (2) Time differentiation of total mechanical energy $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Method 1: Polar Coordinate Force Balance:

Bob position: $\vec{r} = l \hat{e}_r$. Taking time derivatives with $\dot{\hat{e}}_r = \dot{\theta}\hat{e}_\theta$ and $\dot{\hat{e}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta}\hat{e}_r$:

$$ \vec{v} = l \dot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta $$ $$ \vec{a} = \frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = -l \dot{\theta}^2 \hat{e}_r + l \ddot{\theta} \hat{e}_\theta $$

The tangential acceleration is $a_\theta = l \ddot{\theta}$.

Acting forces: String tension $\vec{T} = -T \hat{e}_r$ acts purely radially. Gravity resolves as:

$$ \vec{F}_g = mg \cos\theta \, \hat{e}_r - mg \sin\theta \, \hat{e}_\theta $$

Projecting Newton's second law along the tangential unit vector $\hat{e}_\theta$:

$$ \sum F_\theta = m a_\theta \implies -mg \sin\theta = m (l \ddot{\theta}) $$

Dividing by $m l \neq 0$ and bringing all terms to the left-hand side:

$$ \mathbf{\ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{l} \sin\theta = 0} \tag{1.8.1} $$

Method 2: Energy Conservation Check:

$$ E = E_k + E_p = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 + m g l (1 - \cos\theta) = \frac{1}{2} m l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 + m g l (1 - \cos\theta) $$

Differentiating with respect to time $t$ ($\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$):

$$ \frac{dE}{dt} = m l^2 \dot{\theta} \ddot{\theta} + m g l \sin\theta \, \dot{\theta} = \dot{\theta} \left[ m l^2 \ddot{\theta} + m g l \sin\theta \right] = 0 $$

For non-trivial motion ($\dot{\theta} \not\equiv 0$), dividing by $m l^2$ yields identically: $\mathbf{\ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{l}\sin\theta = 0}$.

Phase 4: Final Result & Analysis
Boxed Result
$$ \ddot{\theta}(t) + \frac{g}{l} \sin\theta(t) = 0 $$

Classification: 2nd-order, autonomous, nonlinear ODE due to the transcendental term $\sin\theta$. For small amplitudes ($\theta \ll 1$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$), it linearizes to the harmonic oscillator $\ddot{\theta} + \omega_0^2 \theta = 0$ with natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/l}$.

Problem 1.9

Logistic Population Growth Model (Verhulst)

Partial Fractions • Carrying Capacity • Malthusian Limit
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: A refinement over Malthus' population growth model discussed in Chapter 1 is provided by the logistic equation: $$ \frac{dp}{dt} = kp \left( 1 - \frac{p}{M} \right) $$ where $p(t)$ is the population size at time $t$, $k$ is its growth rate (difference between birth rate and death rate), and $M$ is the maximum sustainable population (carrying capacity).

Solve the equation for a generic initial condition $p(t_0) = p_0$ and discuss the evolution of the population for $t \to \infty$, comparing with Malthus' ($M \to \infty$) prediction.

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

First-order nonlinear autonomous ODE. Separate variables by moving all terms in $p$ to the LHS, decompose into partial fractions, and integrate from $(t_0, p_0)$ to $(t, p)$.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Step 1: Separation of Variables:

$$ \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{k}{M} p (M - p) \implies \frac{dp}{p(M - p)} = \frac{k}{M} \, dt \tag{1.9.1} $$

Step 2: Partial Fraction Decomposition:

$$ \frac{1}{p(M - p)} = \frac{A}{p} + \frac{B}{M - p} \implies 1 = A(M - p) + B p $$

At $p = 0 \implies A = \frac{1}{M}$; at $p = M \implies B = \frac{1}{M}$. Substituting back:

$$ \frac{1}{M} \left( \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{M - p} \right) dp = \frac{k}{M} \, dt \implies \left( \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{M - p} \right) dp = k \, dt \tag{1.9.2} $$

Step 3: Definite Integration:

$$ \int_{p_0}^p \left( \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{M - u} \right) du = \int_{t_0}^t k \, ds $$

Recall $\int \frac{1}{M - u} du = -\ln|M - u|$:

$$ \left[ \ln|u| - \ln|M - u| \right]_{p_0}^p = k(t - t_0) \implies \left[ \ln\left| \frac{u}{M - u} \right| \right]_{p_0}^p = k(t - t_0) $$ $$ \ln\left| \frac{p(M - p_0)}{(M - p) p_0} \right| = k(t - t_0) $$

Step 4: Exponentiation and Isolation of $p(t)$:

$$ \frac{p(M - p_0)}{(M - p) p_0} = e^{k(t - t_0)} \implies p(M - p_0) = p_0 (M - p) e^{k(t - t_0)} $$ $$ p(M - p_0) + p p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} = M p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} \implies p \left[ (M - p_0) + p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} \right] = M p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} $$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $e^{k(t - t_0)}$:

$$ \mathbf{p(t) = \frac{M p_0}{p_0 + (M - p_0) e^{-k(t - t_0)}}} \tag{1.9.3} $$

Step 5: Asymptotic Limits:

  • As $t \to \infty$: $\lim_{t\to\infty} e^{-k(t-t_0)} = 0 \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} p(t) = \frac{M p_0}{p_0 + 0} = \mathbf{M}$.
  • As $M \to \infty$ (Recovery of Malthus): Dividing numerator and denominator of (1.9.3) by $M$: $$ p(t) = \frac{p_0}{\frac{p_0}{M} + \left(1 - \frac{p_0}{M}\right)e^{-k(t-t_0)}} \implies \lim_{M\to\infty} p(t) = \frac{p_0}{0 + (1 - 0)e^{-k(t-t_0)}} = \mathbf{p_0 e^{k(t-t_0)}} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Analysis
Boxed Result
$$ p(t) = \frac{M p_0}{p_0 + (M - p_0) e^{-k(t - t_0)}} $$ $$ \lim_{t\to\infty} p(t) = M, \quad \lim_{M\to\infty} p(t) = p_0 e^{k(t - t_0)} $$

Physical Interpretation: The logistic curve exhibits a sigmoidal shape when $p_0 < M/2$, passing through an inflection point of maximum growth rate at $p = M/2$.

Problem 1.10

Laminar Viscous Poiseuille Flow from Navier-Stokes

Navier-Stokes Reduction • Centerline Regularity • No-Slip Wall
Phase 1: Problem Statement & Hypotheses

Enunciado: In general, it is not possible to find explicit solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations that govern fluid flow. However, in certain cases the equations simplify very strongly. Suppose that a fluid is flowing down a pipe that has a circular cross-section of radius $a$. Assuming that the velocity $V$ of the fluid depends only on its distance from the centre of the pipe, the equation satisfied by $V$ is: $$ \frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) = -P $$ where $P > 0$.

Multiply by $r$ and integrate once to show that: $$ \frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{Pr}{2} + \frac{c}{r} $$ where $c$ is an arbitrary constant.

Integrate again to find an expression for the velocity, and then use the facts that:
(i) the velocity should be finite at all points in the pipe, and
(ii) fluids 'stick' to boundaries (which means that $V(a) = 0$),
to show that: $$ V(r) = \frac{P}{4}\left( a^2 - r^2 \right) $$ (Poiseuille flow).

Phase 2: Fundamental Formulation

This is a linear second-order Boundary Value Problem (BVP) in radial coordinate $r \in [0, a]$. The parameter $P \equiv -\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{dp}{dz} > 0$ represents the pressure driving force per unit dynamic viscosity.

Phase 3: Step-by-Step Derivation

Step 1: First Integration (Velocity Gradient):

Multiply the differential equation by $r$:

$$ \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) = -P r $$

Integrate directly with respect to $r$:

$$ \int \frac{d}{dr}\left( r \frac{dV}{dr} \right) dr = \int -P r \, dr \implies r \frac{dV}{dr} = -P \frac{r^2}{2} + c $$

Dividing by $r$ ($r > 0$):

$$ \mathbf{\frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{Pr}{2} + \frac{c}{r}} \tag{1.10.1} $$

which rigorously proves the required intermediate formula.

Step 2: Enforcement of Centerline Regularity ($r = 0$):

Condition (i) dictates that velocity and velocity gradients must remain physically finite at all points inside the duct, including the centerline $r = 0$. Since $\lim_{r\to 0^+} \frac{c}{r} = \pm\infty$ for any $c \neq 0$, avoiding an infinite velocity gradient and infinite shear stress $\tau_{rz} = \mu \frac{dV}{dr}$ requires:

$$ \mathbf{c = 0} $$

Therefore, the velocity gradient simplifies to:

$$ \frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{Pr}{2} \tag{1.10.2} $$

Step 3: Second Integration (Velocity Field):

$$ V(r) = \int -\frac{Pr}{2} \, dr = -\frac{Pr^2}{4} + C_2 \tag{1.10.3} $$

where $C_2$ is the second constant of integration.

Step 4: Enforcement of No-Slip Boundary Condition ($r = a$):

Condition (ii) states that viscous fluids adhere to solid surfaces without slip: $V(a) = 0$. Imposing this on equation (1.10.3):

$$ V(a) = -\frac{P a^2}{4} + C_2 = 0 \implies \mathbf{C_2 = \frac{P a^2}{4}} $$

Substituting $C_2$ back into (1.10.3):

$$ V(r) = \frac{P a^2}{4} - \frac{P r^2}{4} = \mathbf{\frac{P}{4}\left( a^2 - r^2 \right)} \tag{1.10.4} $$
Phase 4: Final Result & Aerospace Quantities
Boxed Result
$$ \frac{dV}{dr} = -\frac{Pr}{2} + \frac{c}{r} \implies V(r) = \frac{P}{4}\left(a^2 - r^2\right) $$

Engineering Metrics:

  • Centerline peak velocity: $V_{\max} = V(0) = \frac{P a^2}{4}$.
  • Volumetric Flow Rate (Hagen-Poiseuille Law): $Q = \int_0^a V(r) \cdot 2\pi r \, dr = \frac{\pi P a^4}{8} = \frac{\pi a^4}{8\mu}\left(-\frac{dp}{dz}\right)$.
  • Wall shear stress: $\tau_w = -\mu \left.\frac{dV}{dr}\right|_{r=a} = \frac{\mu P a}{2} = \frac{a}{2}\left(-\frac{dp}{dz}\right)$.
