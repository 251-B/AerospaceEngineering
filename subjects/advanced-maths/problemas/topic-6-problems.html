Exercise 1

Classic Sturm-Liouville Eigenvalue Problems

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf
Dirichlet • Mixed • Neumann • Robin
Statement

Determine the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Sturm-Liouville problems obtained for the equation $\phi''(x) + \lambda\phi(x) = 0$ with $0 < x < 1$ and each of the following boundary conditions:

  • (i) $\phi(0) = \phi(1) = 0$.
  • (ii) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$.
  • (iii) $\phi'(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$.
  • (iv) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$, with $h > 0$.
Classification of the problem

Each of the four boundary-value problems is a regular Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem on the finite interval $[0, 1]$, of the canonical self-adjoint form (Haberman, Sec. 2.3–2.4 and 5.8; Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, pp. 1–5):

$$ [p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0, \qquad 0 < x < 1, $$

with constant coefficients $p(x) = 1 > 0$, $\sigma(x) = 1 > 0$, and $q(x) = 0$. The boundary conditions at the endpoints $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ are separated and homogeneous:

  • (i) Dirichlet–Dirichlet (fixed value at both ends).
  • (ii) Mixed (Dirichlet–Neumann) (fixed value at $x = 0$, zero flux at $x = 1$).
  • (iii) Neumann–Neumann (zero flux at both ends).
  • (iv) Mixed (Dirichlet–Robin) (fixed value at $x = 0$, Newton-cooling / elastic condition at $x = 1$ with $h > 0$).

A number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue if and only if the boundary-value problem admits a non-trivial solution $\phi(x) \not\equiv 0$, called an eigenfunction (unique up to a non-zero multiplicative constant).

Method

We solve the constant-coefficient linear homogeneous ODE $\phi''(x) + \lambda\phi(x) = 0$ by forming its characteristic equation $r^2 + \lambda = 0$. Because the form of the general solution depends on the sign of $\lambda$, we examine all three real regimes—$\lambda > 0$, $\lambda = 0$, and $\lambda < 0$—for each set of boundary conditions (i)–(iv) without assuming in advance that $\lambda \ge 0$. For the Robin condition in (iv), the eigenvalue condition is transcendental, so we locate its roots graphically and derive their large-$n$ asymptotic behaviour.

Case λ > 0

To write the oscillatory general solution cleanly, we set $\lambda = \mu^2$ with $\mu > 0$.

Substituting $\phi(x) = e^{rx}$ into $\phi''(x) + \mu^2\phi(x) = 0$ gives the characteristic equation:

$$ r^2 + \mu^2 = 0 \implies r = \pm i\mu. $$

Using Euler's formula, the real-valued general solution and its first derivative are:

$$ \phi(x) = A\cos(\mu x) + B\sin(\mu x), \qquad \phi'(x) = -A\mu\sin(\mu x) + B\mu\cos(\mu x), $$

where $A, B \in \mathbb{R}$ are arbitrary constants. We now apply each pair of boundary conditions in turn.

Part (i): Dirichlet–Dirichlet, $\phi(0) = \phi(1) = 0$

We first evaluate $\phi(x)$ at $x = 0$ to eliminate one constant:

$$ \phi(0) = A\cos(0) + B\sin(0) = A = 0 \implies \phi(x) = B\sin(\mu x). $$

Next, we impose the boundary condition at $x = 1$:

$$ \phi(1) = B\sin(\mu) = 0. $$

To obtain a non-trivial solution we must take $B \neq 0$, which forces $\sin(\mu) = 0$. Since $\mu > 0$, the roots are:

$$ \mu_n = n\pi, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Squaring $\mu_n$ and setting $B = 1$ yields the positive eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for (i):

$$ \lambda_n = (n\pi)^2, \qquad \phi_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Part (ii): Mixed (Dirichlet–Neumann), $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$

Applying the left boundary condition $\phi(0) = 0$ again eliminates $A$:

$$ \phi(0) = A = 0 \implies \phi(x) = B\sin(\mu x), \qquad \phi'(x) = B\mu\cos(\mu x). $$

We now impose the right boundary condition $\phi'(1) = 0$:

$$ \phi'(1) = B\mu\cos(\mu) = 0. $$

For a non-trivial solution we need $B \neq 0$, and we already have $\mu > 0$, so $\cos(\mu) = 0$. The positive zeros of the cosine function are odd multiples of $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \mu_n = \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi = \frac{(2n - 1)\pi}{2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Squaring $\mu_n$ and setting $B = 1$ gives the positive eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for (ii):

$$ \lambda_n = \left[\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\right]^2, \qquad \phi_n(x) = \sin\!\left[\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi x\right], \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Part (iii): Neumann–Neumann, $\phi'(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$

We first evaluate the derivative $\phi'(x)$ at $x = 0$:

$$ \phi'(0) = -A\mu\sin(0) + B\mu\cos(0) = B\mu = 0. $$

Because $\mu > 0$, this forces $B = 0$, reducing the solution and its derivative to:

$$ \phi(x) = A\cos(\mu x), \qquad \phi'(x) = -A\mu\sin(\mu x). $$

We now impose $\phi'(1) = 0$:

$$ \phi'(1) = -A\mu\sin(\mu) = 0. $$

For a non-trivial solution we require $A \neq 0$; combined with $\mu > 0$, this gives $\sin(\mu) = 0$, hence $\mu_n = n\pi$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Setting $A = 1$ yields the positive eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for (iii):

$$ \lambda_n = (n\pi)^2, \qquad \phi_n(x) = \cos(n\pi x), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Part (iv): Dirichlet–Robin, $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$ ($h > 0$)

Imposing $\phi(0) = 0$ eliminates the cosine term:

$$ \phi(0) = A = 0 \implies \phi(x) = B\sin(\mu x), \qquad \phi'(x) = B\mu\cos(\mu x). $$

Next, we substitute $\phi(1)$ and $\phi'(1)$ into the Robin boundary condition at $x = 1$:

$$ \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = B\mu\cos(\mu) + hB\sin(\mu) = B\bigl(\mu\cos\mu + h\sin\mu\bigr) = 0. $$

For a non-trivial solution ($B \neq 0$), $\mu > 0$ must satisfy:

$$ \mu\cos\mu + h\sin\mu = 0. $$

Before dividing by $\cos\mu$, we verify that $\cos\mu \neq 0$: if $\cos\mu = 0$, then $\sin\mu = \pm 1$, and the equation would reduce to $\pm h = 0$, contradicting $h > 0$. Dividing through by $h\cos\mu$ gives the transcendental eigenvalue equation:

$$ \tan\mu = -\frac{\mu}{h}, \qquad \mu > 0. $$

To locate all positive roots, we intersect the graph of $y = \tan\mu$ with the straight line $y = -\mu/h$ of negative slope $-1/h < 0$ (Haberman, Sec. 5.8).

We check each interval of length $\pi/2$ for $\mu > 0$:

  • On $(0, \frac{\pi}{2})$, $\tan\mu > 0$ while $-\mu/h < 0$, so there is no root in $(0, \frac{\pi}{2})$.
  • On each interval $\bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi, n\pi\bigr)$ ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$), $\tan\mu$ is continuous and strictly increasing from $-\infty$ to $0$, whereas $-\mu/h$ is strictly decreasing and strictly negative. By the Intermediate Value Theorem and monotonicity, there is exactly one root $\mu_n$ in each interval:

$$ \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi < \mu_n < n\pi, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

  • On each interval $\bigl(n\pi, (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\pi\bigr)$, $\tan\mu > 0$ while $-\mu/h < 0$, so no additional roots occur.

For the representative case $h = 1$, the first three positive roots are $\mu_1 \approx 2.0288$, $\mu_2 \approx 4.9132$, and $\mu_3 \approx 7.9787$.

To find the large-$n$ asymptotic behaviour, note that as $n \to \infty$, $-\mu_n/h \to -\infty$, so $\tan\mu_n \to -\infty$ and $\mu_n$ approaches the left vertical asymptote $\bigl(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\pi$ from above. Writing $\mu_n = \bigl(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\pi + \delta_n$ with $0 < \delta_n \ll 1$, we use the trigonometric identity $\tan\bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi + \delta_n\bigr) = -\cot\delta_n$:

$$ -\cot\delta_n = -\frac{\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi + \delta_n}{h}. $$

Since $\cot\delta_n \approx \frac{1}{\delta_n}$ for small $\delta_n$, we obtain at leading order:

$$ \frac{1}{\delta_n} \approx \frac{\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi}{h} \implies \delta_n \approx \frac{h}{\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi}. $$

Therefore, for large $n$:

$$ \mu_n \approx \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi + \frac{h}{\left(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\pi}, \qquad \lambda_n = \mu_n^2 \approx \left[\left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\right]^2. $$

Setting $B = 1$, the eigenfunctions for (iv) are $\phi_n(x) = \sin(\mu_n x)$ with $\lambda_n = \mu_n^2$.

Case λ = 0

When $\lambda = 0$, the ODE simplifies to $\phi''(x) = 0$. Integrating twice with respect to $x$ gives the linear general solution and its derivative:

$$ \phi'(x) = B, \qquad \phi(x) = A + Bx, $$

where $A, B \in \mathbb{R}$. We test all four sets of boundary conditions to see whether $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue.

  • (i) $\phi(0) = \phi(1) = 0$: $\phi(0) = A = 0$, and then $\phi(1) = 0 + B(1) = B = 0$. Thus $\phi(x) \equiv 0$, so $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.
  • (ii) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$: $\phi(0) = A = 0$, and $\phi'(1) = B = 0$. Thus $\phi(x) \equiv 0$, so $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.
  • (iii) $\phi'(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$: $\phi'(0) = B = 0$ and $\phi'(1) = B = 0$ both hold when $B = 0$, leaving $A$ completely arbitrary. Thus $\phi(x) = A \not\equiv 0$ for any $A \neq 0$. Choosing $A = 1$, $\lambda_0 = 0$ is an eigenvalue with constant eigenfunction $\phi_0(x) = 1$.
  • (iv) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$ ($h > 0$): $\phi(0) = A = 0$ gives $\phi(x) = Bx$ and $\phi'(x) = B$. Substituting into the Robin condition at $x = 1$:

$$ \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = B + hB = B(1 + h) = 0. $$

Since $h > 0$, we have $1 + h > 1 \neq 0$, which forces $B = 0$. Hence $\phi(x) \equiv 0$, so $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.

Case λ < 0

To examine negative eigenvalues, we write $\lambda = -k^2$ with $k > 0$.

The ODE becomes $\phi''(x) - k^2\phi(x) = 0$, whose characteristic equation is $r^2 - k^2 = 0 \implies r = \pm k$. Expressing the general solution in hyperbolic form (which is algebraically equivalent to $C_1 e^{kx} + C_2 e^{-kx}$ and simplifies boundary evaluations at $x = 0$) gives:

$$ \phi(x) = A\cosh(kx) + B\sinh(kx), \qquad \phi'(x) = Ak\sinh(kx) + Bk\cosh(kx). $$

We check all four boundary conditions using $\cosh(0) = 1$, $\sinh(0) = 0$, and the fact that $\sinh(k) > 0$ and $\cosh(k) > 1$ for all $k > 0$:

  • (i) $\phi(0) = \phi(1) = 0$: $\phi(0) = A = 0 \implies \phi(1) = B\sinh(k) = 0$. Because $\sinh(k) > 0$, $B = 0$, giving only the trivial solution.
  • (ii) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$: $\phi(0) = A = 0 \implies \phi'(1) = Bk\cosh(k) = 0$. Because $k > 0$ and $\cosh(k) > 0$, $B = 0$, giving only the trivial solution.
  • (iii) $\phi'(0) = \phi'(1) = 0$: $\phi'(0) = Bk = 0 \implies B = 0$. Then $\phi'(1) = Ak\sinh(k) = 0 \implies A = 0$, giving only the trivial solution.
  • (iv) $\phi(0) = \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$ ($h > 0$): $\phi(0) = A = 0$, so $\phi(x) = B\sinh(kx)$ and $\phi'(x) = Bk\cosh(kx)$. Substituting into the Robin condition at $x = 1$:

$$ \phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = B\bigl(k\cosh k + h\sinh k\bigr) = 0. $$

Since $k > 0$, $h > 0$, $\cosh k > 0$, and $\sinh k > 0$, the quantity $k\cosh k + h\sinh k$ is strictly positive (equivalently, $\tanh k = -k/h$ has no solution because $\tanh k > 0$ while $-k/h < 0$). Therefore $B = 0$, giving only the trivial solution.

Hence none of the four problems has any negative eigenvalues.

Answers and checks

Direct substitution check. For every positive mode $\phi_n(x) = \sin(\mu_n x)$ or $\cos(\mu_n x)$, differentiating twice yields $\phi_n''(x) = -\mu_n^2\phi_n(x) = -\lambda_n\phi_n(x)$, so $\phi_n'' + \lambda_n\phi_n = 0$ identically on $(0, 1)$. For $\phi_0(x) = 1$ in (iii), $\phi_0'' + 0\cdot\phi_0 = 0$ and $\phi_0'(0) = \phi_0'(1) = 0$. Checking the endpoints:

  • (i) $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\sin(n\pi) = 0$.
  • (ii) $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\phi_n'(1) = \bigl(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\pi\cos\!\bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi\bigr) = 0$.
  • (iii) $\phi_n'(0) = -n\pi\sin(0) = 0$ and $\phi_n'(1) = -n\pi\sin(n\pi) = 0$.
  • (iv) $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\phi_n'(1) + h\phi_n(1) = \mu_n\cos\mu_n + h\sin\mu_n = h\cos\mu_n\bigl(\tfrac{\mu_n}{h} + \tan\mu_n\bigr) = 0$ by the root equation $\tan\mu_n = -\mu_n/h$.

Limiting-case check for the Robin condition (iv).

  • As $h \to 0^+$, the Robin condition $\phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$ reduces to the Neumann condition $\phi'(1) = 0$ of part (ii). Correspondingly, $-\mu/h \to -\infty$, pushing every root $\mu_n \in \bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi, n\pi\bigr)$ toward the left endpoint $\bigl(n - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\pi$, recovering part (ii).
  • As $h \to +\infty$, dividing $\phi'(1) + h\phi(1) = 0$ by $h$ yields $\phi(1) = 0$, the Dirichlet condition of part (i). Correspondingly, $-\mu/h \to 0^-$, pushing every root $\mu_n$ toward the right endpoint $n\pi$, recovering part (i).

Dimensional check. Here $x \in [0, 1]$ is dimensionless, so $\lambda$, $\mu$, and $h$ are dimensionless; on a physical rod of length $L$ (in $\text{m}$), $[\phi''] = [\phi]\,\text{m}^{-2}$ forces $[\lambda] = \text{m}^{-2}$, $[\mu] = \text{m}^{-1}$, and $[h] = \text{m}^{-1}$, making $\mu L$ and $\mu/h$ dimensionless.

Key results
  • (i) Dirichlet: $\lambda_n = (n\pi)^2$, $\phi_n(x) = \sin(n\pi x)$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$ ($\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue).
  • (ii) Mixed: $\lambda_n = \bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi\bigr)^2$, $\phi_n(x) = \sin\!\bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi x\bigr)$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$ ($\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue).
  • (iii) Neumann: $\lambda_0 = 0$ with $\phi_0(x) = 1$, and $\lambda_n = (n\pi)^2$ with $\phi_n(x) = \cos(n\pi x)$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$
  • (iv) Robin ($h > 0$): $\lambda_n = \mu_n^2$ and $\phi_n(x) = \sin(\mu_n x)$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$, where $\mu_n \in \bigl((n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi, n\pi\bigr)$ are the positive roots of $\tan\mu = -\mu/h$ (asymptotically $\mu_n \approx (n - \tfrac{1}{2})\pi + \frac{h}{(n - 1/2)\pi}$; $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue).
Exercise 2

Constant-Coefficient Eigenvalue Problems with Damping Term

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf
First-Derivative Term • Exponential Factor • Transcendental Roots
Statement

Determine the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the following problems:

  • (i) $y''(x) + y'(x) + (1 + \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y(0) = y(1) = 0$.
  • (ii) $y''(x) + 2y'(x) + (1 - \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y(0) = y'(1) = 0$.
  • (iii) $y''(x) - 3y'(x) + 3(1 + \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y'(0) = y'(1) = 0$.
Classification of the problem

Each problem is a second-order, linear, homogeneous, constant-coefficient boundary-value problem on $[0, 1]$ containing a first-derivative ("damping" or "advection") term $\alpha y'(x)$. Here the unknown $y(x)$ plays the same role as $\phi(x)$ in Notes09-12-Nov.pdf. As shown in Problem - Ch6-P3 Integrating Factor for Sturm-Liouville Form (Haberman, Sec. 5.3), multiplying each equation by the integrating factor $e^{\alpha x}$ converts it into a regular Sturm-Liouville problem with weight $\sigma(x) = e^{\alpha x}$ (or $-e^{2x}$ in part (ii), where the sign of $\lambda$ is flipped). Consequently, all eigenvalues are real and form an unbounded discrete sequence.

Method

Because all coefficients are constant, we solve each ODE directly with the exponential trial solution $y(x) = e^{rx}$, which produces a quadratic characteristic equation whose discriminant $\Delta$ depends linearly on $\lambda$. Rather than assuming the sign of $\lambda$ in advance, we analyse all three discriminant regimes—$\Delta > 0$ (distinct real roots), $\Delta = 0$ (repeated real root), and $\Delta < 0$ (complex-conjugate roots)—and explicitly check whether $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue in every part.

Part (i): $y''(x) + y'(x) + (1 + \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y(0) = y(1) = 0$

Substituting $y(x) = e^{rx}$ gives the characteristic equation and its discriminant:

$$ r^2 + r + (1 + \lambda) = 0, \qquad \Delta = 1^2 - 4(1)(1 + \lambda) = -3 - 4\lambda. $$

The sign of $\Delta$ changes at $\lambda = -\frac{3}{4}$. We examine all three cases.

Case $\Delta > 0$ ($\lambda < -\frac{3}{4}$)

Setting $k = \frac{\sqrt{-3 - 4\lambda}}{2} > 0$, the two distinct real roots are $r = -\frac{1}{2} \pm k$. Writing the general solution in hyperbolic form gives:

$$ y(x) = e^{-x/2}\bigl(A\cosh(kx) + B\sinh(kx)\bigr). $$

We apply the left boundary condition $y(0) = 0$:

$$ y(0) = e^0\bigl(A\cosh 0 + B\sinh 0\bigr) = A = 0 \implies y(x) = Be^{-x/2}\sinh(kx). $$

Next, we impose the right boundary condition $y(1) = 0$:

$$ y(1) = Be^{-1/2}\sinh(k) = 0. $$

Since $e^{-1/2} > 0$ and $\sinh(k) > 0$ for all $k > 0$, we must have $B = 0$. Hence there are no eigenvalues with $\lambda < -\frac{3}{4}$.

Case $\Delta = 0$ ($\lambda = -\frac{3}{4}$)

The characteristic equation has a repeated real root $r = -\frac{1}{2}$, so the general solution is:

$$ y(x) = (A + Bx)e^{-x/2}. $$

Applying $y(0) = 0$ gives $A = 0$, so $y(x) = Bxe^{-x/2}$. Applying $y(1) = 0$ gives $Be^{-1/2} = 0 \implies B = 0$. Hence $\lambda = -\frac{3}{4}$ is not an eigenvalue.

Case $\Delta < 0$ ($\lambda > -\frac{3}{4}$)

Setting $\mu = \frac{\sqrt{3 + 4\lambda}}{2} > 0$, the complex-conjugate roots are $r = -\frac{1}{2} \pm i\mu$, and the real-valued general solution is:

$$ y(x) = e^{-x/2}\bigl(A\cos(\mu x) + B\sin(\mu x)\bigr). $$

Imposing $y(0) = 0$ eliminates the cosine term:

$$ y(0) = A = 0 \implies y(x) = Be^{-x/2}\sin(\mu x). $$

Imposing $y(1) = 0$ gives:

$$ y(1) = Be^{-1/2}\sin(\mu) = 0. $$

Because $e^{-1/2} \neq 0$, a non-trivial solution ($B \neq 0$) requires $\sin(\mu) = 0$. Since $\mu > 0$, this yields $\mu_n = n\pi$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Solving $\frac{\sqrt{3 + 4\lambda_n}}{2} = n\pi$ for $\lambda_n$:

$$ 3 + 4\lambda_n = 4(n\pi)^2 \implies \lambda_n = (n\pi)^2 - \frac{3}{4}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Note that $\lambda = 0$ lies in this regime ($\Delta = -3 < 0$) and corresponds to $\mu = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, which is not a positive integer multiple of $\pi$; therefore $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue of part (i). Setting $B = 1$, the eigenfunctions are $y_n(x) = e^{-x/2}\sin(n\pi x)$.

Part (ii): $y''(x) + 2y'(x) + (1 - \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y(0) = y'(1) = 0$

Substituting $y(x) = e^{rx}$ gives the characteristic equation, which completes the square directly:

$$ r^2 + 2r + (1 - \lambda) = 0 \iff (r + 1)^2 = \lambda. $$

Here the discriminant is $\Delta = 4 - 4(1 - \lambda) = 4\lambda$, so the three discriminant regimes coincide directly with $\lambda > 0$, $\lambda = 0$, and $\lambda < 0$.

Case $\lambda > 0$ ($\Delta > 0$)

Set $\lambda = k^2$ with $k > 0$. Then $r = -1 \pm k$, and the general solution is:

$$ y(x) = e^{-x}\bigl(A\cosh(kx) + B\sinh(kx)\bigr). $$

Applying $y(0) = 0$ gives $A = 0$, so $y(x) = Be^{-x}\sinh(kx)$. We differentiate using the product rule to apply the right boundary condition:

$$ y'(x) = Be^{-x}\bigl(k\cosh(kx) - \sinh(kx)\bigr). $$

Evaluating at $x = 1$ and setting $y'(1) = 0$ gives:

$$ y'(1) = Be^{-1}\bigl(k\cosh k - \sinh k\bigr) = 0. $$

For $B \neq 0$, this requires $k\cosh k = \sinh k$, or equivalently $\tanh k = k$ (since $\cosh k > 0$). Consider the auxiliary function $f(k) = k - \tanh k$ for $k \ge 0$: we have $f(0) = 0$ and

$$ f'(k) = 1 - \operatorname{sech}^2 k = \tanh^2 k > 0 \quad \text{for all } k > 0. $$

Thus $f(k) > 0$ strictly for all $k > 0$, so $\tanh k = k$ has no positive solution. Consequently $B = 0$, and there are no positive eigenvalues.

Case $\lambda = 0$ ($\Delta = 0$)

When $\lambda = 0$, $(r + 1)^2 = 0$ gives the repeated root $r = -1$, so the general solution is:

$$ y(x) = (A + Bx)e^{-x}. $$

Imposing $y(0) = 0$ gives $A = 0$, leaving $y(x) = Bxe^{-x}$. Differentiating by the product rule:

$$ y'(x) = B\bigl(1\cdot e^{-x} + x(-e^{-x})\bigr) = B(1 - x)e^{-x}. $$

Evaluating at $x = 1$ yields:

$$ y'(1) = B(1 - 1)e^{-1} = 0, $$

which is satisfied automatically for any $B \neq 0$. Choosing $B = 1$, we find that $\lambda_0 = 0$ is an eigenvalue with eigenfunction:

$$ y_0(x) = xe^{-x}. $$

Case $\lambda < 0$ ($\Delta < 0$)

Set $\lambda = -\mu^2$ with $\mu > 0$. Then $(r + 1)^2 = -\mu^2 \implies r = -1 \pm i\mu$, and the real-valued general solution is:

$$ y(x) = e^{-x}\bigl(A\cos(\mu x) + B\sin(\mu x)\bigr). $$

Applying $y(0) = 0$ gives $A = 0$, so $y(x) = Be^{-x}\sin(\mu x)$. Differentiating by the product rule:

$$ y'(x) = Be^{-x}\bigl(\mu\cos(\mu x) - \sin(\mu x)\bigr). $$

Imposing $y'(1) = 0$ gives:

$$ y'(1) = Be^{-1}\bigl(\mu\cos\mu - \sin\mu\bigr) = 0. $$

For a non-trivial solution ($B \neq 0$), we need $\mu\cos\mu - \sin\mu = 0$. If $\cos\mu = 0$, then $\sin\mu = \pm 1 \neq 0$, which cannot satisfy the equation; hence $\cos\mu \neq 0$, and dividing by $\cos\mu$ yields the transcendental equation:

$$ \tan\mu = \mu, \qquad \mu > 0. $$

We locate the positive roots graphically by intersecting $y = \tan\mu$ with the line $y = \mu$.

$$ n\pi < \mu_n < \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

The first two positive roots are $\mu_1 \approx 4.4934$ and $\mu_2 \approx 7.7253$, and for large $n$ the intersections approach the right vertical asymptotes: $\mu_n \approx \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi$. Setting $B = 1$ gives the negative eigenvalues and their eigenfunctions:

Part (iii): $y''(x) - 3y'(x) + 3(1 + \lambda)y(x) = 0$, $y'(0) = y'(1) = 0$

Substituting $y(x) = e^{rx}$ yields the characteristic equation and its discriminant:

$$ r^2 - 3r + 3(1 + \lambda) = 0, \qquad \Delta = (-3)^2 - 4(1)\cdot 3(1 + \lambda) = 9 - 12(1 + \lambda) = -3 - 12\lambda. $$

The sign of $\Delta$ changes at $\lambda = -\frac{1}{4}$. We examine all three regimes carefully.

Case $\Delta > 0$ ($\lambda < -\frac{1}{4}$)

Let $k = \sqrt{-3 - 12\lambda} > 0$. The two distinct real roots are:

$$ r_1 = \frac{3 + k}{2}, \qquad r_2 = \frac{3 - k}{2}, \qquad r_1 \neq r_2. $$

The general solution and its derivative are:

$$ y(x) = C_1 e^{r_1 x} + C_2 e^{r_2 x}, \qquad y'(x) = r_1 C_1 e^{r_1 x} + r_2 C_2 e^{r_2 x}. $$

Imposing the two Neumann boundary conditions $y'(0) = 0$ and $y'(1) = 0$ produces a homogeneous $2 \times 2$ linear system for $(C_1, C_2)$:

$$ \begin{cases} r_1 C_1 + r_2 C_2 = 0, \\ r_1 e^{r_1} C_1 + r_2 e^{r_2} C_2 = 0. \end{cases} $$

A non-trivial solution $(C_1, C_2) \neq (0, 0)$ exists if and only if the determinant of the coefficient matrix vanishes:

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} r_1 & r_2 \\ r_1 e^{r_1} & r_2 e^{r_2} \end{pmatrix} = r_1 r_2 \bigl(e^{r_2} - e^{r_1}\bigr) = 0. $$

Since $r_1 \neq r_2$, we have $e^{r_2} \neq e^{r_1}$, so the determinant vanishes if and only if $r_1 r_2 = 0$. By Vieta's formula for $r^2 - 3r + 3(1 + \lambda) = 0$, the product of the roots is $r_1 r_2 = 3(1 + \lambda)$, hence:

$$ 3(1 + \lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_0 = -1. $$

Checking that $\lambda_0 = -1$ indeed lies in the regime $\lambda < -\frac{1}{4}$: $-1 < -\frac{1}{4}$ holds, with $k = \sqrt{-3 - 12(-1)} = \sqrt{9} = 3$, giving $r_1 = 3$ and $r_2 = 0$. Substituting into $r_1 C_1 + r_2 C_2 = 0$ gives $3C_1 + 0\cdot C_2 = 0 \implies C_1 = 0$, while $C_2$ is arbitrary. Choosing $C_2 = 1$ gives the constant eigenfunction:

$$ \lambda_0 = -1, \qquad y_0(x) = 1. $$

Case $\Delta = 0$ ($\lambda = -\frac{1}{4}$)

The characteristic equation has the repeated root $r = \frac{3}{2}$, so the general solution and its derivative are:

$$ y(x) = (A + Bx)e^{3x/2}, \qquad y'(x) = \left[\left(\frac{3}{2}A + B\right) + \frac{3}{2}Bx\right]e^{3x/2}. $$

Applying $y'(0) = 0$ gives $\frac{3}{2}A + B = 0$. Substituting this into $y'(1) = 0$ leaves:

$$ y'(1) = \frac{3}{2}B e^{3/2} = 0 \implies B = 0 \implies A = 0. $$

Thus $\lambda = -\frac{1}{4}$ yields only the trivial solution and is not an eigenvalue.

Case $\Delta < 0$ ($\lambda > -\frac{1}{4}$)

Setting $\mu = \frac{\sqrt{3 + 12\lambda}}{2} > 0$, the roots are $r = \frac{3}{2} \pm i\mu$, and the general solution is:

$$ y(x) = e^{3x/2}\bigl(A\cos(\mu x) + B\sin(\mu x)\bigr). $$

We differentiate $y(x)$ by the product rule and collect the cosine and sine terms:

$$ y'(x) = e^{3x/2}\left[\left(\frac{3}{2}A + \mu B\right)\cos(\mu x) + \left(\frac{3}{2}B - \mu A\right)\sin(\mu x)\right]. $$

Imposing the left boundary condition $y'(0) = 0$ sets the cosine coefficient to zero:

$$ y'(0) = \frac{3}{2}A + \mu B = 0 \implies B = -\frac{3}{2\mu}A \quad \text{(equivalently } A = -\frac{2\mu}{3}B\text{)}. $$

Substituting $\frac{3}{2}A + \mu B = 0$ back into $y'(x)$ simplifies the derivative to a single sine term:

$$ y'(x) = e^{3x/2}\left(\frac{3}{2}B - \mu A\right)\sin(\mu x) = -e^{3x/2}\frac{9 + 4\mu^2}{4\mu}A\sin(\mu x). $$

Now we impose $y'(1) = 0$:

$$ y'(1) = -e^{3/2}\frac{9 + 4\mu^2}{4\mu}A\sin(\mu) = 0. $$

Since $e^{3/2} > 0$ and $\frac{9 + 4\mu^2}{4\mu} > 0$ for all $\mu > 0$, a non-trivial solution ($A \neq 0$) requires $\sin(\mu) = 0$, hence $\mu_n = n\pi$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Solving $\frac{\sqrt{3 + 12\lambda_n}}{2} = n\pi$ for $\lambda_n$:

$$ 3 + 12\lambda_n = 4(n\pi)^2 \implies \lambda_n = \frac{(n\pi)^2}{3} - \frac{1}{4}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Note that $\lambda = 0$ lies in this regime ($\Delta = -3 < 0$) with $\mu = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \neq n\pi$, so $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue of part (iii). To avoid fractions in the eigenfunctions, we choose $A = 2\mu_n = 2n\pi$, which gives $B = -\frac{3}{2\mu_n}(2\mu_n) = -3$, yielding:

$$ y_n(x) = e^{3x/2}\bigl(2n\pi\cos(n\pi x) - 3\sin(n\pi x)\bigr), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Exercise 3

Integrating Factor for Sturm-Liouville Form

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf
Integrating Factor • Self-Adjoint Form
Statement

Consider the ODE $\phi''(x) + \alpha(x)\phi'(x) + [\lambda\beta(x) + \gamma(x)]\phi(x) = 0$. Find an integrating factor $\mu(x)$ in terms of the coefficients of the ODE $\alpha(x)$, $\beta(x)$ and $\gamma(x)$, such that the equation can be brought into Sturm-Liouville form, namely,

$$ [p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0. $$

Classification of the problem

This is a structural reduction problem for general second-order linear homogeneous ODEs with a spectral parameter $\lambda$ (Haberman, Sec. 5.3). Whenever $\alpha(x)$ is continuous on an interval $[a, b]$, multiplying the equation by a suitable strictly positive integrating factor $\mu(x)$ converts the non-self-adjoint differential operator $\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \alpha(x)\frac{d}{dx}$ into the self-adjoint Sturm-Liouville derivative $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(p(x)\frac{d}{dx}\bigr)$, which guarantees orthogonality of eigenfunctions with respect to the weight $\sigma(x)$ and enables the Rayleigh quotient.

Method

We multiply the given ODE by an unknown strictly positive function $\mu(x) > 0$, expand the target Sturm-Liouville derivative $[p(x)\phi'(x)]'$ by the product rule, and match the coefficients of $\phi''(x)$, $\phi'(x)$, and $\phi(x)$. Matching the derivative terms yields a separable first-order ODE for $\mu(x)$, which we integrate explicitly to express $\mu(x)$, $p(x)$, $\sigma(x)$, and $q(x)$ in terms of $\alpha(x)$, $\beta(x)$, and $\gamma(x)$.

Derivation of the integrating factor

First, we multiply the given ODE throughout by an unknown integrating factor $\mu(x) > 0$:

$$ \mu(x)\phi''(x) + \mu(x)\alpha(x)\phi'(x) + \bigl[\lambda\mu(x)\beta(x) + \mu(x)\gamma(x)\bigr]\phi(x) = 0. $$

Next, we expand the derivative term $[p(x)\phi'(x)]'$ in the target Sturm-Liouville equation using the product rule:

$$ [p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = p(x)\phi''(x) + p'(x)\phi'(x) + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0. $$

Comparing the coefficients of $\phi''(x)$ and $\phi'(x)$ in the two equations gives two conditions:

$$ p(x) = \mu(x), \qquad p'(x) = \mu(x)\alpha(x). $$

Substituting $p(x) = \mu(x)$ into the second condition yields a first-order separable ODE for $\mu(x)$:

$$ \mu'(x) = \mu(x)\alpha(x). $$

Because we require $\mu(x) \neq 0$ (indeed $\mu(x) > 0$ so that multiplying by $\mu(x)$ does not introduce spurious solutions or change the sign of $p(x)$), we divide both sides by $\mu(x)$ and separate variables:

$$ \frac{\mu'(x)}{\mu(x)} = \alpha(x) \implies \frac{d\mu}{\mu} = \alpha(x)\,dx. $$

Integrating both sides with respect to $x$ gives:

$$ \ln|\mu(x)| = \int \alpha(x)\,dx + C, $$

where $C \in \mathbb{R}$ is an arbitrary constant of integration. Exponentiating both sides yields:

$$ \mu(x) = K\exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right), \qquad K = \pm e^C \neq 0. $$

Because any non-zero constant multiple of a Sturm-Liouville equation is equivalent, we choose $K = 1$ (i.e., $C = 0$) to obtain a strictly positive canonical integrating factor:

$$ \mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right) > 0. $$

Notice that $\mu(x)$ depends only on $\alpha(x)$ and is completely independent of $\beta(x)$ and $\gamma(x)$.

Sturm-Liouville coefficients

Comparing the remaining terms of the multiplied equation with $[p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0$ immediately identifies all three Sturm-Liouville coefficients:

$$ p(x) = \mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right), $$

$$ \sigma(x) = \mu(x)\beta(x) = \beta(x)\exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right), $$

$$ q(x) = \mu(x)\gamma(x) = \gamma(x)\exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right). $$

If the original ODE is regular on $[a, b]$ with $\beta(x) > 0$, then both $p(x) > 0$ and $\sigma(x) > 0$ automatically hold on $[a, b]$.

Answers and checks

Product-rule verification. Differentiating $p(x)\phi'(x) = \mu(x)\phi'(x)$ with $\mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right)$ using the chain rule $\mu'(x) = \alpha(x)\mu(x)$ gives:

$$ [\mu(x)\phi'(x)]' = \mu(x)\phi''(x) + \mu'(x)\phi'(x) = \mu(x)\phi''(x) + \mu(x)\alpha(x)\phi'(x) = \mu(x)\bigl[\phi''(x) + \alpha(x)\phi'(x)\bigr]. $$

Adding $\bigl[\lambda\mu(x)\beta(x) + \mu(x)\gamma(x)\bigr]\phi(x)$ recovers $\mu(x)$ times the original ODE identically.

Application check 1 (Exercise 6.2(i)). For $y'' + y' + (1 + \lambda)y = 0$, we have $\alpha(x) = 1$, $\beta(x) = 1$, $\gamma(x) = 1$. The formula gives:

$$ \mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int 1\,dx\right) = e^x, $$

yielding the Sturm-Liouville form $[e^x y'(x)]' + (\lambda e^x + e^x)y(x) = 0$, with $p(x) = e^x$, $\sigma(x) = e^x$, and $q(x) = e^x$.

Application check 2 (Exercise 6.4). For the Euler equation $x^2\phi'' + x\phi' + \lambda\phi = 0$ on $[1, 2]$, we first divide by $x^2$ to put it into monic form $\phi'' + \frac{1}{x}\phi' + \frac{\lambda}{x^2}\phi = 0$, giving $\alpha(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, $\beta(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$, and $\gamma(x) = 0$. The formula gives:

$$ \mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx\right) = e^{\ln x} = x \quad (x > 0). $$

Multiplying the monic equation by $x$ (which is equivalent to multiplying the original Euler equation by $\frac{x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{x}$) yields $[x\phi'(x)]' + \frac{\lambda}{x}\phi(x) = 0$, matching Problem - Ch6-P4 Euler Eigenvalue Problem and Rayleigh Quotient.

Dimensional check. Since $\alpha(x)\phi'(x)$ has the same dimensions as $\phi''(x)$, $[\alpha] = [x]^{-1}$, so the integral $\int \alpha(x)\,dx$ is dimensionless and $\mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right)$ is dimensionless as required.

Key results
  • Integrating factor: $\mu(x) = \exp\!\left(\int \alpha(x)\,dx\right)$ (depends only on $\alpha(x)$, strictly positive).
  • Sturm-Liouville form: $[p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0$ with $$p(x) = \mu(x), \qquad \sigma(x) = \mu(x)\beta(x), \qquad q(x) = \mu(x)\gamma(x).$$

Euler Eigenvalue Problem and Rayleigh Quotient

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf
Euler Equation • Rayleigh Quotient • Logarithmic Modes
Statement

Consider the eigenvalue problem $x^2\phi''(x) + x\phi'(x) + \lambda\phi(x) = 0$ on the interval $[1, 2]$, with boundary conditions $\phi(1) = \phi(2) = 0$.

  1. Show that multiplying by $1/x$ puts this equation into Sturm–Liouville form.
  2. Show that $\lambda \ge 0$, and determine all possible eigenvalues. Is $\lambda = 0$ an eigenvalue?
  3. What are the eigenfunctions?
Classification of the problem

This is a Cauchy–Euler eigenvalue problem on $[1, 2]$ with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions $\phi(1) = \phi(2) = 0$ (Haberman, Sec. 5.3 and 5.6). Because the interval $[1, 2]$ does not contain the singular point $x = 0$, dividing by $x$ yields a regular Sturm-Liouville problem with strictly positive $p(x) = x$ and $\sigma(x) = 1/x$.

Method

We proceed in the three steps requested by the statement:

  1. Multiply the ODE by $1/x$ and group the derivative terms into $[p(x)\phi'(x)]'$ to identify $p(x)$, $\sigma(x)$, and $q(x)$.
  2. Multiply the Sturm-Liouville equation by $\phi(x)$, integrate over $[1, 2]$, and integrate by parts explicitly to obtain the Rayleigh quotient and prove $\lambda \ge 0$. Then apply the Euler change of variable $x = e^s$ ($s = \ln x$) using the chain rule explicitly to reduce the ODE to constant coefficients on $[0, \ln 2]$, and examine both $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda > 0$.
  3. Transform the resulting trigonometric modes in $s$ back to $x$ via $s = \ln x$.
Part 1: Reduction to Sturm-Liouville form

Since $x \in [1, 2]$, we have $x \ge 1 > 0$, so multiplying the ODE by $1/x$ is valid everywhere on $[1, 2]$:

$$ \frac{1}{x}\bigl[x^2\phi''(x) + x\phi'(x) + \lambda\phi(x)\bigr] = x\phi''(x) + \phi'(x) + \frac{\lambda}{x}\phi(x) = 0. $$

By the product rule, the first two terms collapse into a single derivative:

$$ [x\phi'(x)]' = 1\cdot\phi'(x) + x\phi''(x) = x\phi''(x) + \phi'(x). $$

Hence the equation takes the canonical Sturm-Liouville form $[p(x)\phi'(x)]' + [\lambda\sigma(x) + q(x)]\phi(x) = 0$:

$$ [x\phi'(x)]' + \frac{\lambda}{x}\phi(x) = 0, \qquad 1 < x < 2, $$

with coefficients:

$$ p(x) = x > 0, \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{x} > 0, \qquad q(x) = 0. $$

This agrees with the integrating-factor formula of Problem - Ch6-P3 Integrating Factor for Sturm-Liouville Form.

Part 2: Non-negativity via the Rayleigh quotient and eigenvalues
Proof that $\lambda \ge 0$ via the Rayleigh quotient

To derive the Rayleigh quotient, we multiply the Sturm-Liouville equation $[x\phi'(x)]' + \frac{\lambda}{x}\phi(x) = 0$ by an eigenfunction $\phi(x) \not\equiv 0$ and integrate from $x = 1$ to $x = 2$:

$$ \int_1^2 \phi(x)[x\phi'(x)]'\,dx + \lambda\int_1^2 \frac{[\phi(x)]^2}{x}\,dx = 0. $$

We evaluate the first integral by integration by parts with the explicit choice:

$$ u = \phi(x), \qquad dv = [x\phi'(x)]'\,dx \implies du = \phi'(x)\,dx, \qquad v = x\phi'(x). $$

Applying $\int_1^2 u\,dv = [uv]_1^2 - \int_1^2 v\,du$ and Barrow's rule $[F]_1^2 = F(2) - F(1)$ gives:

$$ \int_1^2 \phi(x)[x\phi'(x)]'\,dx = \bigl[x\phi(x)\phi'(x)\bigr]_1^2 - \int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx = \bigl(2\phi(2)\phi'(2) - 1\cdot\phi(1)\phi'(1)\bigr) - \int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx. $$

Because the boundary conditions give $\phi(1) = 0$ and $\phi(2) = 0$, the boundary term vanishes identically:

$$ 2\phi(2)\phi'(2) - \phi(1)\phi'(1) = 2(0)\phi'(2) - (0)\phi'(1) = 0. $$

Substituting this back into the integrated equation gives:

$$ -\int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx + \lambda\int_1^2 \frac{[\phi(x)]^2}{x}\,dx = 0. $$

Since $\phi(x) \not\equiv 0$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$ and $1/x > 0$ on $[1, 2]$, the denominator integral $\int_1^2 \frac{[\phi(x)]^2}{x}\,dx$ is strictly positive. Solving for $\lambda$ yields the Rayleigh quotient:

$$ \lambda = \frac{\displaystyle\int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_1^2 \frac{[\phi(x)]^2}{x}\,dx}. $$

On $[1, 2]$, we have $x > 0$ and $[\phi'(x)]^2 \ge 0$, so the numerator is $\ge 0$ while the denominator is $> 0$. Therefore:

$$ \lambda \ge 0. $$

Reduction to constant coefficients via $x = e^s$

To solve the Euler equation explicitly, we introduce the standard logarithmic change of independent variable:

$$ x = e^s \iff s = \ln x, \qquad \frac{ds}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}. $$

As $x$ runs over $[1, 2]$, the new variable $s = \ln x$ runs over $[\ln 1, \ln 2] = [0, \ln 2]$. Writing $\Phi(s) = \phi(e^s)$ (and denoting derivatives with respect to $s$ by subscripts $\Phi_s, \Phi_{ss}$), we compute the first derivative by the chain rule:

$$ \phi'(x) = \frac{d\phi}{dx} = \frac{d\Phi}{ds}\frac{ds}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}\Phi_s(s). $$

Next, we compute the second derivative using the product rule and the chain rule:

$$ \phi''(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{x}\Phi_s(s)\right) = -\frac{1}{x^2}\Phi_s(s) + \frac{1}{x}\frac{d\Phi_s}{ds}\frac{ds}{dx} = -\frac{1}{x^2}\Phi_s(s) + \frac{1}{x^2}\Phi_{ss}(s). $$

We substitute these expressions into $x^2\phi''(x) + x\phi'(x) + \lambda\phi(x) = 0$:

$$ x^2\left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\Phi_s + \frac{1}{x^2}\Phi_{ss}\right) + x\left(\frac{1}{x}\Phi_s\right) + \lambda\Phi = (-\Phi_s + \Phi_{ss}) + \Phi_s + \lambda\Phi = 0. $$

The first-derivative terms cancel identically, leaving the standard Dirichlet problem on $[0, \ln 2]$:

$$ \Phi_{ss}(s) + \lambda\Phi(s) = 0, \qquad 0 < s < \ln 2, \qquad \Phi(0) = \Phi(\ln 2) = 0. $$

Since we already proved $\lambda \ge 0$, we only need to analyse $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda > 0$.

Case $\lambda = 0$

When $\lambda = 0$, the transformed ODE is $\Phi_{ss}(s) = 0$, whose general solution is $\Phi(s) = A + Bs$. Applying the boundary conditions:

$$ \Phi(0) = A = 0, \qquad \Phi(\ln 2) = B\ln 2 = 0. $$

Since $\ln 2 \neq 0$, this forces $B = 0$, so $\Phi(s) \equiv 0$ (and $\phi(x) \equiv 0$). Therefore $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.

(Remark: This also follows directly from the Rayleigh quotient. If $\lambda = 0$, then $\int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx = 0$; since $x[\phi'(x)]^2 \ge 0$ is continuous, $\phi'(x) \equiv 0$, so $\phi(x)$ is constant, and $\phi(1) = 0$ forces $\phi(x) \equiv 0$.)

Case $\lambda > 0$

Set $\lambda = \mu^2$ with $\mu > 0$. The characteristic equation $r^2 + \mu^2 = 0$ gives $r = \pm i\mu$, so the general solution is:

$$ \Phi(s) = A\cos(\mu s) + B\sin(\mu s). $$

Applying the left boundary condition $\Phi(0) = 0$ gives $A = 0$, leaving $\Phi(s) = B\sin(\mu s)$. Applying the right boundary condition $\Phi(\ln 2) = 0$ yields:

$$ \Phi(\ln 2) = B\sin(\mu\ln 2) = 0. $$

For a non-trivial solution ($B \neq 0$), we require $\sin(\mu\ln 2) = 0$. Since $\mu > 0$ and $\ln 2 > 0$, the positive solutions are:

$$ \mu_n\ln 2 = n\pi \implies \mu_n = \frac{n\pi}{\ln 2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Squaring $\mu_n$ gives all eigenvalues of the problem:

$$ \lambda_n = \left(\frac{n\pi}{\ln 2}\right)^2, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Part 3: Eigenfunctions

Setting $B = 1$ in $\Phi_n(s) = B\sin(\mu_n s)$ and substituting back $s = \ln x$ gives the eigenfunctions in the original variable $x$:

$$ \phi_n(x) = \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi\ln x}{\ln 2}\right), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots $$

Answers and checks

Direct substitution check. Let $\mu_n = \frac{n\pi}{\ln 2}$ so that $\phi_n(x) = \sin(\mu_n\ln x)$ and $\lambda_n = \mu_n^2$. Differentiating twice with respect to $x$ by the chain and product rules:

$$ \phi_n'(x) = \cos(\mu_n\ln x)\cdot\frac{\mu_n}{x} = \frac{\mu_n}{x}\cos(\mu_n\ln x), $$

$$ \phi_n''(x) = -\frac{\mu_n}{x^2}\cos(\mu_n\ln x) - \frac{\mu_n^2}{x^2}\sin(\mu_n\ln x). $$

Substituting into $x^2\phi_n''(x) + x\phi_n'(x) + \lambda_n\phi_n(x)$:

$$ x^2\left(-\frac{\mu_n}{x^2}\cos(\mu_n\ln x) - \frac{\mu_n^2}{x^2}\sin(\mu_n\ln x)\right) + x\left(\frac{\mu_n}{x}\cos(\mu_n\ln x)\right) + \mu_n^2\sin(\mu_n\ln x) $$

$$ = \bigl(-\mu_n + \mu_n\bigr)\cos(\mu_n\ln x) + \bigl(-\mu_n^2 + \mu_n^2\bigr)\sin(\mu_n\ln x) = 0. $$

Boundary-condition check.

  • At $x = 1$: $\ln 1 = 0 \implies \phi_n(1) = \sin(0) = 0$.
  • At $x = 2$: $\phi_n(2) = \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi\ln 2}{\ln 2}\right) = \sin(n\pi) = 0$.

Dimensional check. In $x^2\phi'' + x\phi' + \lambda\phi = 0$, every term $x^2\phi''$ and $x\phi'$ has the same dimensions as $\phi$ regardless of the units of $x$, so $\lambda$ is dimensionless; likewise $\frac{\ln x}{\ln 2}$ is dimensionless.

Key results
  1. Sturm-Liouville form: Multiplying by $1/x$ yields $[x\phi'(x)]' + \frac{\lambda}{x}\phi(x) = 0$, with $p(x) = x > 0$, $\sigma(x) = 1/x > 0$, and $q(x) = 0$.
  2. Non-negativity and eigenvalues: The Rayleigh quotient gives $\lambda = \frac{\int_1^2 x[\phi'(x)]^2\,dx}{\int_1^2 [\phi(x)]^2/x\,dx} \ge 0$. $\lambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue. All eigenvalues are strictly positive: $$\lambda_n = \left(\frac{n\pi}{\ln 2}\right)^2, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
  3. Eigenfunctions: $$\phi_n(x) = \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi\ln x}{\ln 2}\right), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Nine Fourier Series Expansions

Euler-Fourier Formulas • Nine Functions • Jump Convergence
Statement

Calculate the Fourier series for the given functions:

  • (i) $f(x) = 1$, $-\pi \le x \le \pi$.
  • (ii) $f(x) = x$, $-\pi \le x \le \pi$.
  • (iii) $f(x) = \pi - x$, $-\pi \le x \le \pi$.
  • (iv) $f(x) = |x|$, $-\pi \le x \le \pi$.
  • (v) $f(x) = x^2$, $-\pi \le x \le \pi$.
  • (vi) $f(x) = \cos x$, $0 \le x \le \pi$.
  • (vii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & -\pi \le x < 0, \\ \pi, & 0 \le x \le \pi. \end{cases}$
  • (viii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} -\pi/2, & -\pi \le x < 0, \\ \pi/2, & 0 \le x \le \pi. \end{cases}$
  • (ix) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le x \le \pi/2, \\ 2, & \pi/2 \le x \le \pi. \end{cases}$
Extension of f

We begin by identifying the domain, period, and parity of each function so we know which periodic extension $\tilde{f}(x)$ the Fourier series represents and which coefficients vanish by symmetry (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, pp. 10–12; Haberman Sec. 3.2–3.3).

For a piecewise smooth function on $[-L, L]$, the full Fourier series is

$$ f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L} + b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right], $$

with the Euler-Fourier coefficients

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx, \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx. $$

For cases (i)–(v), (vii), and (viii), the domain is $[-\pi, \pi]$, so $L = \pi$ and $\frac{n\pi x}{L} = nx$; the series represents the $2\pi$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}(x + 2\pi) = \tilde{f}(x)$. For cases (vi) and (ix), the function is given only on the half-interval $[0, \pi]$ (Haberman Sec. 3.3). Because the statement does not specify which extension is requested on $[0, \pi]$, we compute all three standard expansions:

  1. Even $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier cosine series on $[0, \pi]$, $L = \pi$, $b_n = 0$).
  2. Odd $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier sine series on $[0, \pi]$, $L = \pi$, $a_0 = a_n = 0$).
  3. Direct $\pi$-periodic full Fourier series on $[0, \pi]$ (half-period $L = \pi/2$, basis $\cos(2nx), \sin(2nx)$).

Coefficients a_0, a_n, b_n

We now evaluate $a_0$, $a_n$, and $b_n$ for each function in turn, showing every antiderivative, integration by parts, and application of Barrow's rule explicitly.

(i) $f(x) = 1$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

We compute $a_0$ directly by integrating the constant $1$ over $[-\pi, \pi]$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\Big[x\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\big(\pi - (-\pi)\big) = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi} = 1. $$

Next we compute $a_n$ for $n \ge 1$ using the antiderivative $\int \cos(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{n}\sin(nx)$ and the identity $\sin(n\pi) = 0$.

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{n\pi}\big(\sin(n\pi) - \sin(-n\pi)\big) = \frac{1}{n\pi}(0 - 0) = 0. $$

Next we compute $b_n$ for $n \ge 1$ using the antiderivative $\int \sin(nx)\,dx = -\frac{1}{n}\cos(nx)$ and the parity $\cos(-n\pi) = \cos(n\pi)$.

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = -\frac{1}{n\pi}\big(\cos(n\pi) - \cos(-n\pi)\big) = 0. $$

(ii) $f(x) = x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

Because $f(-x) = -x = -f(x)$ is odd on the symmetric interval $[-\pi, \pi]$, both $f(x)$ and $f(x)\cos(nx)$ are odd integrands, so $a_0 = 0$ and $a_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$ (proved explicitly in Problem - Ch6-P9 Fourier Series of Even and Odd Periodic Functions). Meanwhile $x\sin(nx)$ is even, so we fold the integral onto $[0, \pi]$.

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx. $$

We integrate by parts to remove the linear factor $x$: choose $u = x$ and $dv = \sin(nx)\,dx$, which give $du = dx$ and $v = -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}$.

$$ \int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \left[-\frac{x\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} - \int_{0}^{\pi} \left(-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right)dx = \left[-\frac{x\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} + \frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi}. $$

Now we apply Barrow's rule to both bracketed terms using $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$, $\sin(n\pi) = 0$, and $\sin(0) = 0$.

$$ \int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \left(-\frac{\pi\cos(n\pi)}{n} - 0\right) + \frac{1}{n^2}\big(\sin(n\pi) - \sin(0)\big) = -\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n} + 0 = \frac{\pi(-1)^{n+1}}{n}. $$

Multiplying by the prefactor $\frac{2}{\pi}$ gives $b_n$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi(-1)^{n+1}}{n} = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

(iii) $f(x) = \pi - x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

We compute $a_0$ directly by integrating $\pi - x$ term by term on $[-\pi, \pi]$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (\pi - x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\pi x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\left(\pi^2 - \frac{\pi^2}{2}\right) - \left(-\pi^2 - \frac{\pi^2}{2}\right)\right] = \frac{2\pi^2}{2\pi} = \pi. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we split the integral by linearity into $\pi\cos(nx)$ (whose integral over $[-\pi, \pi]$ is $0$ from case (i)) and $-x\cos(nx)$ (an odd integrand on $[-\pi, \pi]$, whose integral is also $0$).

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (\pi - x)\cos(nx)\,dx = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(nx)\,dx - \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\cos(nx)\,dx = 0 - 0 = 0. $$

For $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we again split by linearity and reuse cases (i) and (ii).

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (\pi - x)\sin(nx)\,dx = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(nx)\,dx - \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = 0 - \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n} = \frac{2(-1)^n}{n}. $$

(iv) $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

Because $|-x| = |x|$, the function $f(x) = |x|$ is even on $[-\pi, \pi]$, so $|x|\sin(nx)$ is odd and $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$. For $a_0$ and $a_n$ we fold the even integrands onto $[0, \pi]$, where $|x| = x$.

We evaluate $a_0$ using the antiderivative $\int x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |x|\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{2} - 0\right) = \frac{\pi}{2}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we integrate by parts with $u = x$, $dv = \cos(nx)\,dx$, so $du = dx$ and $v = \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\cos(nx)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\left[\frac{x\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} - \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\,dx\right). $$

We evaluate the boundary term and integrate $-\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}$ explicitly, whose antiderivative is $+\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi\sin(n\pi) - 0}{n} + \left[\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}\right]_{0}^{\pi}\right) = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(0 + \frac{\cos(n\pi) - \cos(0)}{n^2}\right) = \frac{2\big((-1)^n - 1\big)}{\pi n^2}. $$

Separating even and odd $n$, we note that $(-1)^n - 1 = 0$ when $n = 2k$ is even and $(-1)^n - 1 = -2$ when $n = 2k+1$ is odd.

$$ a_n = \begin{cases} 0, & n \text{ even}, \\ -\dfrac{4}{\pi n^2}, & n \text{ odd}. \end{cases} $$

(v) $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

Because $(-x)^2 = x^2$, the function is even on $[-\pi, \pi]$, so $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$ and we fold $a_0$ and $a_n$ onto $[0, \pi]$.

We compute $a_0$ using the antiderivative $\int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3}$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi^3}{3} - 0\right) = \frac{\pi^2}{3}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we apply integration by parts a first time with $u_1 = x^2$, $dv_1 = \cos(nx)\,dx$, giving $du_1 = 2x\,dx$ and $v_1 = \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}$.

$$ \int_{0}^{\pi} x^2\cos(nx)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} - \frac{2}{n}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \left(\frac{\pi^2\sin(n\pi)}{n} - 0\right) - \frac{2}{n}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = -\frac{2}{n}\int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx. $$

We apply integration by parts a second time to $\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx$ with $u_2 = x$, $dv_2 = \sin(nx)\,dx$, giving $du_2 = dx$ and $v_2 = -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}$.

$$ \int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \left[-\frac{x\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} + \frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos(nx)\,dx = -\frac{\pi\cos(n\pi)}{n} + \frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = -\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n} + 0. $$

Substituting this back into $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi x^2\cos(nx)\,dx$ yields $a_n$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(-\frac{2}{n}\right)\left(-\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}\right) = \frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

(vi) $f(x) = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi]$

Because $f(x) = \cos x$ is given only on $[0, \pi]$, we compute all three standard Fourier expansions on this interval.

1. Even $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier cosine series on $[0, \pi]$)

Extending $\cos x$ evenly to $[-\pi, \pi]$ reproduces $\cos x$ itself, so $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$, and by orthogonality of $\{1, \cos(nx)\}$ on $[0, \pi]$ (proved in Problem - Ch6-P7 Orthogonality of Sine and Cosine Systems), we verify each coefficient explicitly.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\Big[\sin x\Big]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}(0 - 0) = 0. $$

For $n = 1$, we use the half-angle identity $\cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos(2x)}{2}$.

$$ a_1 = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos^2 x\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1 + \cos(2x)}{2}\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[x + \frac{\sin(2x)}{2}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\big((\pi + 0) - (0 + 0)\big) = 1. $$

For $n \ge 2$, we apply the product-to-sum identity $\cos x\cos(nx) = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos((n-1)x) + \cos((n+1)x)\big]$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\cos((n-1)x) + \cos((n+1)x)}{2}\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{\sin((n-1)x)}{n-1} + \frac{\sin((n+1)x)}{n+1}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = 0. $$

2. Odd $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier sine series on $[0, \pi]$)

For the odd extension, $a_0 = 0$ and $a_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$, while $b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\sin(nx)\,dx$.

First we check $n = 1$ using $\sin(2x) = 2\sin x\cos x$.

$$ b_1 = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\sin x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \sin(2x)\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\cos(2x)}{2}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\big(\cos(2\pi) - \cos(0)\big) = -\frac{1}{2\pi}(1 - 1) = 0. $$

For $n \ge 2$, we apply the product-to-sum identity $\sin A\cos B = \frac{1}{2}\big[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)\big]$ with $A = nx$ and $B = x$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin((n+1)x) + \sin((n-1)x)}{2}\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\cos((n+1)x)}{n+1} - \frac{\cos((n-1)x)}{n-1}\right]_{0}^{\pi}. $$

We apply Barrow's rule using $\cos((n+1)\pi) = (-1)^{n+1} = -(-1)^n$ and $\cos((n-1)\pi) = (-1)^{n-1} = -(-1)^n$.

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{1 - (-1)^{n+1}}{n+1} + \frac{1 - (-1)^{n-1}}{n-1}\right] = \frac{1 + (-1)^n}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{n+1} + \frac{1}{n-1}\right) = \frac{1 + (-1)^n}{\pi}\cdot\frac{2n}{n^2 - 1} = \frac{2n\big(1 + (-1)^n\big)}{\pi(n^2 - 1)}. $$

When $n$ is odd, $1 + (-1)^n = 0$, so $b_{2k+1} = 0$. When $n = 2k$ ($k \ge 1$) is even, $1 + (-1)^{2k} = 2$, so

$$ b_{2k} = \frac{2(2k)\cdot 2}{\pi((2k)^2 - 1)} = \frac{8k}{\pi(4k^2 - 1)}, \qquad k \ge 1. $$

3. Direct $\pi$-periodic full Fourier series on $[0, \pi]$ ($L = \pi/2$)

Taking $[0, \pi]$ itself as one full period $2L = \pi$ ($L = \pi/2$), the basis functions are $\cos(2nx)$ and $\sin(2nx)$ with $\frac{1}{2L} = \frac{1}{\pi}$ and $\frac{1}{L} = \frac{2}{\pi}$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\Big[\sin x\Big]_{0}^{\pi} = 0. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we apply $\cos x\cos(2nx) = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos((2n-1)x) + \cos((2n+1)x)\big]$; since $2n \pm 1 \neq 0$, every sine vanishes at $0$ and $\pi$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\cos(2nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{\sin((2n-1)x)}{2n-1} + \frac{\sin((2n+1)x)}{2n+1}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = 0. $$

For $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we use $\sin(2nx)\cos x = \frac{1}{2}\big[\sin((2n+1)x) + \sin((2n-1)x)\big]$ and note that $\cos((2n\pm 1)\pi) = -1$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos x\sin(2nx)\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\cos((2n+1)x)}{2n+1} - \frac{\cos((2n-1)x)}{2n-1}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{2n+1} + \frac{2}{2n-1}\right) = \frac{8n}{\pi(4n^2 - 1)}. $$

Notice that the direct $\pi$-periodic series coincides identically with the odd $2\pi$-periodic sine series because the odd extension of $\cos x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ already has period $\pi$!

(vii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & -\pi \le x < 0, \\ \pi, & 0 \le x \le \pi \end{cases}$

Because $f(x) = 0$ on $[-\pi, 0)$, every integral collapses to $[0, \pi]$ where $f(x) = \pi$.

We compute $a_0$ directly.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \pi\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\Big[x\Big]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{\pi}{2}. $$

Next we compute $a_n$ for $n \ge 1$.

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \pi\cos(nx)\,dx = \left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{\sin(n\pi) - \sin(0)}{n} = 0. $$

Next we compute $b_n$ for $n \ge 1$ using $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$ and $\cos(0) = 1$.

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \pi\sin(nx)\,dx = \left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = -\frac{\cos(n\pi) - \cos(0)}{n} = \frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n}. $$

Thus $b_n = 0$ for even $n$ and $b_{2k+1} = \frac{2}{2k+1}$ for odd $n = 2k+1$ ($k \ge 0$).

(viii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} -\pi/2, & -\pi \le x < 0, \\ \pi/2, & 0 \le x \le \pi \end{cases}$

Observe that $f_{\text{viii}}(x) = f_{\text{vii}}(x) - \frac{\pi}{2}$ for all $x \in [-\pi, \pi]$, and moreover $f_{\text{viii}}(-x) = -f_{\text{viii}}(x)$ for $x \neq 0$, so $f_{\text{viii}}$ is odd on $[-\pi, \pi]$. We verify $a_0$, $a_n$, and $b_n$ directly by splitting at $x = 0$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{0} \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)dx + \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\pi}{2}\,dx\right) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{\pi^2}{2} + \frac{\pi^2}{2}\right) = 0. $$

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{-\pi}^{0} + \frac{\pi}{2}\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi}\right) = 0. $$

For $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), since the integrand is even, we fold onto $[0, \pi]$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\pi}{2}\sin(nx)\,dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin(nx)\,dx = \left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n}. $$

Thus $b_{2k} = 0$ and $b_{2k+1} = \frac{2}{2k+1}$, identical to case (vii) except that the constant term $a_0$ shifts from $\pi/2$ to $0$.

(ix) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le x \le \pi/2, \\ 2, & \pi/2 \le x \le \pi \end{cases}$

Because $f$ is given only on $[0, \pi]$, we again compute all three standard expansions.

1. Even $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier cosine series on $[0, \pi]$)

Here $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$. We split the integrals at $x = \pi/2$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} 1\,dx + \int_{\pi/2}^{\pi} 2\,dx\right) = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{3}{2}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we integrate $\cos(nx)$ on each subinterval and apply Barrow's rule.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos(nx)\,dx + \int_{\pi/2}^{\pi} 2\cos(nx)\,dx\right) = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi/2} + 2\left[\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\right). $$

Evaluating at the endpoints using $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\sin(n\pi) = 0$:

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{n\pi}\left(\sin\frac{n\pi}{2} - 0 + 2\left(0 - \sin\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)\right) = -\frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi}. $$

When $n$ is even, $\sin(n\pi/2) = 0$, so $a_{2k} = 0$. When $n = 2k+1$ ($k \ge 0$) is odd, $\sin\big((2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}\big) = (-1)^k$, so

$$ a_{2k+1} = -\frac{2(-1)^k}{(2k+1)\pi}, \qquad k \ge 0. $$

2. Odd $2\pi$-periodic extension (Fourier sine series on $[0, \pi]$)

Here $a_0 = 0$ and $a_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$, and we split $b_n$ at $x = \pi/2$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin(nx)\,dx + \int_{\pi/2}^{\pi} 2\sin(nx)\,dx\right) = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{0}^{\pi/2} + 2\left[-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\right). $$

Applying Barrow's rule with $\cos(0) = 1$ and $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$:

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{n\pi}\left(1 - \cos\frac{n\pi}{2} + 2\left(\cos\frac{n\pi}{2} - (-1)^n\right)\right) = \frac{2\big(1 + \cos(n\pi/2) - 2(-1)^n\big)}{n\pi}. $$

3. Direct $\pi$-periodic full Fourier series on $[0, \pi]$ ($L = \pi/2$)

With $L = \pi/2$, the basis is $\cos(2nx)$ and $\sin(2nx)$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\pi/2} 1\,dx + \int_{\pi/2}^{\pi} 2\,dx\right) = \frac{3}{2}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), every boundary sine vanishes because $\sin(n\pi) = 0$ and $\sin(2n\pi) = 0$.

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\left[\frac{\sin(2nx)}{2n}\right]_{0}^{\pi/2} + 2\left[\frac{\sin(2nx)}{2n}\right]_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\right) = \frac{1}{n\pi}\big((\sin(n\pi) - 0) + 2(\sin(2n\pi) - \sin(n\pi))\big) = 0. $$

For $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we use $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$ and $\cos(2n\pi) = 1$.

$$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\left[-\frac{\cos(2nx)}{2n}\right]_{0}^{\pi/2} + 2\left[-\frac{\cos(2nx)}{2n}\right]_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\right) = \frac{1}{n\pi}\big((1 - (-1)^n) + 2((-1)^n - 1)\big) = -\frac{1 - (-1)^n}{n\pi}. $$

Thus $b_{2k} = 0$ and $b_{2k+1} = -\frac{2}{(2k+1)\pi}$ ($k \ge 0$).

The series

Assembling the coefficients from the previous section gives the nine Fourier series:

  • (i) $f(x) = 1$:

$$ 1 \sim 1. $$

  • (ii) $f(x) = x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ x \sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx) = 2\left(\sin x - \frac{\sin(2x)}{2} + \frac{\sin(3x)}{3} - \cdots\right). $$

  • (iii) $f(x) = \pi - x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ \pi - x \sim \pi + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n}}{n}\sin(nx) = \pi - 2\left(\sin x - \frac{\sin(2x)}{2} + \frac{\sin(3x)}{3} - \cdots\right). $$

  • (iv) $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ |x| \sim \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\cos((2k+1)x)}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\cos x + \frac{\cos(3x)}{9} + \frac{\cos(5x)}{25} + \cdots\right). $$

  • (v) $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ x^2 \sim \frac{\pi^2}{3} + 4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos(nx) = \frac{\pi^2}{3} - 4\left(\cos x - \frac{\cos(2x)}{4} + \frac{\cos(3x)}{9} - \cdots\right). $$

  • (vi) $f(x) = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi]$:
  • Even (cosine) extension of (vi): $\cos x \sim \cos x$.
  • Odd (sine) extension and direct $\pi$-periodic extension of (vi) (identical):

$$ \cos x \sim \frac{8}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{4k^2 - 1}\sin(2kx) = \frac{8}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{3}\sin(2x) + \frac{2}{15}\sin(4x) + \frac{3}{35}\sin(6x) + \cdots\right). $$

  • (vii) Step function $0$ on $[-\pi, 0)$, $\pi$ on $[0, \pi]$:

$$ f(x) \sim \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin((2k+1)x)}{2k+1} = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2\left(\sin x + \frac{\sin(3x)}{3} + \frac{\sin(5x)}{5} + \cdots\right). $$

  • (viii) Odd step function $-\pi/2$ on $[-\pi, 0)$, $\pi/2$ on $[0, \pi]$:

$$ f(x) \sim 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin((2k+1)x)}{2k+1} = 2\left(\sin x + \frac{\sin(3x)}{3} + \frac{\sin(5x)}{5} + \cdots\right). $$

  • (ix) Step function $1$ on $[0, \pi/2]$, $2$ on $[\pi/2, \pi]$:
  • Even (cosine) extension of (ix):

$$ f(x) \sim \frac{3}{2} - \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\cos\big((2k+1)x\big). $$

  • Odd (sine) extension of (ix):

$$ f(x) \sim \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1 + \cos(n\pi/2) - 2(-1)^n}{n}\sin(nx). $$

  • Direct $\pi$-periodic extension of (ix) ($L = \pi/2$):

$$ f(x) \sim \frac{3}{2} - \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\sin\big(2(2k+1)x\big)}{2k+1}. $$

Convergence at the jumps

By Fourier's convergence theorem (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, pp. 12–13; Haberman Sec. 3.2), the Fourier series of a piecewise smooth function converges to $\tilde{f}(x)$ wherever the periodic extension $\tilde{f}$ is continuous, and to the midpoint $\frac{1}{2}\big[\tilde{f}(x^+) + \tilde{f}(x^-)\big]$ at every jump discontinuity.

  • (i), (iv), (v): The $2\pi$-periodic extensions of $1$, $|x|$, and $x^2$ are continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ because $f(-\pi) = f(\pi)$ in each case. Hence the series converge to $\tilde{f}(x)$ at every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ (with no jumps).
  • (ii): The periodic extension of $x$ jumps from $+\pi$ to $-\pi$ at $x = (2k+1)\pi$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), where the series converges to $\frac{\pi + (-\pi)}{2} = 0$ (matching $\sin(n(2k+1)\pi) = 0$).
  • (iii): The periodic extension of $\pi - x$ jumps from $0$ to $2\pi$ at $x = (2k+1)\pi$, where the series converges to $\frac{0 + 2\pi}{2} = \pi$.
  • (vi): The cosine series equals $\cos x$ everywhere (continuous). The sine / $\pi$-periodic series has jumps at $x = m\pi$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$) between $-1$ and $+1$, where it converges to $\frac{-1 + 1}{2} = 0$.
  • (vii): The $2\pi$-periodic step function jumps between $0$ and $\pi$ at every integer multiple $x = m\pi$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$), where the series converges to $\frac{0 + \pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

  • (ix): At the interior jump $x = \pi/2$, all three extensions jump between $1$ and $2$ and converge to $\frac{1 + 2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}$. Exercise 6

Fourier Series via Trigonometric Identities

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf, p. 2
Uniqueness • Trigonometric Identities • No Integrals
Statement

Compute the Fourier series of the following functions in $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ f(x) = \sin 2x, \qquad f(x) = 2 + 3\cos(2x), \qquad f(x) = \sin(4x), \qquad f(x) = 8\cos^3(3x). $$

Can you compute the Fourier series without using the integral formulas for the coefficients in these cases?

Classification of the problem

All four functions are smooth, $2\pi$-periodic functions on $[-\pi, \pi]$ ($L = \pi$) that are finite linear combinations of the Fourier basis elements $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx) : n = 1, 2, \dots\}$—in the fourth case after reducing $\cos^3(3x)$ via elementary trigonometric identities (Haberman Sec. 3.2; Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 10).

Method

Yes, we can compute every Fourier series here without evaluating any Euler-Fourier integral, by appealing to the uniqueness of the Fourier expansion on $[-\pi, \pi]$.

Suppose a function $f(x)$ can be written as a finite trigonometric polynomial

$$ f(x) = c_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} \big[c_k \cos(kx) + d_k \sin(kx)\big]. $$

If we substitute this finite sum into the Euler-Fourier formulas on $[-\pi, \pi]$,

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\,dx, \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx, $$

linearity of the integral and the orthogonality relations of $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ (proved explicitly in Problem - Ch6-P7 Orthogonality of Sine and Cosine Systems) immediately yield

$$ a_0 = c_0, \qquad a_n = c_n, \qquad b_n = d_n \quad (1 \le n \le N), \qquad a_n = b_n = 0 \quad (n > N). $$

Therefore, any finite linear combination of $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ is its own Fourier series. Our task is simply to rewrite each given function in that basis and read off the coefficients directly.

Step-by-step derivation

We examine each of the four functions in turn.

1. $f(x) = \sin(2x)$

This function is already a single basis element of the Fourier system $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$, namely $\sin(nx)$ with $n = 2$ and coefficient $1$.

Matching terms against $a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n\cos(nx) + b_n\sin(nx)]$, we read off

$$ a_0 = 0, \qquad a_n = 0 \;\; (n \ge 1), \qquad b_2 = 1, \qquad b_n = 0 \;\; (n \neq 2). $$

Hence the Fourier series is simply

$$ f(x) = \sin(2x). $$

2. $f(x) = 2 + 3\cos(2x)$

This function is already a finite linear combination of the constant basis element $1$ (with coefficient $2$) and the cosine basis element $\cos(2x)$ (with coefficient $3$).

Matching terms against $a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n\cos(nx) + b_n\sin(nx)]$, we read off

$$ a_0 = 2, \qquad a_2 = 3, \qquad a_n = 0 \;\; (n \neq 2), \qquad b_n = 0 \;\; (n \ge 1). $$

Hence the Fourier series is simply

$$ f(x) = 2 + 3\cos(2x). $$

3. $f(x) = \sin(4x)$

This function is already the single basis element $\sin(4x)$ ($n = 4$) with coefficient $1$.

Matching terms against the Fourier expansion gives

$$ a_0 = 0, \qquad a_n = 0 \;\; (n \ge 1), \qquad b_4 = 1, \qquad b_n = 0 \;\; (n \neq 4). $$

Hence the Fourier series is simply

$$ f(x) = \sin(4x). $$

4. $f(x) = 8\cos^3(3x)$

Because $\cos^3(3x)$ is a third power rather than a linear combination of $\cos(nx)$, we first derive the power-reduction identity for $\cos^3\theta$ step by step.

First we split $\cos^3\theta = \cos^2\theta \cdot \cos\theta$ and replace $\cos^2\theta$ using the half-angle identity $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$ (which follows from $\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$).

$$ \cos^3\theta = \cos^2\theta \cdot \cos\theta = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}\cos\theta + \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta)\cos\theta. $$

Next we expand the product $\cos(2\theta)\cos\theta$ using the product-to-sum identity

$$ \cos A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(A + B) + \cos(A - B)\big], $$

which follows by adding the cosine addition formulas $\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$ and $\cos(A-B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B$ and dividing by $2$. Setting $A = 2\theta$ and $B = \theta$ gives

$$ \cos(2\theta)\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(2\theta + \theta) + \cos(2\theta - \theta)\big] = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(3\theta) + \cos\theta\big]. $$

Substituting this back into our expression for $\cos^3\theta$ and collecting the $\cos\theta$ terms yields

$$ \cos^3\theta = \frac{1}{2}\cos\theta + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(3\theta) + \cos\theta\big] = \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}\right)\cos\theta + \frac{1}{4}\cos(3\theta) = \frac{3}{4}\cos\theta + \frac{1}{4}\cos(3\theta). $$

Now we substitute $\theta = 3x$ and multiply both sides by $8$:

$$ 8\cos^3(3x) = 8\left(\frac{3}{4}\cos(3x) + \frac{1}{4}\cos(9x)\right) = 6\cos(3x) + 2\cos(9x). $$

This is now a finite linear combination of the Fourier basis functions $\cos(3x)$ and $\cos(9x)$. By uniqueness, we read off the coefficients directly:

$$ a_0 = 0, \qquad a_3 = 6, \qquad a_9 = 2, \qquad a_n = 0 \;\; (n \notin \{3, 9\}), \qquad b_n = 0 \;\; (n \ge 1). $$

Hence the Fourier series is

$$ 8\cos^3(3x) = 6\cos(3x) + 2\cos(9x). $$

Answers and checks

We verify the fourth identity numerically at two independent test points, and confirm parity and convergence for all four functions:

  1. Test at $x = 0$: - Original function: $8\cos^3(3\cdot 0) = 8\cos^3(0) = 8(1)^3 = 8$. - Fourier expansion: $6\cos(0) + 2\cos(0) = 6 + 2 = 8$. Exact match.
  2. Test at $x = \pi/9$: - Original function: $8\cos^3\!\left(3\cdot\frac{\pi}{9}\right) = 8\cos^3\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = 8\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 8\cdot\frac{1}{8} = 1$. - Fourier expansion: $6\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) + 2\cos(\pi) = 6\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + 2(-1) = 3 - 2 = 1$. Exact match.
  3. Parity check: $\sin(2x)$ and $\sin(4x)$ are odd functions (pure sine terms, $a_0 = a_n = 0$); $2 + 3\cos(2x)$ and $8\cos^3(3x)$ are even functions (pure cosine terms, $b_n = 0$). All four are smooth and $2\pi$-periodic, so each Fourier series equals $f(x)$ identically for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
Key results
  • Can we avoid the integrals? Yes, because the orthogonal basis $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ makes the Fourier expansion of any finite trigonometric polynomial unique: each function is its own Fourier series once expressed as a linear combination of basis terms.
  • $\sin(2x) = \sin(2x)$ ($b_2 = 1$, all other coefficients $0$).
  • $2 + 3\cos(2x) = 2 + 3\cos(2x)$ ($a_0 = 2,\; a_2 = 3$, all other coefficients $0$).
  • $\sin(4x) = \sin(4x)$ ($b_4 = 1$, all other coefficients $0$).
  • $8\cos^3(3x) = 6\cos(3x) + 2\cos(9x)$ ($a_3 = 6,\; a_9 = 2$, all other coefficients $0$).
Exercise 7

Orthogonality of Sine and Cosine Systems

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf, p. 2
Orthogonality • Product-to-Sum • Barrow Rule
Statement

Show that the functions $y_n(x) = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ satisfy

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} y_n(x)y_m(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n \neq m, \\ L, & n = m, \end{cases} $$

for $m, n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ Show that a similar property holds for $u_n(x) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$:

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} u_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n \neq m, \\ L, & n = m \neq 0, \\ 2L, & n = m = 0, \end{cases} $$

for $m, n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$

Classification of the problem

This is a direct verification of the $L^2([-L, L])$ orthogonality and normalization integrals of the trigonometric Fourier basis $\left\{\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right\}$ (Haberman, Appendix to Sec. 2.3, p. 49, and Sec. 3.2, p. 77). These identities are the exact algebraic foundation of the Euler-Fourier coefficient formulas (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 10).

Method

To evaluate products of sines and cosines without integration by parts, we convert every product into a sum or difference of single cosines using the product-to-sum identities:

$$ \sin A \sin B = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B)\big], \qquad \cos A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}\big[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)\big]. $$

These follow immediately from the cosine addition and subtraction formulas:

$$ \cos(A - B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B, \qquad \cos(A + B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B. $$

Subtracting the second from the first and dividing by $2$ gives the $\sin A\sin B$ identity; adding them and dividing by $2$ gives the $\cos A\cos B$ identity.

We must separate the cases where the angular frequency $(n - m)\frac{\pi}{L}$ or $(n + m)\frac{\pi}{L}$ is zero (so $\int_{-L}^L 1\,dx = 2L$) from the cases where it is a nonzero integer multiple $k\frac{\pi}{L}$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$), where

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} \cos\left(\frac{k\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \left[\frac{L}{k\pi}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L} = \frac{L}{k\pi}\big(\sin(k\pi) - \sin(-k\pi)\big) = \frac{L}{k\pi}(0 - 0) = 0. $$

Step-by-step derivation
1. Orthogonality of the sine system $y_n(x) = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$

Let $I_{nm} = \int_{-L}^{L} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\sin\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)dx$ for integers $m, n \ge 0$.

Case 1a: $n = 0$ or $m = 0$

If either $n = 0$ or $m = 0$, then $y_0(x) = \sin(0) \equiv 0$ identically on $[-L, L]$, so the integrand vanishes everywhere and

$$ I_{nm} = \int_{-L}^{L} 0\,dx = 0. $$

In particular, when $n = m = 0$, we obtain $I_{00} = 0$ (not $L$; see the erratum note below).

Sheet erratum for $n = m = 0$ in the sine identity

The problem sheet writes $m, n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ with value $L$ for $n = m$. However, for $n = m = 0$, $y_0(x) = \sin(0) \equiv 0$, so $\int_{-L}^{L} y_0(x)^2\,dx = 0 \neq L$. The normalization value $L$ holds for $n = m \ge 1$, while $n = 0$ is excluded from the sine basis (which starts at $n = 1$).

Case 1b: $n \neq m$ with $n, m \ge 1$

We apply the product-to-sum identity with $A = \frac{n\pi x}{L}$ and $B = \frac{m\pi x}{L}$.

$$ I_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[\cos\left(\frac{(n - m)\pi x}{L}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{(n + m)\pi x}{L}\right)\right]dx. $$

Since $n \neq m$ and $n, m \ge 1$, both integers $n - m \neq 0$ and $n + m \ge 2 \neq 0$ are nonzero, so we integrate both cosines explicitly using $\int \cos(\alpha x)\,dx = \frac{\sin(\alpha x)}{\alpha}$.

$$ I_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{L}{(n - m)\pi}\sin\left(\frac{(n - m)\pi x}{L}\right) - \frac{L}{(n + m)\pi}\sin\left(\frac{(n + m)\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L}. $$

Applying Barrow's rule $[F]_{-L}^{L} = F(L) - F(-L)$ and using $\sin(k\pi) = 0$ and $\sin(-k\pi) = 0$ for every integer $k$:

$$ I_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{L}{(n - m)\pi}\big(\sin((n-m)\pi) - \sin(-(n-m)\pi)\big) - \frac{L}{(n + m)\pi}\big(\sin((n+m)\pi) - \sin(-(n+m)\pi)\big)\right] = \frac{1}{2}(0 - 0) = 0. $$

Case 1c: $n = m \ge 1$

When $n = m \ge 1$, the first angle is $A - B = 0$, so $\cos(0) = 1$, and the product-to-sum formula reduces to the half-angle identity $\sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 - \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]$.

$$ I_{nn} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[1 - \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]dx = \frac{1}{2}\left[x - \frac{L}{2n\pi}\sin\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L}. $$

Applying Barrow's rule with $\sin(2n\pi) = 0$ and $\sin(-2n\pi) = 0$:

$$ I_{nn} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\big(L - (-L)\big) - \frac{L}{2n\pi}\big(\sin(2n\pi) - \sin(-2n\pi)\big)\right] = \frac{1}{2}(2L - 0) = L. $$

2. Orthogonality of the cosine system $u_n(x) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$

Let $J_{nm} = \int_{-L}^{L} \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)dx$ for integers $m, n \ge 0$.

Case 2a: $n = m = 0$

Here $u_0(x) = \cos(0) = 1$, so we integrate the constant $1$ over $[-L, L]$.

$$ J_{00} = \int_{-L}^{L} 1 \cdot 1\,dx = \Big[x\Big]_{-L}^{L} = L - (-L) = 2L. $$

Case 2b: $n \neq m$ with $n, m \ge 0$

We apply the product-to-sum identity $\cos A\cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]$ with $A = \frac{n\pi x}{L}$ and $B = \frac{m\pi x}{L}$.

$$ J_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[\cos\left(\frac{(n - m)\pi x}{L}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{(n + m)\pi x}{L}\right)\right]dx. $$

Because $n \neq m$ and both are nonnegative integers, $n - m \neq 0$ and at least one of $n, m$ is positive, so $n + m \ge 1 \neq 0$. Both denominators are nonzero, and we integrate term by term.

$$ J_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{L}{(n - m)\pi}\sin\left(\frac{(n - m)\pi x}{L}\right) + \frac{L}{(n + m)\pi}\sin\left(\frac{(n + m)\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L}. $$

Applying Barrow's rule with $\sin(k\pi) = \sin(-k\pi) = 0$ for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$ J_{nm} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{L}{(n - m)\pi}(0 - 0) + \frac{L}{(n + m)\pi}(0 - 0)\right] = 0. $$

(Note that if one index is $0$ and the other is $k \ge 1$, this directly reduces to $\int_{-L}^L \cos\left(\frac{k\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \left[\frac{L}{k\pi}\sin\left(\frac{k\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L} = 0$.)

Case 2c: $n = m \ge 1$

When $n = m \ge 1$, the identity reduces to $\cos^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]$.

$$ J_{nn} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-L}^{L} \left[1 + \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]dx = \frac{1}{2}\left[x + \frac{L}{2n\pi}\sin\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{-L}^{L}. $$

Applying Barrow's rule with $\sin(2n\pi) = \sin(-2n\pi) = 0$:

$$ J_{nn} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\big(L - (-L)\big) + \frac{L}{2n\pi}(0 - 0)\right] = \frac{1}{2}(2L) = L. $$

3. Bonus check: cross-orthogonality between sines and cosines

To complete the orthogonality of the full Fourier system on $[-L, L]$, we also check $K_{nm} = \int_{-L}^{L} y_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = \int_{-L}^{L} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)dx$ for all $n, m \ge 0$.

Because $\sin\left(\frac{n\pi(-x)}{L}\right)\cos\left(\frac{m\pi(-x)}{L}\right) = -\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)$, the integrand is an odd function on the symmetric interval $[-L, L]$. Splitting the integral at $0$ and substituting $u = -x$ ($du = -dx$, limits $x = -L \to u = L$ and $x = 0 \to u = 0$) gives

$$ K_{nm} = \int_{-L}^{0} y_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx + \int_{0}^{L} y_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = -\int_{0}^{L} y_n(u)u_m(u)\,du + \int_{0}^{L} y_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = 0 $$

for every $m, n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$

Answers and checks
  1. Dimensional check: If $x$ and $L$ carry length units $[\text{m}]$, then $\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ is dimensionless, $y_n(x)$ and $u_n(x)$ are dimensionless, and $\int_{-L}^{L} y_n(x)y_m(x)\,dx$ has units of $[\text{m}]$, matching $L$ and $2L$.
  2. Pythagorean sum check: For every $n \ge 1$, adding the two diagonal integrals gives

$$ I_{nn} + J_{nn} = \int_{-L}^{L} \left[\sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) + \cos^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]dx = \int_{-L}^{L} 1\,dx = 2L, $$

which matches $L + L = 2L$. For $n = 0$, $I_{00} + J_{00} = 0 + 2L = 2L$, confirming why $I_{00}$ must be $0$ and not $L$.

  1. Recovery of Euler-Fourier prefactors: Multiplying $f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}[a_m u_m(x) + b_m y_m(x)]$ by $1 = u_0(x)$, $u_n(x)$, and $y_n(x)$ and integrating over $[-L, L]$ yields $2L a_0$, $L a_n$, and $L b_n$, explaining why $a_0$ carries $\frac{1}{2L}$ while $a_n, b_n$ ($n \ge 1$) carry $\frac{1}{L}$.
Key results
  • Sine system $y_n(x) = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: $$\int_{-L}^{L} y_n(x)y_m(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n \neq m \text{ or } n = m = 0, \\ L, & n = m \ge 1. \end{cases}$$ (Note the sheet erratum: $I_{00} = 0 \neq L$ since $y_0(x) \equiv 0$.)
  • Cosine system $u_n(x) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: $$\int_{-L}^{L} u_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n \neq m, \\ L, & n = m \ge 1, \\ 2L, & n = m = 0. \end{cases}$$
  • Cross-orthogonality: $\int_{-L}^{L} y_n(x)u_m(x)\,dx = 0$ for all $m, n \ge 0$.
Exercise 8

Sketching Fourier Series vs Original Functions

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf, p. 2
Periodic Extension • Jump Midpoints • Three Sketches
Statement

Sketch the Fourier series of $f(x)$ on $[-L, L]$ for the given function, and compare with the graph of $f(x)$ itself.

  • (i) $f(x) = x^2$.
  • (ii) $f(x) = e^x$.
  • (iii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x < 0, \\ x + 1, & x \ge 0. \end{cases}$
Extension of f

To compare the graph of the Fourier series with the graph of $f(x)$ itself on $\mathbb{R}$, we first form the $2L$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}(x)$ of $f|_{[-L, L]}$ and check continuity both inside $(-L, L)$ and at the periodic joints $x = (2k+1)L$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$) (Haberman Sec. 3.2; Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 7 and pp. 12–13).

  • (i) $f(x) = x^2$: Smooth on $[-L, L]$ and even ($f(-x) = f(x)$). At the endpoints, $f(-L) = L^2 = f(L)$, so the $2L$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}$ is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ (with corners at $x = (2k+1)L$, where the left slope is $+2L$ and the right slope is $-2L$). Outside $[-L, L]$, the Fourier series repeats the parabolic arch on every $2L$-interval, whereas $f(x) = x^2$ keeps growing quadratically to $+\infty$.
  • (ii) $f(x) = e^x$: Smooth on $[-L, L]$ with no even/odd symmetry. At the endpoints, $f(-L) = e^{-L} \neq e^{L} = f(L)$, so the $2L$-periodic extension $\tilde{f}$ has jump discontinuities at every odd multiple $x = (2k+1)L$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), jumping from $e^L$ on the left to $e^{-L}$ on the right. Outside $[-L, L]$, the Fourier series is bounded and $2L$-periodic, whereas $e^x \to +\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$ and $e^x \to 0$ as $x \to -\infty$.
  • (iii) $f(x) = 1$ ($x < 0$), $x + 1$ ($x \ge 0$): Inside $(-L, L)$, we check continuity at $x = 0$: $\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = 1$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = 0 + 1 = 1$, so $f$ is continuous at $x = 0$ (with a corner where the slope jumps from $0$ to $1$). At the endpoints, $f(-L) = 1 \neq L + 1 = f(L)$, so the $2L$-periodic extension has jump discontinuities at every $x = (2k+1)L$, jumping from $L + 1$ on the left to $1$ on the right. Outside $[-L, L]$, the Fourier series repeats this ramp-and-plateau wave periodically, whereas $f(x)$ stays flat at $1$ for $x < -L$ and climbs linearly for $x > L$.
Coefficients a_0, a_n, b_n

We compute the exact Euler-Fourier coefficients on $[-L, L]$ using the lecturer's formulas (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 10):

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx, \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx. $$

(i) $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-L, L]$

Because $x^2$ is even on $[-L, L]$, $x^2\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ is odd and $b_n = 0$ for all $n \ge 1$. For $a_0$ and $a_n$ we fold the even integrands onto $[0, L]$.

We compute $a_0$ using $\int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3}$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{L} = \frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{L^3}{3} - 0\right) = \frac{L^2}{3}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), we write $\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ for clarity and apply integration by parts twice. First choose $u_1 = x^2$, $dv_1 = \cos(\omega_n x)\,dx$, giving $du_1 = 2x\,dx$ and $v_1 = \frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}$.

$$ \int_{0}^{L} x^2\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\right]_{0}^{L} - \frac{2}{\omega_n}\int_{0}^{L} x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx. $$

Since $\sin(\omega_n L) = \sin(n\pi) = 0$ and $\sin(0) = 0$, the boundary term vanishes. For the remaining integral we integrate by parts a second time with $u_2 = x$, $dv_2 = \sin(\omega_n x)\,dx$, giving $du_2 = dx$ and $v_2 = -\frac{\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}$.

$$ \int_{0}^{L} x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx = \left[-\frac{x\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\right]_{0}^{L} + \frac{1}{\omega_n}\int_{0}^{L} \cos(\omega_n x)\,dx = \left(-\frac{L\cos(n\pi)}{\omega_n} - 0\right) + \frac{1}{\omega_n}\left[\frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\right]_{0}^{L}. $$

Using $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$ and $\sin(n\pi) = \sin(0) = 0$, we obtain $\int_{0}^{L} x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx = -\frac{L(-1)^n}{\omega_n}$. Substituting back into $a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x^2\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx$ and replacing $\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ gives

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{L}\left(-\frac{2}{\omega_n}\right)\left(-\frac{L(-1)^n}{\omega_n}\right) = \frac{4(-1)^n}{\omega_n^2} = \frac{4L^2(-1)^n}{(n\pi)^2}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

(ii) $f(x) = e^x$ on $[-L, L]$

First we compute $a_0$ directly using $\int e^x\,dx = e^x$ and the definition $\sinh L = \frac{e^L - e^{-L}}{2}$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} e^x\,dx = \frac{1}{2L}\Big[e^x\Big]_{-L}^{L} = \frac{e^L - e^{-L}}{2L} = \frac{\sinh L}{L}. $$

To compute $a_n$ and $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), set $\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ and define the two integrals

$$ C_n = \int_{-L}^{L} e^x\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx, \qquad S_n = \int_{-L}^{L} e^x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx. $$

We integrate $C_n$ by parts with $u_1 = \cos(\omega_n x)$ and $dv_1 = e^x\,dx$, giving $du_1 = -\omega_n\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx$ and $v_1 = e^x$.

$$ C_n = \Big[e^x\cos(\omega_n x)\Big]_{-L}^{L} + \omega_n \int_{-L}^{L} e^x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx = \big(e^L\cos(n\pi) - e^{-L}\cos(-n\pi)\big) + \omega_n S_n. $$

Since $\cos(n\pi) = \cos(-n\pi) = (-1)^n$ and $e^L - e^{-L} = 2\sinh L$, this simplifies to

$$ C_n = 2(-1)^n\sinh L + \omega_n S_n. $$

Next we integrate $S_n$ by parts with $u_2 = \sin(\omega_n x)$ and $dv_2 = e^x\,dx$, giving $du_2 = \omega_n\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx$ and $v_2 = e^x$.

$$ S_n = \Big[e^x\sin(\omega_n x)\Big]_{-L}^{L} - \omega_n \int_{-L}^{L} e^x\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx = \big(e^L\sin(n\pi) - e^{-L}\sin(-n\pi)\big) - \omega_n C_n = -\omega_n C_n, $$

because $\sin(n\pi) = \sin(-n\pi) = 0$. Substituting $S_n = -\omega_n C_n$ into our equation for $C_n$ lets us solve algebraically for $C_n$ and $S_n$:

$$ C_n = 2(-1)^n\sinh L - \omega_n^2 C_n \implies (1 + \omega_n^2)C_n = 2(-1)^n\sinh L \implies C_n = \frac{2(-1)^n\sinh L}{1 + \omega_n^2}, $$

$$ S_n = -\omega_n C_n = -\frac{2\omega_n(-1)^n\sinh L}{1 + \omega_n^2}. $$

Now substitute $\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ (so $1 + \omega_n^2 = \frac{L^2 + (n\pi)^2}{L^2}$) and divide by $L$:

$$ a_n = \frac{C_n}{L} = \frac{2(-1)^n\sinh L}{L\left(1 + \frac{(n\pi)^2}{L^2}\right)} = \frac{2L(-1)^n\sinh L}{L^2 + (n\pi)^2}, \qquad n \ge 1, $$

$$ b_n = \frac{S_n}{L} = -\frac{2\frac{n\pi}{L}(-1)^n\sinh L}{L\left(1 + \frac{(n\pi)^2}{L^2}\right)} = -\frac{2n\pi(-1)^n\sinh L}{L^2 + (n\pi)^2}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

(iii) $f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & -L \le x < 0, \\ x + 1, & 0 \le x \le L \end{cases}$

Observe that $f(x) = 1 + g(x)$ where $g(x) = 0$ on $[-L, 0)$ and $g(x) = x$ on $[0, L]$. Splitting every integral at $x = 0$ (or using linearity directly) makes the algebra completely transparent.

First we compute $a_0$ by splitting at $x = 0$.

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\left(\int_{-L}^{0} 1\,dx + \int_{0}^{L} (x + 1)\,dx\right) = \frac{1}{2L}\left(\Big[x\Big]_{-L}^{0} + \left[\frac{x^2}{2} + x\right]_{0}^{L}\right) = \frac{1}{2L}\left(L + \frac{L^2}{2} + L\right) = 1 + \frac{L}{4}. $$

For $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$), set $\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ and note that $\int_{-L}^{L} 1\cdot\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx = 0$, leaving only $\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx$. We integrate by parts with $u = x$, $dv = \cos(\omega_n x)\,dx$, giving $du = dx$ and $v = \frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}$.

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x\cos(\omega_n x)\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\left(\left[\frac{x\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\right]_{0}^{L} - \int_{0}^{L} \frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\,dx\right). $$

Evaluating the boundary term ($\sin(n\pi) = \sin(0) = 0$) and integrating $-\frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}$ explicitly (antiderivative $+\frac{\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n^2}$):

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{L}\left(0 + \left[\frac{\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n^2}\right]_{0}^{L}\right) = \frac{\cos(n\pi) - \cos(0)}{L\omega_n^2} = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{L\cdot\frac{(n\pi)^2}{L^2}} = \frac{L\big((-1)^n - 1\big)}{(n\pi)^2}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

Thus $a_n = 0$ for even $n$ and $a_{2k+1} = -\frac{2L}{((2k+1)\pi)^2}$ for odd $n = 2k+1$.

For $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$), note that $\int_{-L}^{L} 1\cdot\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx = 0$, leaving $\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx$. We integrate by parts with $u = x$, $dv = \sin(\omega_n x)\,dx$, giving $du = dx$ and $v = -\frac{\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}$.

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} x\sin(\omega_n x)\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\left(\left[-\frac{x\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\right]_{0}^{L} + \int_{0}^{L} \frac{\cos(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n}\,dx\right). $$

Applying Barrow's rule with $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n$ and $\sin(n\pi) = \sin(0) = 0$:

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{L}\left(-\frac{L\cos(n\pi)}{\omega_n} + \left[\frac{\sin(\omega_n x)}{\omega_n^2}\right]_{0}^{L}\right) = -\frac{(-1)^n}{\omega_n} + 0 = -\frac{L(-1)^n}{n\pi}, \qquad n \ge 1. $$

The series

Assembling the coefficients gives the three Fourier series on $[-L, L]$:

  • (i) $f(x) = x^2$:

$$ x^2 \sim \frac{L^2}{3} + \frac{4L^2}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right). $$

  • (ii) $f(x) = e^x$:

$$ e^x \sim \frac{\sinh L}{L} + 2\sinh L \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{L^2 + (n\pi)^2}\left[L\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) - n\pi\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]. $$

  • (iii) $f(x) = 1$ ($x < 0$), $x + 1$ ($x \ge 0$):

$$ f(x) \sim \left(1 + \frac{L}{4}\right) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{L\big((-1)^n - 1\big)}{(n\pi)^2}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) - \frac{L(-1)^n}{n\pi}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right]. $$

Convergence at the jumps

We now compare the graph of each Fourier series (its pointwise limit $\tilde{f}_{\text{FS}}(x)$) against the graph of the original function $f(x)$ on $[-3L, 3L]$.

(i) $f(x) = x^2$

Because $f(-L) = f(L) = L^2$, the $2L$-periodic extension has no jumps anywhere on $\mathbb{R}$. Hence the Fourier series converges to $x^2$ at every point of $[-L, L]$ (including $x = \pm L$), and outside $[-L, L]$ it forms a continuous periodic chain of parabolic arches of height $L^2$, diverging from the unbounded parabola $y = x^2$.

(ii) $f(x) = e^x$

On the open interval $(-L, L)$, the Fourier series converges to $e^x$. At every odd multiple $x = (2k+1)L$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), including $x = -L$ and $x = L$, the periodic extension jumps between $e^L$ (from the left) and $e^{-L}$ (from the right), so by Fourier's convergence theorem the series converges to the midpoint

$$ \tilde{f}_{\text{FS}}\big((2k+1)L\big) = \frac{e^L + e^{-L}}{2} = \cosh L. $$

Outside $[-L, L]$, the Fourier series repeats this bounded exponential sawtooth periodically, while $e^x$ grows without bound for $x > L$ and decays toward $0$ for $x < -L$.

(iii) $f(x) = 1$ ($x < 0$), $x + 1$ ($x \ge 0$)

On the open interval $(-L, L)$, the function is continuous everywhere—including at $x = 0$, where $\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = 1 = \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)$—so the Fourier series converges to $f(x)$ throughout $(-L, L)$. At every odd multiple $x = (2k+1)L$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), the periodic extension jumps from $L + 1$ (on the left) to $1$ (on the right), so the Fourier series converges to the midpoint

$$ \tilde{f}_{\text{FS}}\big((2k+1)L\big) = \frac{(L + 1) + 1}{2} = 1 + \frac{L}{2}. $$

Outside $[-L, L]$, the Fourier series repeats the flat-then-ramp wave periodically, whereas $f(x)$ remains constant at $1$ for $x < -L$ and increases linearly as $x + 1$ for $x > L$.

$$ \frac{\sinh L}{L} + 2L\sinh L\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{L^2 + (n\pi)^2} = \cosh L, $$

which is the classical partial-fraction expansion of $\coth L = \frac{1}{L} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2L}{L^2 + (n\pi)^2}$ multiplied by $\sinh L$.

Exercise 9

Fourier Series of Even and Odd Periodic Functions

Original statement · probls_ch6_2627.pdf, p. 2
Even Cosine Series • Odd Sine Series • Parity Proof
Statement

What can we say about the Fourier series on $[-L, L]$ of a function $f(x)$ that is $2L$ periodic and even, that is, satisfies $f(-x) = f(x)$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}$? And if $f(x)$ is $2L$ periodic and odd, $f(-x) = -f(x)$?

Classification of the problem

This is a structural symmetry theorem for Fourier series on a symmetric interval $[-L, L]$ (Haberman Sec. 3.3; Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 11). It shows how parity of $f$ eliminates half of the Euler-Fourier coefficients and halves the integration interval for the remaining ones.

Method

Start from the full Fourier series of a $2L$-periodic piecewise smooth function on $[-L, L]$ (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 10):

$$ f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) + b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right], $$

with

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx, \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx. $$

We first prove two elementary integration lemmas on the symmetric interval $[-L, L]$ using the change of variable $u = -x$, and then apply them to the products $f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ and $f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$, noting that $\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ is even and $\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ is odd.

Step-by-step derivation
1. Parity lemmas for integrals on $[-L, L]$

Let $g(x)$ be any integrable function on $[-L, L]$. We split the integral at $x = 0$:

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} g(x)\,dx = \int_{-L}^{0} g(x)\,dx + \int_{0}^{L} g(x)\,dx. $$

In the first integral $\int_{-L}^{0} g(x)\,dx$, we introduce the substitution $u = -x$. Then $x = -u$, the differential is $du = -dx$ (so $dx = -du$), and the limits transform as:

  • when $x = -L$, $u = -(-L) = L$;
  • when $x = 0$, $u = 0$.

Substituting into the first integral and reversing the limits (which absorbs the minus sign from $-du$) gives

$$ \int_{-L}^{0} g(x)\,dx = \int_{L}^{0} g(-u)\,(-du) = \int_{0}^{L} g(-u)\,du = \int_{0}^{L} g(-x)\,dx, $$

where in the last step we renamed the dummy integration variable $u$ back to $x$. Combining with the second integral yields the general identity

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} g(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{L} \big[g(-x) + g(x)\big]\,dx. $$

From this identity, both parity rules follow immediately:

  • If $g$ is odd ($g(-x) = -g(x)$): then $g(-x) + g(x) = 0$, so

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} g(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{L} 0\,dx = 0. $$

  • If $g$ is even ($g(-x) = g(x)$): then $g(-x) + g(x) = 2g(x)$, so

$$ \int_{-L}^{L} g(x)\,dx = 2\int_{0}^{L} g(x)\,dx. $$

2. Case 1: $f(x)$ is $2L$-periodic and even ($f(-x) = f(x)$)

We check the parity of each integrand in the Euler-Fourier formulas:

  1. $f(x)$: even by hypothesis. Applying the even-function lemma to $a_0$ gives

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2L}\cdot 2\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\,dx. $$

  1. $f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: since $\cos(-z) = \cos z$,

$$ f(-x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi(-x)}{L}\right) = f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), $$

so the integrand is even (even $\times$ even $=$ even). Applying the even-function lemma to $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$) gives

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx. $$

  1. $f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: since $\sin(-z) = -\sin z$,

$$ f(-x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi(-x)}{L}\right) = f(x)\left(-\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right) = -f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), $$

so the integrand is odd (even $\times$ odd $=$ odd). Applying the odd-function lemma to $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$) gives

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = 0. $$

Therefore, the Fourier series of an even $2L$-periodic function reduces to a pure Fourier cosine series:

$$ f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad a_0 = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\,dx, \quad a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx. $$

3. Case 2: $f(x)$ is $2L$-periodic and odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$)

We again check the parity of each integrand in the Euler-Fourier formulas:

  1. $f(x)$: odd by hypothesis. Applying the odd-function lemma to $a_0$ gives

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx = 0. $$

  1. $f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: since $\cos(-z) = \cos z$,

$$ f(-x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi(-x)}{L}\right) = \big(-f(x)\big)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) = -f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), $$

so the integrand is odd (odd $\times$ even $=$ odd). Applying the odd-function lemma to $a_n$ ($n \ge 1$) gives

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = 0. $$

  1. $f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$: since $\sin(-z) = -\sin z$,

$$ f(-x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi(-x)}{L}\right) = \big(-f(x)\big)\left(-\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right) = +f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), $$

so the integrand is even (odd $\times$ odd $=$ even). Applying the even-function lemma to $b_n$ ($n \ge 1$) gives

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx. $$

Therefore, the Fourier series of an odd $2L$-periodic function reduces to a pure Fourier sine series:

$$ f(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx. $$

Answers and checks
  1. Cross-check with Exercise 6.5 (Notes09-12-Nov.pdf, p. 11): - Even functions in Problem - Ch6-P5 Nine Fourier Series Expansions: (iv) $f(x) = |x|$ and (v) $f(x) = x^2$ both gave $b_n = 0$ and pure cosine series. - Odd functions in Problem - Ch6-P5 Nine Fourier Series Expansions: (ii) $f(x) = x$ and (viii) the $-\frac{\pi}{2}/\frac{\pi}{2}$ step both gave $a_0 = a_n = 0$ and pure sine series.
  2. Converse check: Conversely, every convergent cosine series $a_0 + \sum a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ is automatically $2L$-periodic and even, and every convergent sine series $\sum b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$ is automatically $2L$-periodic and odd (and vanishes at $x = 0$ and $x = \pm L$, consistent with the jump midpoint $\frac{f(0^+) + f(0^-)}{2} = \frac{f(0^+) - f(0^+)}{2} = 0$ of any odd function).
  3. Half-range implication: This theorem also justifies half-range expansions on $[0, L]$ (Haberman Sec. 3.3): extending any function defined on $[0, L]$ evenly to $[-L, L]$ produces its Fourier cosine series, while extending it oddly produces its Fourier sine series.
Key results
  • If $f$ is $2L$-periodic and even ($f(-x) = f(x)$): all sine coefficients vanish ($b_n = 0$), and the Fourier series is a pure cosine series: $$f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad a_0 = \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\,dx, \quad a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx.$$
  • If $f$ is $2L$-periodic and odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$): all constant and cosine coefficients vanish ($a_0 = 0$, $a_n = 0$), and the Fourier series is a pure sine series: $$f(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(x)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx.$$
