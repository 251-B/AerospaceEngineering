§ 1

Crystalline Systems & The 14 Bravais Lattices

In condensed matter physics and aerospace materials engineering, solid state matter is partitioned into two fundamental thermodynamic arrangements: amorphous solids (lacking long-range spatial periodicity, such as window glasses and certain thermosetting polymers) and crystalline solids (metals, alloys, and structural ceramics), which organize into infinite three-dimensional periodic arrays of atoms or ions [Session 3 Slides 3–5].

To describe any periodic crystal quantitatively, crystallography defines the unit cell—the smallest repeating structural volume whose spatial translation through lattice vectors completely fills space without gaps or overlaps. A unit cell is characterized by six independent geometric constants known as lattice parameters [Slide 7]: the axial edge lengths $a, b, c$ along the primary axes $\vec{x}, \vec{y}, \vec{z}$, and the interaxial angles $\alpha$ (between $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$), $\beta$ (between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{c}$), and $\gamma$ (between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$).

Figure 1.1: Unit cell lattice parameters (a, b, c; α, β, γ) and the 4 Bravais centring types (P, I, C, F) [Slide 7, 9].

In 1848, Auguste Bravais proved mathematically that combining the 7 crystal systems with the 4 cell types generates exactly 14 unique three-dimensional space lattices [Slides 8–13]. Any other arrangement collapses by symmetry into one of these 14 lattices:

Crystal System Axial Relationships Interaxial Angles Bravais Lattices Present Lattices Total
Cubic $a = b = c$ $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90^\circ$ Simple (P), Body-Centered (I), Face-Centered (F) 3
Tetragonal $a = b \neq c$ $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90^\circ$ Simple (P), Body-Centered (I) 2
Orthorhombic $a \neq b \neq c$ $\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90^\circ$ Simple (P), Base-Centered (C), Body-Centered (I), Face-Centered (F) 4
Rhombohedral $a = b = c$ $\alpha = \beta = \gamma \neq 90^\circ < 120^\circ$ Simple (P) 1
Hexagonal $a = b \neq c$ $\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ, \gamma = 120^\circ$ Simple (P) 1
Monoclinic $a \neq b \neq c$ $\alpha = \gamma = 90^\circ \neq \beta$ Simple (P), Base-Centered (C) 2
Triclinic $a \neq b \neq c$ $\alpha \neq \beta \neq \gamma \neq 90^\circ$ Simple (P) 1
§ 2

Fundamental Metallic Structures: BCC, FCC & HCP

Because metallic bonding consists of delocalized valence electrons freely surrounding positively charged ion cores, metallic bonding is fundamentally non-directional. Metallic crystals therefore maximize electrostatic cohesion by packing hard spherical atoms of radius $R$ into the densest possible geometric arrangements [Slide 16].

The volumetric packing density is evaluated by the Atomic Packing Factor (APF): $$\text{APF} = \frac{V_{\text{atoms}}}{V_C} = \frac{n \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)}{V_C}$$ where $n$ is the net number of atoms contained within the unit cell, $R$ is the atomic radius, and $V_C$ is the total unit cell volume [Slide 16].

§ 3

Interstitial Sites: Tetrahedral & Octahedral Voids

Even in the closest packing of identical spheres ($\text{APF} = 0.74$), $26\%$ of the crystal volume remains unoccupied void space. These voids constitute interstitial sites [Session 3 Slide 29]. In solid solutions, light solute atoms ($\text{H, C, N, B}$) inhabit these voids:

  • Tetrahedral Sites: Coordinated by 4 lattice atoms situated at the vertices of a regular tetrahedron. In close-packed structures (FCC and HCP), their multiplicity is strictly $N_{\text{tet}} = 2n$ [Slide 29].
  • Octahedral Sites: Coordinated by 6 lattice atoms situated at the vertices of an octahedron. In close-packed structures, their multiplicity is strictly $N_{\text{oct}} = n$ [Slide 29].
Lattice Type Octahedral Count ($N_{\text{oct}}$) Octahedral Radius ($r_{\text{oct}}$) Tetrahedral Count ($N_{\text{tet}}$) Tetrahedral Radius ($r_{\text{tet}}$) Void Volume Fraction
FCC ($n=4$) $\mathbf{4}$ (1 center + 12 edges $\times 1/4$) $(\sqrt{2}-1)r \approx \mathbf{0.414\, r}$ $\mathbf{8}$ (inside 8 sub-cubes) $(\sqrt{3/2}-1)r \approx \mathbf{0.225\, r}$ $1 - 0.74 = \mathbf{0.26}$
BCC ($n=2$) $\mathbf{6}$ (6 faces $\times 1/2$ + 12 edges $\times 1/4$) $(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}-1)r \approx \mathbf{0.155\, r}$ $\mathbf{12}$ (4 per face $\times 6 \times 1/2$) $(\sqrt{5/3}-1)r \approx \mathbf{0.291\, r}$ $1 - 0.68 = \mathbf{0.32}$
HCP ($n=6$) $\mathbf{6}$ $\approx \mathbf{0.414\, r}$ $\mathbf{12}$ $\approx \mathbf{0.225\, r}$ $1 - 0.74 = \mathbf{0.26}$
Aeronautical Insight: Carbon Solubility in Steels
In ferritic $\alpha\text{-Fe}$ (BCC), the octahedral void is severely flattened ($r_{\text{oct}} = 0.155\, r \approx 0.019\text{ nm}$), while in austenitic $\gamma\text{-Fe}$ (FCC) it is isotropic and spacious ($r_{\text{oct}} = 0.414\, r \approx 0.053\text{ nm}$). Because a carbon atom ($r_C \approx 0.071\text{ nm}$) causes massive tetragonal distortion in BCC, carbon solubility in ferrite is limited to $< 0.022\text{ wt\%}$ at $727^\circ\text{C}$, whereas austenite dissolves up to $2.14\text{ wt\%}$ C. Quenching traps this carbon, generating the ultra-hard martensitic phase used in aerospace landing gear forgings [Session 4 Slide 49].
§ 4

Miller Indices, Crystallographic Directions & Bragg Spacing

Crystallographic features are indexed through Miller notation [Session 3 Slides 36–52]:

Figure 4.1: Miller planes in cubic lattices: (100), (110), and (111) [Session 3 Slides 41–48].

1. Directions $[u\, v\, w]$: Defined by subtracting the tail coordinates from the head coordinates and reducing to the smallest whole integers. Square brackets denote a specific direction $[u\, v\, w]$; angular brackets denote a crystallographic family $\langle u\, v\, w \rangle$ [Slide 37].

2. Planes $(h\, k\, l)$: Computed by taking the reciprocals of axial intercepts $(1/p, 1/q, 1/r)$ cleared of fractions. Round parentheses designate a specific plane $(h\, k\, l)$; curly braces denote a family $\{h\, k\, l\}$. In cubic systems exclusively: $$[h\, k\, l] \perp (h\, k\, l) \quad (\text{cubic only})$$

3. Hexagonal Miller-Bravais 4-Index Notation: Basal planes use $(h\, k\, i\, l)$ with the closed constraint $i = -(h+k)$ and directions $[u\, v\, t\, w]$ with $t = -(u+v)$ [Slides 50–52].

4. Interplanar Spacing ($d_{hkl}$) and Bragg's Law: The perpendicular separation between adjacent parallel $(hkl)$ planes in orthogonal lattices is governed by [Slide 53]: $$\frac{1}{d_{hkl}^2} = \frac{h^2}{a^2} + \frac{k^2}{b^2} + \frac{l^2}{c^2} \implies d_{hkl}^{\text{cubic}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}}$$ Under monochromatic X-ray diffraction, constructive interference obeys Bragg's law: $$\lambda = 2\, d_{hkl}\sin\theta$$

§ 5

Crystalline Density Calculations: Volumetric, Linear & Planar

Mechanical strength, dislocation mobility, and chemical corrosion rates are intrinsically anisotropic and dictated by packing density across three spatial scales [Session 4 Slides 7–9]:

1. Volumetric Density ($\rho_v$): Theoretical mass per unit cell volume [Slide 7]: $$\rho_v = \frac{n \cdot M}{V_C \cdot N_A} \quad [\text{g/cm}^3]$$ Benchmark Cu (FCC): $n=4, M=63.55\text{ g/mol}, a=3.615\text{ \AA} \implies \rho_v = 8.89\text{ g/cm}^3$.

2. Linear Density ($\rho_l$): Number of diametrically intersected atoms per unit vector length: $$\rho_l = \frac{N_{\text{intersected atoms}}}{L_{[u\, v\, w]}} \quad [\text{atoms/cm}]$$ Along the close-packed directions ($\langle 110 \rangle$ in FCC, $\langle 111 \rangle$ in BCC), atoms are in direct contact: $\rho_l = \frac{1}{2R}$.

3. Planar Density ($\rho_p$): Number of atoms centered in a crystallographic plane per unit cross-sectional plane area [Slide 8]: $$\rho_p = \frac{N_{\text{atoms centered in plane}}}{A_{(h\, k\, l)}} \quad [\text{atoms/cm}^2]$$ In any orthogonal lattice, volumetric, planar, and interplanar metrics satisfy the exact physical identity: $$\rho_v = \frac{\rho_p(hkl)}{d_{hkl}}$$

§ 6

Crystalline Defects: 0D Point, 1D Dislocations & 2D Planar

Real crystals deviate from mathematical perfection through thermodynamic, thermal, or mechanical perturbations. Defects are classified by spatial dimensionality [Session 4 Slide 14]:

Figure 6.1: Edge dislocation Burgers circuit and Hall-Petch grain boundary strengthening [Session 4 Slides 28, 41].

1. 0D Point Defects: Vacancies are thermodynamically unavoidable at any $T > 0\text{ K}$ due to configurational entropy ($S = k_B \ln W$). In metals [Slide 15]: $$\frac{n_v}{N} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta H_v}{RT}\right), \quad N = \frac{\rho N_A}{M}$$ In ionic ceramics, charge neutrality mandates paired defects [Slide 16]: Schottky defects ($n_s = N\exp(-\Delta H_s / 2RT)$) and Frenkel defects ($n_F = \sqrt{N N_i}\exp(-\Delta H_F / 2RT)$).

2. 1D Linear Defects (Dislocations): Dislocations resolve the theoretical vs experimental shear strength paradox ($\tau_{\text{theor}} \sim G/10 \gg \tau_{\text{exp}} \sim 10^{-3}G$) [Slide 25].

  • Edge (Taylor): $\vec{b} \perp \vec{t}$ (extra half-plane of atoms).
  • Screw (Burgers): $\vec{b} \parallel \vec{t}$ (spiral ramp distortion).
  • Dislocation Energy: $E \propto |\vec{b}|^2$. Nature minimizes energy by forcing slip strictly along closest packed directions [Slide 35].
  • Slip Systems & Ductility: FCC possesses $12$ systems ($\{111\}\langle 110 \rangle$), providing superior plastic workability across all temperatures; BCC has $12$ systems ($\{110\}\langle 111 \rangle$) prone to ductile-to-brittle transition; HCP possesses only $3$ basal systems ($(0001)\langle 11\bar{2}0 \rangle$), causing low room-temperature ductility [Slide 36].

3. 2D Planar Defects: Grain boundaries (width 2–5 atomic spacings) act as impenetrable barriers to dislocation pile-up, governed by the Hall-Petch relationship [Slide 41]: $$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y \cdot d^{-1/2}$$ Grain refinement is the singular strengthening mechanism in metallurgy that increases yield strength ($\sigma_y$) without sacrificing fracture toughness.

§ 7

Solid Solutions, Non-Stoichiometry & Polymorphism

1. Hume-Rothery Rules for Substitutional Solid Solutions: Appreciable mutual solubility requires satisfying four empirical criteria [Session 4 Slide 22]:

  1. $\Delta R = \left|\frac{R_{\text{solute}} - R_{\text{solvent}}}{R_{\text{solvent}}}\right| \times 100 < 15\%$ ($< 30\%$ for ceramics).
  2. Identical crystal structure (e.g., both FCC, or both BCC).
  3. Similar electronegativities ($\Delta \chi \approx 0$) to avoid intermetallic compounds.
  4. Matching chemical valency.

2. Non-Stoichiometry & Charge Compensation in Ceramics: In wüstite ($\text{Fe}_{1-\delta}\text{O}$), oxidation of $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ produces cationic vacancies to maintain electroneutrality [Slide 21]: $$3\,\text{Mg}^{2+} \longleftrightarrow 2\,\text{Al}^{3+} + 1\,V_{\text{Mg}}''$$

3. Polymorphism and Allotropy: The ability of a material to exist in multiple crystallographic forms [Slide 48]:

  • Carbon: $sp^3$ covalent diamond (ultra-hard electrical insulator) vs $sp^2$ planar graphite (soft solid lubricant and electrical conductor).
  • Zirconia ($\text{ZrO}_2$): Monoclinic $\to$ Tetragonal $\to$ Cubic. Transformation toughening in yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) provides extreme crack-arresting capability in aero-engine Thermal Barrier Coatings (TBC) [Slide 50].
  • Iron Allotropy: $\alpha\text{-Fe (BCC)} \xrightarrow{912^\circ\text{C}} \gamma\text{-Fe (FCC)} \xrightarrow{1394^\circ\text{C}} \delta\text{-Fe (BCC)}$ drives steel hardening and precipitation heat treatments across aerospace metallurgy [Slide 49].
