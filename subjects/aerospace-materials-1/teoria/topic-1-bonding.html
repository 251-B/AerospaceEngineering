Bonding in Solids & Macroscopic Material Properties

A rigorous treatise on interatomic potential wells, Born-Landé lattice energy, primary and secondary bonding regimes, and their direct manifestation in mechanical stiffness, thermal expansion, hardness, and ductility.

📖 Official Ground Truth: Universidad Carlos III de Madrid · Session 2 T1 Bonding_2025.pdf (Slides 1–39)

§ 1 The Materials Science & Engineering Tetrahedron [Slide 2]

In modern aerospace engineering, materials science operates on a fundamental governing axiom: the physical and mechanical performance of an aerospace component is dictated by its properties, which in turn are determined by its internal atomic bonding and microstructure, both created and controlled through thermomechanical processing [Slide 2].

Figure 1.1: The Materials Science Tetrahedron. Interdependence of structure, processing, properties, and applications [Slide 2].

The central paradigm establishes that no material property can be analyzed in isolation. Whether designing single-crystal nickel superalloy turbine blades or carbon-fiber composite wing skins, the ultimate limit of performance is governed by interatomic binding forces and the resulting potential energy well.

§ 2 Interatomic Forces, Potential Energy Well & Macroscopic Properties [Slides 7–13, 20–21]

When two isolated atoms or ions approach one another from an infinite separation, the net interaction force $F_{\text{net}}(r)$ represents a competition between long-range electrostatic or orbital attraction and short-range quantum electron-cloud repulsion [Slides 7–8]:

$$F_{\text{net}}(r) = F_{\text{attractive}}(r) + F_{\text{repulsive}}(r) = -\frac{A}{r^m} - \frac{n B}{r^{n+1}} \quad (n > m)$$ $$E_{\text{net}}(r) = -\int_\infty^r F_{\text{net}}(r')\,dr' = -\frac{A}{r^m} + \frac{B}{r^n}$$

At the equilibrium interatomic spacing $r = r_0$, the attractive and repulsive forces reach exact mechanical equilibrium, causing the net force to vanish and the potential energy to attain its global minimum:

$$\left.F_{\text{net}}\right|_{r = r_0} = -\left.\frac{dE_{\text{net}}}{dr}\right|_{r = r_0} = 0 \iff E_{\text{net}}(r_0) = -E_0$$
Figure 2.1: Interatomic potential energy well $E(r)$, showing bond energy $E_0$, equilibrium spacing $r_0$, elastic curvature, and anharmonic thermal expansion shift [Slides 8, 10–13].

Mathematical Mapping to Macroscopic Properties

  1. Melting Temperature ($T_m$) [Slide 10]: Governed directly by well depth $E_0$. Melting is a thermal phase transformation wherein lattice thermal agitation $k_B T_m$ overcomes the cohesive energy: $$k_B T_m \propto E_0 \implies \text{Deeper Well } (E_0 \uparrow) \implies T_m \uparrow$$
  2. Young's Modulus of Elasticity ($E$) [Slide 13]: Governed by the local curvature (second derivative) at the bottom of the well: $$E_{\text{Young}} = \frac{1}{r_0}\left.\frac{d^2 E_{\text{net}}}{dr^2}\right|_{r = r_0} = \frac{1}{r_0}\left.\frac{dF_{\text{net}}}{dr}\right|_{r = r_0}$$ A steep, narrow potential well requires immense mechanical force per unit displacement to stretch bonds, yielding a high elastic modulus.
  3. Coefficient of Thermal Expansion ($\alpha$) [Slide 12]: Governed by the anharmonic asymmetry of the potential well. If the well were perfectly parabolic/symmetric ($E(r) = \frac{1}{2}k(r-r_0)^2$), the mean vibrational position $\bar{r}(T)$ would remain identical to $r_0$, resulting in zero thermal expansion ($\alpha = 0$). In real materials, the attractive wall ($r > r_0$) is flatter than the steep repulsive wall ($r < r_0$), causing the average atomic spacing $\bar{r}(T)$ to shift outwards as temperature rises: $$\alpha = \frac{1}{r_0}\frac{d\bar{r}}{dT} \quad \implies \text{Deeper, steeper wells are more symmetric } \implies \alpha \downarrow$$
  4. Mohs Hardness [Slide 11]: Resistance to localized plastic scratching requires displacing atomic planes against the cohesive potential well. Materials with high binding energy and narrow potential wells exhibit elevated Mohs hardness.

§ 3 Primary Bonding I — Ionic Bonding & Born-Landé Lattice Energy [Slides 4–15]

Ionic bonding occurs between strongly electropositive metals and strongly electronegative non-metals ($\Delta\chi > 2.0$). Valence electrons are completely transferred from the metal atom (forming a contracted cation, $r_{c+} < r_{\text{atom}}$) to the non-metal (forming an expanded anion, $r_{a-} > r_{\text{atom}}$) [Slides 4–6].

Because electrostatic fields are spherically symmetric, ionic bonds are non-directional. Geometric crystal arrangements are dictated by maintaining charge neutrality and maximizing electrostatic coordination without mutual anion-anion overlap (radius ratio rule $r_+/r_-$) [Slide 15].

The Born-Landé Equation for Lattice Energy ($U$) [Slide 9]

In an infinite three-dimensional crystal lattice, summing all infinite pairwise Coulomb attractions and repulsions introduces the dimensionless geometric Madelung constant ($A$):

$$U = -\frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2 N_A A}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a_0}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)$$

Where:

  • $Z_1, Z_2$: Formal valence charges (e.g., $+1$ and $-1$ for $\text{KCl}$; $+2$ and $-2$ for $\text{MgO}$).
  • $e$: Electron charge ($1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$).
  • $\varepsilon_0$: Vacuum permittivity ($8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2)$).
  • $a_0$: Equilibrium interionic separation ($a_0 = r_{c+} + r_{a-}$).
  • $A$: Madelung constant ($A_{\text{NaCl}} = 1.7476$, $A_{\text{CsCl}} = 1.7627$).
  • $n$: Born repulsion exponent ($n \in [5, 9]$) [Slide 7].

The Born-Haber Cycle for $\text{KCl}$ [Slide 9]

Because gaseous ions cannot be synthesized directly in a laboratory calorimeter, the lattice energy $U$ is deduced using Hess's Law through a closed thermodynamic cycle. Slide 9 provides the official thermochemical data:

Empirical Correlations with Lattice Energy [Slides 10–14]

1. Melting Temperature ($T_m$) [Slide 10]:

  • Valence effect: Increasing valence $Z$ massively scales $U \propto Z_1 Z_2$. For $\text{NaCl}$ ($Z=1$), $T_m = 801^\circ\text{C}$; for $\text{MgO}$ ($Z=2$), $T_m = 2852^\circ\text{C}$.
  • Ionic size effect: In an isovalent series, increasing ion radius $a_0$ reduces $U \propto 1/a_0$, causing $T_m$ to decline monotonically ($\text{LiF} > \text{NaCl} > \text{KBr} > \text{CsI}$).

2. Mohs Hardness vs Interionic Distance ($d$) [Slide 11]:

Compound $\text{BeO}$ $\text{MgO}$ $\text{CaO}$ $\text{SrO}$ $\text{BaO}$
Distance $d$ ($\text{\AA}$) $1.65$ $2.10$ $2.40$ $2.57$ $2.77$
Mohs Hardness $9.0$ $6.5$ $4.5$ $3.5$ $3.3$

Benchmarks: Hydroxyapatite $= 5.0$, Keratin fingernail $= 2.5$ [Slide 11]. Cation radius $r_{c+} \downarrow \implies U \uparrow \implies \text{Hardness} \uparrow$.

3. Volumetric Thermal Expansion ($\alpha_V$) [Slide 12]:

$\text{NaF } (39 \times 10^{-6}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1}) < \text{NaCl } (40) < \text{NaBr } (43) < \text{NaI } (48 \times 10^{-6}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1})$. Larger anion radius weakens lattice energy, flattening the well and driving up thermal expansion.

4. Brittleness & Electrical Conductivity [Slide 14]:

Ionic solids are brittle because shear slip brings like-charged ions into juxtaposition, causing catastrophic Coulombic repulsion. In the solid state, ions are locked in place and valence electrons are deeply localized in wide band gaps, making them electrical insulators. When melted or dissolved, ions become mobile, yielding high ionic conductivity.

§ 4 Primary Bonding II — Covalent Bonding & Polar Character [Slides 16–21, 38]

Covalent bonding occurs predominantly between non-metallic elements with small differences in electronegativity ($\Delta\chi < 1.7$). Atoms share outer $s$ and $p$ valence electrons in localized molecular orbitals to attain closed-shell noble gas configurations ($s^2 p^6$) [Slide 17].

Directionality and Tetrahedral Architecture [Slide 18]

Because bonding orbitals represent hybridized quantum states ($sp^3$), covalent bonds possess strict spatial directionality:

  • Elemental Silicon ($\text{Si}$): Valence of 4 requires 4 covalent bonds. $sp^3$ hybridization forms a regular tetrahedron with equilibrium inter-bond angles of exactly $109.5^\circ$.
  • Silica ($\text{SiO}_2$): Corner-sharing $\text{SiO}_4^{4-}$ tetrahedra linked by directional $\text{Si}-\text{O}$ bonds form the structural backbone of quartz, glass, and ceramic matrices.

Continuous Network Covalent Solids [Slides 19, 38]

Substances composed of a continuous three-dimensional network of covalent bonds (e.g., diamond, cubic boron nitride, quartz $\text{SiO}_2$, silicon carbide $\text{SiC}$) display extraordinary mechanical and thermal extremes:

  • Extreme hardness and incompressibility (Mohs 9–10).
  • Extremely high melting / sublimation points ($T_m > 2000\text{--}3800^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Complete insolubility in all polar and non-polar solvents.
  • Electrical insulators in both solid and molten states due to wide localized band gaps ($E_g > 3\text{--}6\text{ eV}$).

Polarity and the Hannay-Smyth Relation [Slide 20]

When bonded atoms possess unequal electronegativities, electron sharing becomes unequal, generating a partial charge separation $\delta^+ - \delta^-$ and an electric dipole moment:

$$\mu = Q \times d \quad (\text{Units: Debye, D}; \quad 1\text{ D} = 3.33 \times 10^{-30}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m})$$

The continuous transition between pure covalent and ionic bonding is quantified by the Hannay-Smyth equation [Slide 20]:

$$\% \text{ Ionic Character} = \left[1 - \exp\left(-0.25\,(X_A - X_B)^2\right)\right] \times 100\%$$

Bond Order, Length, and Energy [Slide 21]

For a given atomic pair, multiplying shared electron pairs increases cohesive bond order, which tightens interatomic distance and elevates bond dissociation energy:

$$\text{Bond Order } \uparrow \implies \text{Bond Length } (d) \downarrow \implies \text{Bond Energy } (E_b) \uparrow$$ $$\text{C}-\text{C } (1.54\text{ \AA}, 348\text{ kJ/mol}) \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{C}=\text{C } (1.34\text{ \AA}, 614\text{ kJ/mol}) \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{C}\equiv\text{C } (1.20\text{ \AA}, 839\text{ kJ/mol})$$

§ 5 Primary Bonding III — Metallic Bonding & Energy Band Theory [Slides 22–27]

In metallic crystals, electropositive atoms shed their outer valence electrons into a crystal-spanning, delocalized "electron sea". Cohesive bonding is the isotropic electrostatic attraction between the positively charged ion cores (cations) and the pervasive, negatively charged conduction electron gas [Slide 23].

Valence Dependence of Metallic Cohesive Energy [Slide 24]

The cohesive strength of a metallic crystal scales directly with the number of valence electrons contributed per atom to the electron sea:

Metal Valence Electron Configuration Bonding Energy ($E_b$) [Slide 24] Melting Temp ($T_m$) [Slide 24]
Potassium ($\text{K}$) $+1$ $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1$ $89.6\text{ kJ/mol}$ $63.5^\circ\text{C}$
Calcium ($\text{Ca}$) $+2$ $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2$ $177.0\text{ kJ/mol}$ $851.0^\circ\text{C}$
Titanium ($\text{Ti}$) $+4$ ($3d^2 4s^2$) $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^2 4s^2$ $\approx 470\text{ kJ/mol}$ $1668.0^\circ\text{C}$

Ductility vs Dislocation Slip Mechanism [Slide 25]

Because the metallic electron sea is homogeneous, fluid, and completely non-directional, planes of metal cations slide past one another under shear stress without encountering like-charge repulsion. The electron sea instantly screens the moving cations, rendering pure metals exceptionally ductile and malleable.

Figure 5.1: Comparative dislocation slip. Metallic screening enables ductile slip, whereas ionic plane shift induces Coulombic cleavage [Slides 14, 25].

Band Theory in Metals [Slides 26–27]

When $N$ atoms assemble into a crystalline solid, discrete molecular orbitals split into continuous energy bands containing $N$ quantum states [Slide 26]:

  • Monovalent Metals ($\text{Li}, 1s^2 2s^1$): The $2s$ valence band is half-filled. Electrons absorb infinitesimal energy to move into adjacent vacant states, granting high electrical conductivity.
  • Divalent Metals ($\text{Mg}, 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2$): Although the $3s$ subshell is fully occupied, at equilibrium spacing $a_0$, the empty $3p$ conduction band physically and energetically overlaps with the $3s$ valence band [Slide 27], eliminating the band gap ($E_g = 0$) and enabling uninhibited metallic conduction.

§ 6 Secondary Bonding & Intermolecular Forces [Slides 28–35, 37–38]

Secondary bonds (van der Waals interactions) are weak physical attractions ($< 10\text{ kcal/mol} \approx 42\text{ kJ/mol}$) arising from dipole-dipole electrostatic interactions, contrasting sharply with primary bonds ($125\text{--}370\text{ kcal/mol}$) [Slide 29]:

Bond Category Energy Scale Mechanism Benchmark Examples
Primary: Ionic $150\text{--}370\text{ kcal/mol}$ Valence $e^-$ transfer $\to$ Coulomb attraction $\text{NaCl}, \text{MgO}, \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$
Primary: Covalent $125\text{--}300\text{ kcal/mol}$ Shared localized $e^-$ pairs $\text{Diamond}, \text{Si}, \text{SiO}_2$
Primary: Metallic $25\text{--}200\text{ kcal/mol}$ Delocalized electron sea $\text{Al}, \text{Ti}, \text{Fe}, \text{Ni}$
Secondary: Permanent Dipoles $1\text{--}10\text{ kcal/mol}$ Constant dipole moment in asymmetrical molecules $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$, PVC inter-chain stiffness
Secondary: Hydrogen Bond $\approx 7\text{ kcal/mol}$ $\text{H}$ bonded to compact $\text{F}, \text{O}, \text{N}$ $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ boiling point anomaly
Secondary: Fluctuating Dipoles $< 2\text{ kcal/mol}$ Instantaneous quantum charge fluctuations (London) Solid noble gases, Polyethylene

Polymer Chain Stiffening: Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) [Slide 32]

In polyvinyl chloride ($-[\text{CH}_2-\text{CHCl}]_n-$), the strongly electronegative chlorine atoms ($\text{Cl}^{\delta-}$) attract the electropositive hydrogens ($\text{H}^{\delta+}$) on adjacent polymer chains. These secondary van der Waals cross-links restrict inter-chain sliding, significantly increasing the mechanical stiffness (modulus) of PVC. When an excessive external stress is applied, these weak secondary bonds break, permitting chains to slide past one another in plastic deformation [Slide 32].

Hydrogen Bonding & Boiling Point Anomalies [Slides 33, 35]

In molecular hydrides of Group 14 ($\text{CH}_4 \to \text{SiH}_4 \to \text{GeH}_4 \to \text{SnH}_4$), boiling points increase smoothly with period number due to increased London dispersion forces. However, for Groups 15, 16, and 17, the lightest hydrides ($\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{HF}, \text{NH}_3$) exhibit massive positive boiling point spikes due to extensive hydrogen bonding ($\approx 7\text{ kcal/mol}$), keeping water liquid up to $100^\circ\text{C}$ compared to $-60^\circ\text{C}$ for $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ [Slide 35].

§ 7 Official Exam Questions (UC3M Slide 39)

The following four problems represent the official exam questions formulated by the Department of Materials Science & Engineering at UC3M [Slide 39], resolved exhaustively following the 4-phase pedagogical methodology.

Question 1: Metallic vs Covalent Primary Bonds UC3M SLIDE 39 · Q1

"Compare and contrast metallic and covalent primary bonds in terms of: a) the nature of the bond, b) the valence of the atoms involved, and c) the ductility of the materials bonded in these ways."

Fase 1: Enunciado y Clasificación de Enlaces

Both bond types are primary chemical bonds with cohesive energies exceeding $100\text{ kJ/mol}$. We evaluate them across electronic wavefunctions, valence occupancy, and crystal slip dynamics.

Fase 2: Formulación y Mecanismos Físicos

Covalent bonding is defined by localized shared electron pairs occupying directional hybridized orbitals ($sp^3, 109.5^\circ$) governed by the $(8-N)$ rule [Slides 17–18]. Metallic bonding is defined by delocalized electron gas screening an array of positive cations in an isotropic, non-directional potential [Slide 23].

Fase 3: Desarrollo Comparativo Paso a Paso
  • a) Nature of the Bond: Metallic: Non-directional electrostatic attraction between positive ion cores and a pervasive delocalized electron sea. Covalent: Highly directional sharing of electron pairs in localized orbitals concentrated along specific bond vectors.
  • b) Valence of the Atoms: Metallic: Formed by electropositive elements with few valence electrons (1, 2, or 3, plus incomplete inner $d$-shells). The small number of valence electrons relative to high coordination numbers ($CN=8\text{ or }12$) compels electron delocalization. Covalent: Formed by electronegative non-metals/metalloids with higher valences (4, 5, 6, 7) that satisfy the $(8-N)$ octet rule.
  • c) Ductility of Materials: Metallic: Highly ductile. Under shear, dislocation slip translates cation planes smoothly through the fluid, non-directional electron sea without bond breakage. Covalent: Extremely brittle. Plastic shear requires the catastrophic angular distortion and rupture of rigid, directional covalent bonds, resulting in cleavage fracture prior to plastic flow.
Fase 4: Interpretación Física & Verificación

This fundamental electronic contrast explains why structural airframes use metallic alloys (e.g., Al, Ti) for fracture toughness, whereas covalent materials (e.g., SiC, diamond) are reserved for extreme hardness and wear-resistant coatings.

Question 2: Potential Well Curves for Titanium vs Aluminum UC3M SLIDE 39 · Q2

"Titanium is stiffer than aluminum, has a lower thermal expansion coefficient than aluminum, and has a higher melting temperature than aluminum. On the same graph, carefully and schematically draw the potential well curves for both metals and showing how the physical properties are manifested in these curves."

Fase 1: Datos Físicos Oficiales

$E_{\text{Ti}} \approx 115\text{ GPa} > E_{\text{Al}} \approx 69\text{ GPa}$; $T_{m,\text{Ti}} = 1668^\circ\text{C} \gg T_{m,\text{Al}} = 660^\circ\text{C}$; $\alpha_{\text{Ti}} \approx 8.6 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1} \ll \alpha_{\text{Al}} \approx 23.1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ K}^{-1}$; $r_{0,\text{Ti}} \approx r_{0,\text{Al}} \approx 0.286\text{--}0.289\text{ nm}$.

Fase 2: Mapeo Matemático Riguroso

1. $T_m \propto E_0$ (Trough Depth): Titanium's minimum is deeper ($E_{0,\text{Ti}} \gg E_{0,\text{Al}}$).
2. $E \propto d^2E/dr^2|_{r_0}$ (Curvature): Titanium's well is narrower and sharper at the bottom.
3. $\alpha \propto$ Asymmetry: Aluminum's well is broader, shallower, and strongly anharmonic on the attractive branch.

Fase 3: Gráfico Comparativo de Pozos de Potencial
Figure 7.1: Superimposed potential well curves for Titanium vs Aluminum [Slide 39, Q2].
Fase 4: Interpretación Físico-Química

Titanium's superior stiffness and refractoriness originate from its $3d^2 4s^2$ transition configuration, where localized $d$-band hybridization augments the metallic electron sea. Aluminum utilizes only simple $3s^2 3p^1$ valence electrons, forming a more compliant, shallower well.

Question 3: Modulus of Elasticity of MgO vs Magnesium UC3M SLIDE 39 · Q3

"Would you expect MgO or magnesium to have the higher modulus of elasticity? Explain."

Fase 1: Planteamiento y Datos Experimentales

Magnesium oxide ($\text{MgO}$): Refractory ceramic with $E_{\text{MgO}} \approx 250\text{--}300\text{ GPa}$, $T_m = 2852^\circ\text{C}$.
Pure Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$): Lightweight metal with $E_{\text{Mg}} \approx 45\text{ GPa}$, $T_m = 650^\circ\text{C}$.

Fase 2: Formulación Teórica

The elastic modulus scales with interionic potential curvature: $$E \propto \left.\frac{d^2 E}{dr^2}\right|_{r = r_0} \propto \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{r_0^4}$$

Fase 3: Deducción Analítica

In $\text{MgO}$, bonding is dominated by divalent ionic point charges: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($Z_1 = +2$) and $\text{O}^{2-}$ ($Z_2 = -2$), giving a massive charge product $Z_1 Z_2 = 4$ across a very compact interionic spacing ($a_0 = 2.10\text{ \AA}$ [Slide 11]). This produces an enormous lattice energy ($|U| \approx 3790\text{ kJ/mol}$) and a steep potential well with an extremely high second derivative.
In metallic $\text{Mg}$, the divalent $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ cations are immersed in a smeared-out, delocalized electron gas ($r_0 \approx 3.20\text{ \AA}$). The diffuse electron gas provides far lower resistance to elastic bond stretching ($E_b \approx 146\text{ kJ/mol}$).

Fase 4: Conclusión Definitiva

$\text{MgO}$ has a far higher modulus of elasticity than pure magnesium ($E_{\text{MgO}} \approx 250\text{--}300\text{ GPa}$ vs $E_{\text{Mg}} \approx 45\text{ GPa}$, roughly a factor of $6\times$ higher), directly attributable to the intense Coulombic curvature of localized divalent ionic bonds.

Question 4: Modulus of Elasticity of Aluminum vs Silicon UC3M SLIDE 39 · Q4

"Aluminum and silicon are side-by-side in the periodic table. Which would you expect to have the higher modulus of elasticity (E)? Explain."

Fase 1: Planteamiento y Posición Periódica

Aluminum ($Z=13$, Group 13, $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^1$): FCC metal, $E_{\text{Al}} \approx 69\text{ GPa}$, $T_m = 660^\circ\text{C}$.
Silicon ($Z=14$, Group 14, $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^2$): Diamond-cubic covalent network, $E_{\text{Si}} \approx 130\text{--}185\text{ GPa}$, $T_m = 1414^\circ\text{C}$.

Fase 2: Formulación de Rigidez Radial y Angular

In metallic Aluminum, elastic deformation entails only radial stretching against a non-directional electron sea. In covalent Silicon, four localized $sp^3$ hybrid bonds form a rigid tetrahedral network ($109.5^\circ$ [Slide 18]). Elastic deformation requires both stretching covalent bonds and bending rigid bond angles: $$S_{\text{eff}} = \left.\frac{d^2 E}{dr^2}\right|_{\text{stretch}} + \frac{k_\theta}{r_0^2}\left.\frac{d^2 E}{d\theta^2}\right|_{\text{bend}}$$

Fase 3: Deducción Paso a Paso

Silicon's directional $sp^3$ covalent bonds exhibit strong orbital overlap resistance ($E_{\text{bond}} \approx 325\text{ kJ/mol}$, $T_m = 1414^\circ\text{C}$). The quantum mechanical resistance to bond bending ($k_\theta \gg 0$) adds massive mechanical stiffness. In Aluminum, the non-directional metallic sea provides zero angular stiffness ($k_\theta = 0$), yielding a more compliant lattice.

Fase 4: Conclusión Definitiva

Silicon has a significantly higher modulus of elasticity than Aluminum ($E_{\text{Si}} \approx 150\text{ GPa}$ vs $E_{\text{Al}} \approx 69\text{ GPa}$, more than double the stiffness). While Aluminum's ductility makes it the benchmark airframe structural metal, Silicon's high stiffness and dimensional stability make it the premier choice for aerospace MEMS gyroscopes and accelerometers.
