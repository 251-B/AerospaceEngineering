P01 — Two eutectics around a congruently melting compound in a hypothetical A-B system

The figure shows the equilibrium phase diagram of a hypothetical binary system A-B (composition in wt% B). Every invariant temperature and the compositions of the phases on each invariant line are printed on the diagram.

Figure 1. Hypothetical A-B phase diagram (wt% B): α and β terminal solid solutions, compound γ of 60 wt% B melting at 1150 °C, eutectics at 700 °C (α 10, L 35, γ 60) and 850 °C (γ 60, L 78, β 92); A melts at 900 °C and B at 1050 °C.

a) Determine all the invariant reactions: type of reaction, temperature and composition of every phase involved.

b) For an alloy A-50 wt% B at 700 − ΔT °C, give the phases present with their compositions and amounts, and the microconstituents present with their amounts.

c) Sketch the microstructure of an alloy A-20 wt% B at 700 + ΔT °C and at 700 − ΔT °C, labelling every microconstituent and giving its amount.

d) Indicate whether the intermetallic compound γ melts congruently or incongruently, and give the maximum solubility of B in A and of A in B, with the temperature at which each is observed.

Unit conversion: a composition in wt% is a mass fraction times 100, so 50 wt% B means 0.50 kg of B per kg of alloy. The lever rule uses the ratio of two differences of compositions, so the fractions it gives are dimensionless mass fractions whether the compositions are written in wt% or as fractions. No formula of this problem needs kelvin, so the temperatures stay in °C.

Model

  • Invariant reactions [Session 8 Slide 6]: three phases coexist on a horizontal line at a fixed temperature. On cooling, eutectic $L \rightarrow \alpha + \beta$ (a liquid field ends in a V on the line), peritectic $\alpha + L \rightarrow \beta$ (a solid field ends in an inverted V below the line), eutectoid $\gamma \rightarrow \alpha + \beta$ and peritectoid $\alpha + \beta \rightarrow \gamma$ in the solid state.
  • Tie line and lever rule [Session 7 Slides 15-16]: in a two-phase field, the horizontal line through the alloy point gives the compositions of the two phases at its ends; each phase takes the arm on the side of the other phase, divided by the length of the tie line.
  • Microconstituents [Session 7 Slides 30-32]: the primary (proeutectic) constituent is counted on the tie line just above the eutectic temperature, where the remaining liquid has the eutectic composition and becomes the eutectic; the total phases are counted on the tie line just below it.
  • Intermetallic compounds and melting [Session 8 Slides 14-15, 17]: a stoichiometric compound is a vertical line; it melts congruently when it sits at a maximum of the liquidus (the liquid keeps its composition) and incongruently when it ends at a peritectic line.
  • Hypotheses: equilibrium cooling, pressure fixed at 1 atm, values read on the figure as printed.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Determine all the invariant reactions, with their type, temperature and the composition of every phase

Find the three-phase lines on the diagram

The diagram has two horizontal lines, at 700 °C (from 10 to 60 wt% B) and at 850 °C (from 60 to 92 wt% B). By the Gibbs phase rule at constant pressure, $F = C - P + 1$ [Session 7 Slide 13], three phases in a two-component system leave $F = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$, so a three-phase equilibrium can only appear as a horizontal line at one temperature with three fixed compositions. Those are the invariant reactions [Session 8 Slide 6].

Classify the line at 700 °C

Above the line, between 10 and 60 wt% B, there are two fields, α + L and γ + L, whose liquidus branches fall from 900 °C (pure A) and from 1150 °C (γ) and meet in a V at 35 wt% B on the line. Below the line there is the single two-solid field α + γ. A liquid that ends in a V on the line and gives two solids below it is a eutectic [Session 8 Slide 6]. Reading the three points on the figure, on cooling:

$$L\,(35\ \text{wt\% B}) \;\xrightarrow{\;700\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \alpha\,(10\ \text{wt\% B}) + \gamma\,(60\ \text{wt\% B}) \qquad \text{eutectic}$$

Classify the line at 850 °C

Between 60 and 92 wt% B the liquidus falls from the maximum of γ (1150 °C) and from the melting point of B (1050 °C) to a V at 78 wt% B on the line; below the line lies γ + β. Again a liquid gives two solids, so this is a second eutectic:

$$L\,(78\ \text{wt\% B}) \;\xrightarrow{\;850\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \gamma\,(60\ \text{wt\% B}) + \beta\,(92\ \text{wt\% B}) \qquad \text{eutectic}$$

Check that no other point is invariant

The melting points of A (900 °C) and B (1050 °C) and the maximum of γ (1150 °C) are points where one solid melts to a liquid of its own composition; they behave as one-component transformations ($F = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$ with the composition fixed) and involve two phases, not three, so they are not invariant reactions of the binary system. Each eutectic temperature lies below the melting points of its two neighbouring solids (700 < 900 and 700 < 1150; 850 < 1150 and 850 < 1050), as a eutectic must.

Key results
  • 700 °C, eutectic: L (35 wt% B) → α (10 wt% B) + γ (60 wt% B).
  • 850 °C, eutectic: L (78 wt% B) → γ (60 wt% B) + β (92 wt% B).
  • The maximum of γ at 1150 °C is a congruent melting point, not a three-phase reaction.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Phases and microconstituents of A-50 wt% B at 700 − ΔT °C

Locate the alloy and derive the lever rule

The alloy, 50 wt% B, lies between the eutectic composition (35) and γ (60), so on cooling it first crosses the γ liquidus and forms primary γ. Just below 700 °C it lies in the α + γ field. On a tie line with phases α and γ the two mass balances are, for the alloy and for B [Session 7 Slide 16]:

$$W_\alpha + W_\gamma = 1 \qquad W_\alpha C_\alpha + W_\gamma C_\gamma = C_0$$

Substituting $W_\gamma = 1 - W_\alpha$ in the second balance:

$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + (1 - W_\alpha) C_\gamma = C_0$$
$$W_\alpha (C_\alpha - C_\gamma) = C_0 - C_\gamma$$

Dividing by $(C_\alpha - C_\gamma)$ and changing the sign of numerator and denominator:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_\gamma - C_0}{C_\gamma - C_\alpha} \qquad W_\gamma = 1 - W_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\gamma - C_\alpha}$$

This is the lever rule: each phase takes the arm on the side of the other phase.

Apply it to the phases just below 700 °C

Tie line read on the figure: $C_\alpha$ = 10 wt% B, $C_\gamma$ = 60 wt% B, length 60 − 10 = 50 wt% B.

$$W_\alpha = \frac{60 - 50}{60 - 10} = \frac{10}{50} = 0.200 \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{50 - 10}{60 - 10} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.800$$

Count the microconstituents on the tie line just above 700 °C

Just above 700 °C the alloy is γ (60 wt% B) + liquid that has reached the eutectic composition, 35 wt% B. All that liquid becomes eutectic at 700 °C, and the γ already present stays as primary γ. The same lever rule on the tie line from L (35) to γ (60), length 25 wt% B:

$$W_{\gamma,\text{primary}} = \frac{C_0 - C_E}{C_\gamma - C_E} = \frac{50 - 35}{60 - 35} = \frac{15}{25} = 0.600 \qquad W_{E} = W_{L}(700 + \Delta T) = \frac{60 - 50}{60 - 35} = \frac{10}{25} = 0.400$$

Split the eutectic into its phases

All the α of the alloy is inside the eutectic (there is no primary α), so eutectic α = $W_\alpha$ = 0.200. The γ inside the eutectic is the total γ minus the primary γ:

$$W_{\gamma,\text{eutectic}} = W_\gamma - W_{\gamma,\text{primary}} = 0.800 - 0.600 = 0.200$$

Check: $0.200 + 0.200 = 0.400 = W_E$. The eutectic itself has the composition 35 wt% B, so inside it the fraction of α is $(60 - 35)/(60 - 10) = 25/50 = 0.500$, and $0.500 \times 0.400 = 0.200$, the same eutectic α.

Answer and checks

At 700 − ΔT °C: phases α (10 wt% B, 20.0 %) and γ (60 wt% B, 80.0 %); microconstituents primary γ (60.0 %) and eutectic α + γ (40.0 %). B balance: $0.200 \times 10 + 0.800 \times 60 = 2 + 48 = 50$ wt% B $= C_0$.

Key results
  • Phases at 700 − ΔT °C: α (10 wt% B) 0.200 and γ (60 wt% B) 0.800.
  • Microconstituents: primary γ 0.600 and eutectic (α + γ) 0.400.
  • Inside the eutectic: eutectic α 0.200 and eutectic γ 0.200 of the alloy.

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Sketch the microstructure of A-20 wt% B at 700 + ΔT and 700 − ΔT °C

Follow the cooling path to the eutectic line

The vertical line of 20 wt% B crosses the α liquidus (at about 800 °C, a reading of the figure): grains of primary α nucleate and grow, while the liquid becomes richer in B along the liquidus. Just above 700 °C the α has 10 wt% B and the liquid 35 wt% B, the eutectic composition [Session 7 Slides 30-31].

Amounts at 700 + ΔT °C

Lever rule derived in part (b), on the tie line from α (10) to L (35), length 25 wt% B:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{35 - 20}{35 - 10} = \frac{15}{25} = 0.600 \qquad W_L = \frac{20 - 10}{35 - 10} = \frac{10}{25} = 0.400$$

Amounts at 700 − ΔT °C

At 700 °C the liquid (0.400) transforms at constant temperature into the eutectic $L \rightarrow \alpha + \gamma$; the primary α (0.600) does not change. The total phases come from the tie line from α (10) to γ (60), length 50:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{60 - 20}{60 - 10} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.800 \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{20 - 10}{60 - 10} = \frac{10}{50} = 0.200$$

The eutectic holds all the γ (0.200) and $0.800 - 0.600 = 0.200$ of α, and $0.200 + 0.200 = 0.400$, the eutectic fraction.

Left: rounded grains of primary α (60 %) surrounded by liquid. Right: the same α grains, now surrounded by a lamellar eutectic of alternating α and γ layers (40 %).

Key results
  • 700 + ΔT °C: primary α (10 wt% B) 0.600 in liquid (35 wt% B) 0.400.
  • 700 − ΔT °C: primary α 0.600 in eutectic α + γ 0.400; total phases α 0.800 and γ 0.200.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Melting of γ and maximum solubilities

Classify the melting of γ

The vertical line of γ (60 wt% B) reaches the liquidus at its maximum, 1150 °C: heated to that point, γ gives a liquid of 60 wt% B, its own composition, and no other solid appears. By Session 8 Slide 17 that is congruent melting. Had γ ended at a horizontal peritectic line below the liquidus, it would decompose into a liquid plus another solid (incongruent melting); this diagram has no peritectic line.

Maximum solubility of B in A

The α field is bounded by the solidus (from 900 °C) and the solvus (down to 200 °C); both meet the eutectic line at 10 wt% B, the widest point of the field. So α dissolves at most 10 wt% B, at 700 °C; below 700 °C the solvus moves back towards A and the solubility falls.

Maximum solubility of A in B

The β field is widest where its boundaries meet the 850 °C line, at 92 wt% B, so β holds at most

$$100 - 92 = 8\ \text{wt\% A}$$

at 850 °C.

Key results
  • γ melts congruently at 1150 °C (maximum of the liquidus).
  • Maximum solubility of B in A (α): 10 wt% B at 700 °C.
  • Maximum solubility of A in B (β): 100 − 92 = 8 wt% A at 850 °C.

Interpretation and checks

The compound γ splits the diagram into two simple eutectic sub-systems, A-γ and γ-B, each read exactly like Pb-Sn: a hypoeutectic alloy of the A-γ side (20 wt% B) gives primary α in an α + γ eutectic, a hypereutectic one (50 wt% B) gives primary γ. Control cases of the lever rule: as $C_0 \to 35$ wt% B the primary γ of part (b) tends to $(35-35)/25 = 0$ and the alloy becomes 100 % eutectic; as $C_0 \to 60$ it tends to 1. All fractions lie between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. Dimensions: the lever rule is a ratio of compositions (wt% over wt%), so every fraction is dimensionless, as a mass fraction must be. A common exam mistake is to give the primary γ from the tie line below 700 °C ($W_\gamma$ = 0.800): that number is the total γ, which also contains the γ inside the eutectic.

P02 — Alloy composition from the eutectic fraction in an M-N system with an incongruent compound (at%)

The figure shows the equilibrium phase diagram of a hypothetical binary system M-N, with the composition in atomic percent of N. The intermediate phase δ is the stoichiometric compound MN₂.

Figure 1. Hypothetical M-N phase diagram (at% N): α solid solution, compound δ = MN₂ (66.7 at% N) formed by a peritectic reaction at 1000 °C (α 25, δ 66.7, L 78), eutectic at 600 °C (δ 66.7, L 92, β 98); M melts at 1400 °C and N at 700 °C.

a) Write all the invariant reactions: type, temperature and composition (at% N) of every phase involved.

b) Does δ melt congruently or incongruently? Give the phases and their amounts when δ is heated to 1000 + ΔT °C, and the maximum solubility of N in M and of M in N, with their temperatures.

c) A slowly cooled M-N alloy, examined just below 600 °C, shows grains of primary δ surrounded by 30.0 % of eutectic microconstituent (atom fraction 0.300). Find the composition of the alloy.

d) For the alloy of part (c), give the phases present, their compositions and their amounts at 1000 + ΔT °C, at 1000 − ΔT °C and at 600 − ΔT °C.

Data and model

Units: the axis is in at% N, so a composition of 66.7 at% N means 0.667 mol of N atoms per mol of atoms. The lever rule is a ratio of composition differences, so on an atomic axis it gives atom (mole) fractions $x$ of each phase or constituent; this reading of the lever rule on an at% axis is an extension of Session 7 Slide 16, where the axis is in wt% and the fractions are mass fractions $W$ (the same construction, with the balance written for atoms instead of mass). The value 66.7 is $2/3 \times 100$, the N content of MN₂ (two N atoms out of three).

Model

  • Invariant reactions [Session 8 Slide 6]: eutectic $L \rightarrow \alpha + \beta$; peritectic $\alpha + L \rightarrow \beta$, where a solid field ends in an inverted V below the line [Session 8 Slides 8-9].
  • Gibbs phase rule at constant pressure, $F = C - P + 1$ [Session 7 Slide 13]: three phases of two components give $F = 0$, a horizontal line.
  • Lever rule on a tie line [Session 7 Slides 15-16]; microconstituents counted on the tie line just above the eutectic line [Session 7 Slides 30-32].
  • Congruent and incongruent melting [Session 8 Slide 17]: a compound at a maximum of the liquidus melts congruently; one ending at a peritectic line melts incongruently (liquid plus another solid).
  • Hypotheses: equilibrium cooling (complete peritectic reaction, no coring), 1 atm, compositions read on the figure as printed.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Write all the invariant reactions

Why horizontal lines are the invariant reactions

With $C = 2$ and $P = 3$ the phase rule gives $F = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$ [Session 7 Slide 13]: temperature and the three compositions are fixed, so every three-phase equilibrium is one horizontal line. The diagram has two of them.

Classify the line at 1000 °C

The line runs from α (25) to L (78). Above it lies the two-phase field α + L. Below it, the vertical line of δ (66.7) starts at the line and splits it into α + δ (left) and δ + L (right): a new solid appears below the line between the two phases above it, the inverted V of a peritectic [Session 8 Slides 8-9]. On cooling:

$$\alpha\,(25\ \text{at\% N}) + L\,(78\ \text{at\% N}) \;\xrightarrow{\;1000\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \delta\,(66.7\ \text{at\% N}) \qquad \text{peritectic}$$

Classify the line at 600 °C

The liquidus of δ falls from 78 at% N at 1000 °C and the liquidus of β falls from 700 °C (pure N); they meet in a V at 92 at% N on the line, and below it lies δ + β. A liquid that gives two solids is a eutectic:

$$L\,(92\ \text{at\% N}) \;\xrightarrow{\;600\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \delta\,(66.7\ \text{at\% N}) + \beta\,(98\ \text{at\% N}) \qquad \text{eutectic}$$

Check

Each reaction has the composition of the product (or the liquid of the eutectic) between the other two: $25 < 66.7 < 78$ and $66.7 < 92 < 98$. The eutectic temperature, 600 °C, is below the melting point of N (700 °C) and below the temperature at which δ stops existing (1000 °C).

Key results
  • 1000 °C, peritectic: α (25 at% N) + L (78 at% N) → δ (66.7 at% N).
  • 600 °C, eutectic: L (92 at% N) → δ (66.7 at% N) + β (98 at% N).

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Melting of δ, decomposition at 1000 + ΔT °C and maximum solubilities

Classify the melting of δ

The δ line stops at 1000 °C, on the peritectic line, well below the liquidus. Heating δ through 1000 °C runs the peritectic backwards, $\delta \rightarrow \alpha + L$: the compound gives a liquid of a different composition (78 at% N) plus another solid (α). By Session 8 Slide 17 this is incongruent melting.

Derive the lever rule for atom fractions

Just above 1000 °C the δ composition, $C_0$ = 66.7 at% N, lies in α + L on the tie line from α (25) to L (78). The atoms of the alloy are shared by the two phases, and so are the N atoms:

$$x_\alpha + x_L = 1 \qquad x_\alpha C_\alpha + x_L C_L = C_0$$

Substituting $x_L = 1 - x_\alpha$:

$$x_\alpha C_\alpha + (1 - x_\alpha) C_L = C_0$$
$$x_\alpha (C_\alpha - C_L) = C_0 - C_L$$
$$x_\alpha = \frac{C_L - C_0}{C_L - C_\alpha} \qquad x_L = 1 - x_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_L - C_\alpha}$$

Amounts at 1000 + ΔT °C

Tie line length $78 - 25 = 53$ at% N:

$$x_\alpha = \frac{78 - 66.7}{78 - 25} = \frac{11.3}{53} = 0.213 \qquad x_L = \frac{66.7 - 25}{78 - 25} = \frac{41.7}{53} = 0.787$$

Most of the compound turns into liquid, and about a fifth of its atoms stay as solid α.

Maximum solubilities

The α field is widest where it touches the peritectic line, at 25 at% N: N dissolves in M up to 25 at% N at 1000 °C. The β field touches the eutectic line at 98 at% N, so M dissolves in N up to

$$100 - 98 = 2\ \text{at\% M}$$

at 600 °C.

Key results
  • δ melts incongruently (it ends at the peritectic line, 1000 °C).
  • At 1000 + ΔT °C, δ has become α (25 at% N) 0.213 + L (78 at% N) 0.787.
  • Maximum solubility of N in M: 25 at% N at 1000 °C; of M in N: 2 at% M at 600 °C.

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Composition of the alloy with primary δ and 30.0 % eutectic

Choose the side of the eutectic

The eutectic microconstituent at 600 °C forms from alloys between 66.7 and 98 at% N. Those between 66.7 and 92 at% N reach 600 °C with primary δ, those between 92 and 98 at% N with primary β. The alloy shows primary δ, so $66.7 < C_0 < 92$.

Write the eutectic fraction with the lever rule

Just above 600 °C the alloy is δ (66.7) + liquid of eutectic composition (92). All that liquid becomes eutectic, so the eutectic fraction is the liquid fraction on this tie line (lever rule of part b):

$$x_E = x_L(600 + \Delta T) = \frac{C_0 - C_\delta}{C_E - C_\delta}$$

Isolate the composition

Multiply both sides by $(C_E - C_\delta)$:

$$x_E\,(C_E - C_\delta) = C_0 - C_\delta$$

Add $C_\delta$ to both sides:

$$C_0 = C_\delta + x_E\,(C_E - C_\delta)$$

Substitute $x_E$ = 0.300, $C_\delta$ = 66.7 and $C_E$ = 92 at% N:

$$C_0 = 66.7 + 0.300 \times (92 - 66.7) = 66.7 + 0.300 \times 25.3 = 66.7 + 7.59 = 74.29\ \text{at\% N}$$

Check

$66.7 < 74.29 < 92$, as required. Back-substitution: $(74.29 - 66.7)/(92 - 66.7) = 7.59/25.3 = 0.300$. The primary δ is $1 - 0.300 = 0.700$. The other root, from the hypereutectic side, $x_E = (98 - C_0)/(98 - 92)$, would give $C_0 = 98 - 0.300 \times 6 = 96.2$ at% N, but that alloy has primary β, not δ, so it is rejected.

Key results
  • The alloy is between δ (66.7) and the eutectic point (92), because its primary constituent is δ.
  • $C_0$ = 74.29 at% N (about 74.3 at% N).

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Phases of the alloy M-74.29 at% N at 1000 ± ΔT and 600 − ΔT °C

Just above the peritectic line

The alloy, 74.29 at% N, lies on the tie line from α (25) to L (78), length 53:

$$x_\alpha = \frac{78 - 74.29}{78 - 25} = \frac{3.71}{53} = 0.0700 \qquad x_L = \frac{74.29 - 25}{78 - 25} = \frac{49.29}{53} = 0.930$$

Just below the peritectic line

At 1000 °C all the α reacts with part of the liquid to form δ. Since $C_0 = 74.29 > 66.7$, the α runs out first and liquid is left over (as for alloy 2 of Session 8 Slide 9). Tie line from δ (66.7) to L (78), length 11.3:

$$x_\delta = \frac{78 - 74.29}{78 - 66.7} = \frac{3.71}{11.3} = 0.328 \qquad x_L = \frac{74.29 - 66.7}{78 - 66.7} = \frac{7.59}{11.3} = 0.672$$

Just below the eutectic line

Between 1000 and 600 °C more δ grows from the liquid, which follows the liquidus to 92 at% N; at 600 °C the remaining liquid (0.300, part c) becomes eutectic δ + β. Tie line from δ (66.7) to β (98), length 31.3:

$$x_\delta = \frac{98 - 74.29}{98 - 66.7} = \frac{23.71}{31.3} = 0.758 \qquad x_\beta = \frac{74.29 - 66.7}{98 - 66.7} = \frac{7.59}{31.3} = 0.242$$

Check with the microconstituents

The δ total (0.758) is primary δ (0.700) plus eutectic δ, so the eutectic δ is 0.0575 and the eutectic β 0.242; together $0.0575 + 0.242 = 0.300 = x_E$. N balance at 600 − ΔT: $0.758 \times 66.7 + 0.242 \times 98 = 50.56 + 23.72 = 74.28$ at% N, equal to $C_0$ = 74.29 within the rounding of the fractions.

Key results
  • 1000 + ΔT °C: α (25 at% N) 0.0700 and L (78 at% N) 0.930.
  • 1000 − ΔT °C: δ (66.7 at% N) 0.328 and L (78 at% N) 0.672.
  • 600 − ΔT °C: δ (66.7 at% N) 0.758 and β (98 at% N) 0.242.

Interpretation and checks

δ cannot be melted as a single liquid of its own composition: it decomposes at 1000 °C into a liquid and the M-rich α, which is why it ends in a peritectic and not at a maximum of the liquidus. The fraction of eutectic is a fingerprint of the composition: on the δ side it grows linearly from 0 at 66.7 at% N to 1 at 92 at% N, so a measured eutectic fraction fixes $C_0$ uniquely once the primary constituent is known. Limiting cases: $x_E \to 0$ gives $C_0 \to 66.7$ (pure δ) and $x_E \to 1$ gives $C_0 \to 92$ (fully eutectic). Dimensions: every fraction is at% over at%, dimensionless, and every composition stays between the ends of its tie line. Common mistake: using mass fractions or quoting the result in wt%; on an at% diagram the lever rule gives atom fractions, and converting to wt% would need the atomic masses of M and N, which the problem does not give.

P03 — Cooling an X-40 wt% Y alloy through a peritectic, a eutectic and a eutectoid

The figure shows the equilibrium phase diagram of a hypothetical binary system X-Y (composition in wt% Y). γ is an intermetallic compound of 55 wt% Y; β is an intermediate solid solution that exists only between 1100 and 600 °C.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Invariant reactions and the melting of γ

Why each horizontal line is an invariant reaction

Three phases of a two-component system at constant pressure leave $F = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$ [Session 7 Slide 13], so each three-phase equilibrium is one horizontal line with three fixed compositions. The diagram has four.

The line at 1100 °C

Above it lies α + L, from α (15) to L (45). Below it the field of β starts at 30 wt% Y, between the two: a new solid forms from a solid and a liquid, the inverted V of a peritectic [Session 8 Slides 8-9]:

$$\alpha\,(15) + L\,(45) \;\xrightarrow{\;1100\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \beta\,(30) \qquad \text{peritectic, wt\% Y}$$

The lines at 900 and 650 °C

At 900 °C the liquidus of β (falling from 45 at 1100 °C) and the left branch of the liquidus of γ (falling from 1000 °C) meet in a V at 50 wt% Y; below lies β + γ. At 650 °C the right branch of the γ liquidus and the liquidus of δ (from 800 °C, pure Y) meet in a V at 80 wt% Y; below lies γ + δ. Both are eutectics:

$$L\,(50) \;\xrightarrow{\;900\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \beta\,(38) + \gamma\,(55) \qquad L\,(80) \;\xrightarrow{\;650\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \gamma\,(55) + \delta\,(95)$$

The line at 600 °C

No liquid reaches this line. The single-phase field β narrows downwards between its two solvus lines and closes in a V at 25 wt% Y on the line; below it lies α + γ. A solid that transforms into two other solids is a eutectoid [Session 8 Slide 7]:

$$\beta\,(25) \;\xrightarrow{\;600\ ^\circ\text{C}\;}\; \alpha\,(10) + \gamma\,(55) \qquad \text{eutectoid}$$

Melting of γ

The vertical line of γ reaches the liquidus at its maximum, 1000 °C, where γ gives a liquid of its own composition (55 wt% Y): congruent melting [Session 8 Slide 17].

Key results
  • 1100 °C, peritectic: α (15) + L (45) → β (30).
  • 900 °C, eutectic: L (50) → β (38) + γ (55).
  • 650 °C, eutectic: L (80) → γ (55) + δ (95).
  • 600 °C, eutectoid: β (25) → α (10) + γ (55). All in wt% Y.
  • γ melts congruently at 1000 °C.

Part (b) · 0.5 points

Gibbs phase rule at the eutectoid point, in β + L and in β

State the rule and why N = 1

The phase rule is $F = C - P + N$ [Session 7 Slide 13]. Pressure is fixed at 1 atm in a phase diagram of this kind, so only temperature is a non-compositional variable, $N = 1$, and $F = C - P + 1$. The system has two components, X and Y, so $C = 2$.

At the eutectoid point

Three phases coexist, β, α and γ, so $P = 3$:

$$F = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$$

Nothing can be changed: the transformation happens only at 600 °C, with β of 25, α of 10 and γ of 55 wt% Y. On a cooling curve it gives a plateau, like the eutectic.

Inside β + L

Two phases, $P = 2$:

$$F = 2 - 2 + 1 = 1$$

Choosing the temperature uses the only degree of freedom: the compositions of β and L are then fixed by the ends of the tie line, and the alloy composition only changes their amounts.

Inside β

One phase, $P = 1$:

$$F = 2 - 1 + 1 = 2$$

Temperature and composition can both change (within the field) without a new phase appearing.

Key results
  • Eutectoid point (25 wt% Y, 600 °C): $F = 0$.
  • β + L field: $F = 1$.
  • β field: $F = 2$.

Part (c) · 1 point

Solidification of X-40 wt% Y down to 900 °C

First solid: primary α

The 40 wt% Y vertical crosses the liquidus of α just above 1100 °C (the liquidus reaches 45 wt% Y at 1100 °C, so for 40 wt% Y it is a few tens of degrees higher; a reading of the figure). Crystals of α start to grow, and the liquid becomes richer in Y along the liquidus.

Derive the lever rule and apply it at 1100 + ΔT

On a tie line with phases α and L the alloy and the element Y must both be conserved:

$$W_\alpha + W_L = 1 \qquad W_\alpha C_\alpha + W_L C_L = C_0$$

Substituting $W_L = 1 - W_\alpha$ in the balance of Y:

$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + (1 - W_\alpha) C_L = C_0$$

Expanding the bracket:

$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + C_L - W_\alpha C_L = C_0$$

Grouping the terms in $W_\alpha$ and moving $C_L$ to the right-hand side:

$$W_\alpha (C_\alpha - C_L) = C_0 - C_L$$

Dividing by $(C_\alpha - C_L)$ and changing the sign of numerator and denominator:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_L - C_0}{C_L - C_\alpha} \qquad W_L = 1 - W_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_L - C_\alpha}$$

Just above 1100 °C, α has 15 and L has 45 wt% Y (length 30):

$$W_\alpha = \frac{45 - 40}{45 - 15} = \frac{5}{30} = 0.167 \qquad W_L = \frac{40 - 15}{45 - 15} = \frac{25}{30} = 0.833$$

The peritectic reaction at 1100 °C

At 1100 °C, α (15) reacts with L (45) to give β (30). The alloy (40) lies to the right of β, between β and L, so the α runs out before the liquid. Just below 1100 °C the tie line runs from β (30) to L (45), length 15:

$$W_\beta = \frac{45 - 40}{45 - 30} = \frac{5}{15} = 0.333 \qquad W_L = \frac{40 - 30}{45 - 30} = \frac{10}{15} = 0.667$$

From 1100 to 900 °C: β grows from the liquid

In β + L the liquid follows the β liquidus from 45 to 50 wt% Y and β follows its solidus from 30 to 38 wt% Y; the amount of β grows. Just above 900 °C (β 38, L 50, length 12):

$$W_\beta = \frac{50 - 40}{50 - 38} = \frac{10}{12} = 0.833 \qquad W_L = \frac{40 - 38}{50 - 38} = \frac{2}{12} = 0.167$$

This β, formed partly by the peritectic and partly from the liquid, is the primary β (0.833), and the liquid, now of eutectic composition, is the future eutectic (0.167).

The eutectic at 900 °C

The remaining liquid transforms at 900 °C into β (38) + γ (55). Just below the line, the total phases on the tie line from β (38) to γ (55), length 17:

$$W_\beta = \frac{55 - 40}{55 - 38} = \frac{15}{17} = 0.882 \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{40 - 38}{55 - 38} = \frac{2}{17} = 0.118$$

Microconstituents: primary β 0.833 and eutectic (β + γ) 0.167. All the γ is in the eutectic, and the eutectic β is $0.882 - 0.833 = 0.049$; $0.049 + 0.118 = 0.167$.

Key results
  • 1100 + ΔT: α (15) 0.167, L (45) 0.833.
  • 1100 − ΔT: β (30) 0.333, L (45) 0.667.
  • 900 + ΔT: β (38) 0.833, L (50) 0.167.
  • 900 − ΔT: β (38) 0.882, γ (55) 0.118; microconstituents primary β 0.833 and eutectic β + γ 0.167.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Cooling to 600 − ΔT °C and the three sketches

From 900 to 600 °C: precipitation of γ

In β + γ the boundary of β moves from 38 to 25 wt% Y as the temperature falls, so the β loses Y and γ precipitates from it in the solid state (Session 8 Slides 2-4 call this solid-state precipitation). Just above 600 °C (β 25, γ 55, length 30):

$$W_\beta = \frac{55 - 40}{55 - 25} = \frac{15}{30} = 0.500 \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{40 - 25}{55 - 25} = \frac{15}{30} = 0.500$$

The γ grew from 0.118 to 0.500, so $0.500 - 0.118 = 0.382$ of γ precipitated between 900 and 600 °C.

The eutectoid at 600 °C

All the β (0.500), which now has the eutectoid composition 25 wt% Y, transforms into the fine eutectoid α + γ. Below the line the total phases on the tie line from α (10) to γ (55), length 45:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{55 - 40}{55 - 10} = \frac{15}{45} = 0.333 \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{40 - 10}{55 - 10} = \frac{30}{45} = 0.667$$

The microconstituents are counted just above the line, like a primary constituent above a eutectic: eutectoid = $W_\beta(600 + \Delta T)$ = 0.500, and γ outside it = 0.500. Check: inside the eutectoid (25 wt% Y) the fraction of α is $(55 - 25)/(55 - 10) = 30/45 = 0.667$, and $0.667 \times 0.500 = 0.333$, all the α of the alloy.

The three sketches

At 1100 + ΔT: a few rounded grains of primary α in a large amount of liquid. At 900 − ΔT: large grains of primary β (the α has disappeared in the peritectic) in a lamellar eutectic β + γ. At 600 − ΔT: every former β region, primary and inside the eutectic, is now a fine eutectoid α + γ, with films of γ precipitated on the boundaries of the former β grains.

  • 600 + ΔT: β (25) 0.500, γ (55) 0.500.
  • 600 − ΔT: α (10) 0.333, γ (55) 0.667.

Interpretation and checks

The alloy passes through three invariant reactions, but none of the original microconstituents survives unchanged: the primary α is consumed by the peritectic, and the primary β disappears in the eutectoid, leaving only a trace of its shape. This is the solid-state counterpart of the eutectic: like a eutectic liquid, the β of 25 wt% Y transforms at one temperature into two phases ($F = 0$). Checks: every fraction lies between 0 and 1, each pair adds up to 1, and the Y balance at 600 − ΔT is $0.333 \times 10 + 0.667 \times 55 = 3.33 + 36.67 = 40.0$ wt% Y. Limiting case: an alloy of exactly 30 wt% Y would leave neither α nor liquid after the peritectic and be 100 % β just below 1100 °C. Dimensions: all fractions are wt% over wt%, dimensionless. Common mistake: taking the eutectoid fraction from the tie line below 600 °C ($W_\alpha$ = 0.333, a phase) instead of from the β just above it (0.500, a microconstituent).

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Masses of the phases of 2.00 kg of G-55 wt% H at 1150 ± ΔT °C

Derive the lever rule

On a tie line with phases L and β the mass of the alloy and the mass of H are conserved:

$$W_L + W_\beta = 1 \qquad W_L C_L + W_\beta C_\beta = C_0$$

Substituting $W_\beta = 1 - W_L$:

$$W_L C_L + (1 - W_L) C_\beta = C_0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad W_L (C_L - C_\beta) = C_0 - C_\beta$$
$$W_L = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_L} \qquad W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_L}{C_\beta - C_L}$$

Just above 1150 °C

The alloy lies in β + L on the tie line from L (50) to β (88), length 38 wt% H:

$$W_L = \frac{88 - 55}{88 - 50} = \frac{33}{38} = 0.8684 \qquad W_\beta = \frac{55 - 50}{88 - 50} = \frac{5}{38} = 0.1316$$

Masses, with $m_i = W_i\,m_0$:

$$m_L = 0.8684 \times 2.00\ \text{kg} = 1.737\ \text{kg} \qquad m_\beta = 0.1316 \times 2.00\ \text{kg} = 0.263\ \text{kg}$$

Just below 1150 °C

At 1150 °C, L (50) and β (88) react to form η (60). The alloy (55) lies left of η, between L and η, so the β runs out first and liquid remains. Tie line from L (50) to η (60), length 10:

$$W_\eta = \frac{55 - 50}{60 - 50} = \frac{5}{10} = 0.500 \qquad W_L = \frac{60 - 55}{60 - 50} = \frac{5}{10} = 0.500$$
$$m_\eta = 0.500 \times 2.00\ \text{kg} = 1.000\ \text{kg} \qquad m_L = 0.500 \times 2.00\ \text{kg} = 1.000\ \text{kg}$$

Mass balance of the reaction

All 0.263 kg of β disappear. The η formed (1.000 kg) comes from that β and from $1.000 - 0.263 = 0.737$ kg of liquid, which is exactly the drop of the liquid from 1.737 to 1.000 kg. H check: $0.263 \times 0.88 + 0.737 \times 0.50 = 0.2316 + 0.3684 = 0.600$ kg of H in 1.000 kg of η, that is 60 wt% H, the composition of η.

Key results
  • 1150 + ΔT: L (50 wt% H) 1.737 kg, β (88 wt% H) 0.263 kg.
  • 1150 − ΔT: η (60 wt% H) 1.000 kg, L (50 wt% H) 1.000 kg.
  • All the β, 0.263 kg, is used up by the peritectic.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Microconstituents and phases of the casting at 1000 − ΔT °C

Primary η just above 1000 °C

The tie line runs from the eutectic liquid (40) to η (60), length 20 wt% H. The η is primary and the liquid will become eutectic:

$$W_{\eta,\text{primary}} = \frac{55 - 40}{60 - 40} = \frac{15}{20} = 0.750 \qquad W_E = \frac{60 - 55}{60 - 40} = \frac{5}{20} = 0.250$$
$$m_{\eta,\text{primary}} = 0.750 \times 2.00 = 1.500\ \text{kg} \qquad m_E = 0.250 \times 2.00 = 0.500\ \text{kg}$$

Phases just below 1000 °C

Tie line from θ (30) to η (60), length 30:

$$W_\theta = \frac{60 - 55}{60 - 30} = \frac{5}{30} = 0.1667 \qquad W_\eta = \frac{55 - 30}{60 - 30} = \frac{25}{30} = 0.8333$$
$$m_\theta = 0.1667 \times 2.00 = 0.333\ \text{kg} \qquad m_\eta = 0.8333 \times 2.00 = 1.667\ \text{kg}$$

Split the eutectic

All the θ is in the eutectic (there is no primary θ), so eutectic θ = 0.333 kg. The eutectic η is the total η minus the primary η:

$$m_{\eta,E} = 1.667 - 1.500 = 0.167\ \text{kg}$$

Check: $0.333 + 0.167 = 0.500$ kg, the mass of eutectic. The eutectic has 40 wt% H, so inside it the θ fraction is $(60 - 40)/(60 - 30) = 20/30 = 0.667$, and $0.667 \times 0.500 = 0.333$ kg, as found.

Key results
  • Primary η 1.500 kg; eutectic (θ + η) 0.500 kg.
  • Phases: θ (30 wt% H) 0.333 kg, η (60 wt% H) 1.667 kg.
  • Inside the eutectic: θ 0.333 kg and η 0.167 kg.

Interpretation and checks

The two compounds split the diagram into three simpler parts: G-θ (a simple eutectic), θ-η (a simple eutectic) and η-H (a peritectic). θ behaves like a pure metal because it melts at a maximum of the liquidus; η cannot be melted as a single liquid. The casting of 55 wt% H shows the peritectic in action: the 0.263 kg of β formed first is completely consumed at 1150 °C, so the final microstructure has no trace of β, only primary η in a θ + η eutectic. Checks: every pair of fractions adds up to 1 and the masses add up to 2.00 kg; H balance at 1000 − ΔT: $0.333 \times 0.30 + 1.667 \times 0.60 = 0.100 + 1.000 = 1.100$ kg of H $= 0.55 \times 2.00$ kg. Limiting case: a casting of exactly 60 wt% H would leave no liquid after the peritectic and be 100 % η. Dimensions: $W$ is wt% over wt%, dimensionless, and $m = W\,m_0$ has units of kg. Common mistake: drawing η up to the liquidus as if it melted congruently; the table says η exists only below 1150 °C, which is the signature of a peritectic.

The figure shows the Pb-Sn phase diagram with the values of Session 7 Slide 32. A Pb-50 wt% Sn alloy is cooled slowly (equilibrium) from 300 °C to 100 °C. The readings needed are printed on the figure: the alloy meets the liquidus at 215 °C; at 200 °C the tie line through the alloy ends at 16.7 and 55.7 wt% Sn; at 100 °C it ends at 4.8 and 99.8 wt% Sn.

Figure 1. Pb-Sn phase diagram with the values of Session 7 Slide 32 (Pb melts at 327 °C, Sn at 232 °C; eutectic at 183 °C and 61.9 wt% Sn; α of 18.3 and β of 97.8 wt% Sn at 183 °C). The dashed vertical line is the alloy of 50 wt% Sn, which meets the liquidus at 215 °C; the tie line at 200 °C ends at 16.7 (α) and 55.7 (L) wt% Sn, and the one at 100 °C at 4.8 (α) and 99.8 (β) wt% Sn.

(a) Write the invariant reaction of the diagram (name, temperature, compositions of the three phases). Classify the alloy and say at which temperatures its solidification starts and ends, and which phase forms first.

(b) At 200 °C, give the phases present, their compositions and their mass fractions.

(c) Just above (183 + ΔT) and just below (183 − ΔT) the eutectic temperature, give the phases with their compositions and mass fractions, and the microconstituents with their mass fractions. What fraction of all the α of the alloy is inside the eutectic?

(d) At 100 °C, give the phases, their compositions and mass fractions. Explain where the extra β comes from.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Invariant reaction, type of alloy and start and end of solidification

Identify the invariant reaction at point E

The only horizontal line of the diagram is at 183 °C. It touches three phase fields from above and below: the liquid meets it at a single point E (the minimum of the liquidus), α ends at its left end and β at its right end. A liquid that turns into two solids at a fixed temperature and composition is a eutectic reaction [Session 7 Slides 26-27]. Reading the three compositions on the line:

$$\text{cooling: } L\ (61.9\ \text{wt\% Sn}) \rightarrow \alpha\ (18.3\ \text{wt\% Sn}) + \beta\ (97.8\ \text{wt\% Sn}) \quad \text{at } 183\ ^\circ\text{C (eutectic)}$$

It is invariant: with three phases, $F = 3 - P = 3 - 3 = 0$, so neither the temperature nor any composition can change while the reaction proceeds [Session 7 Slide 13].

Classify the alloy

The eutectic line runs from 18.3 to 97.8 wt% Sn, and the eutectic point is at 61.9 wt% Sn. An alloy between the α end and E is hypoeutectic, and its primary (proeutectic) phase is α [Session 7 Slide 30]:

$$C_\alpha = 18.3 < C_0 = 50 < C_E = 61.9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{hypoeutectic alloy, primary phase } \alpha$$

Read the start and the end of solidification

Following the dashed vertical line $C_0 = 50$ from above: it stays in L down to the liquidus, which it meets at 215 °C (printed on the figure). Below that the alloy is in the α + L field, so the first solid is α, the phase on the left boundary of that field. The liquid keeps changing its composition along the liquidus until it reaches E; the last liquid, of 61.9 wt% Sn, solidifies as eutectic at 183 °C, where solidification ends. Solidification therefore takes place over the interval $215 - 183 = 32$ °C.

Key results
  • Eutectic at 183 °C: L (61.9 wt% Sn) → α (18.3 wt% Sn) + β (97.8 wt% Sn) on cooling.
  • $18.3 < 50 < 61.9$: hypoeutectic alloy; the primary phase is α.
  • Solidification starts at 215 °C (first solid α) and ends at 183 °C.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Phases, compositions and amounts at 200 °C

Place the point and read the tie line

At 200 °C the alloy point (50, 200) is in the α + L field. The horizontal tie line through it meets the α solidus at $C_\alpha = 16.7$ wt% Sn and the liquidus at $C_L = 55.7$ wt% Sn (printed on the figure). These are the compositions of the two phases [Session 7 Slide 15].

Derive the lever rule from two mass balances

Take a mass of alloy as the unit. The alloy is made only of α and L, so their mass fractions add up to one (total mass balance); the tin in the alloy is the tin in α plus the tin in L (mass balance of Sn):

$$W_\alpha + W_L = 1$$
$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + W_L C_L = C_0$$

From the first equation, $W_\alpha = 1 - W_L$. Substituting into the second:

$$(1 - W_L)\,C_\alpha + W_L C_L = C_0$$
$$C_\alpha + W_L\,(C_L - C_\alpha) = C_0$$
$$W_L = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_L - C_\alpha}$$

and then $W_\alpha = 1 - W_L = \dfrac{C_L - C_\alpha - C_0 + C_\alpha}{C_L - C_\alpha} = \dfrac{C_L - C_0}{C_L - C_\alpha}$. Each phase is given by the arm of the lever on the side of the other phase, divided by the length of the tie line [Session 7 Slide 16].

Substitute the readings at 200 °C

The tie line has length $C_L - C_\alpha = 55.7 - 16.7 = 39.0$ wt% Sn:

$$W_L = \frac{50 - 16.7}{55.7 - 16.7} = \frac{33.3}{39.0} = 0.854$$
$$W_\alpha = \frac{55.7 - 50}{55.7 - 16.7} = \frac{5.7}{39.0} = 0.146$$

Check: $0.854 + 0.146 = 1.000$, and the Sn balance $0.1462 \times 16.7 + 0.8538 \times 55.7 = 2.44 + 47.56 = 50.0$ wt% Sn returns $C_0$. The alloy is still mostly liquid, because 200 °C is only 15 °C below its liquidus.

Key results
  • Phases: α (16.7 wt% Sn) and L (55.7 wt% Sn).
  • $W_L = 0.854$, $W_\alpha = 0.146$.

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Phases and microconstituents at 183 ± ΔT °C

Lever rule just above 183 °C

At 183 + ΔT the alloy is still in α + L; the tie line ends at the two ends of the left part of the eutectic line, $C_\alpha = 18.3$ and $C_L = C_E = 61.9$ wt% Sn (length $61.9 - 18.3 = 43.6$). With the lever rule of part (b):

$$W_L = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_E - C_\alpha} = \frac{50 - 18.3}{61.9 - 18.3} = \frac{31.7}{43.6} = 0.727$$
$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_E - C_0}{C_E - C_\alpha} = \frac{61.9 - 50}{43.6} = \frac{11.9}{43.6} = 0.273$$

Lever rule just below 183 °C (total phases)

At 183 − ΔT the eutectic reaction has consumed all the liquid and the alloy is in α + β. The tie line is the whole eutectic line, from $C_\alpha = 18.3$ to $C_\beta = 97.8$ wt% Sn (length $97.8 - 18.3 = 79.5$) [Session 7 Slide 32]:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{97.8 - 50}{79.5} = \frac{47.8}{79.5} = 0.601$$
$$W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{50 - 18.3}{79.5} = \frac{31.7}{79.5} = 0.399$$

Microconstituents just below 183 °C

The α that existed at 183 + ΔT does not take part in the reaction: it stays as primary α. The liquid, which has exactly the eutectic composition, becomes the eutectic microconstituent (alternating α and β) [Session 7 Slides 30, 32]. Therefore the microconstituent fractions are the phase fractions just above the eutectic temperature:

$$W_{\alpha'} = W_\alpha(183 + \Delta T) = 0.273 \qquad W_E = W_L(183 + \Delta T) = 0.727$$

Split the α between primary and eutectic

All the β is inside the eutectic (the primary phase is α). The α inside the eutectic is the total α minus the primary α; with four significant figures, $W_\alpha = 47.8/79.5 = 0.6013$ and $W_{\alpha'} = 11.9/43.6 = 0.2729$:

$$W_{\alpha,E} = W_\alpha - W_{\alpha'} = 0.6013 - 0.2729 = 0.328$$
$$\frac{W_{\alpha,E}}{W_\alpha} = \frac{0.3283}{0.6013} = 0.546$$

So 54.6 % of all the α of the alloy is eutectic α. Check: the eutectic is $W_{\alpha,E} + W_{\beta,E} = 0.328 + 0.399 = 0.727 = W_E$, and its own make-up is $(97.8 - 61.9)/79.5 = 0.452$ α, so $0.727 \times 0.452 = 0.328$ (checked).

Key results
  • 183 + ΔT: α (18.3) and L (61.9 wt% Sn); $W_\alpha = 0.273$, $W_L = 0.727$.
  • 183 − ΔT: α (18.3) and β (97.8 wt% Sn); $W_\alpha = 0.601$, $W_\beta = 0.399$.
  • Microconstituents: primary α $W_{\alpha'} = 0.273$, eutectic $W_E = 0.727$; α inside the eutectic 0.328 of the alloy; 54.6 % of all the α is eutectic α.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Phases at 100 °C and precipitation of β

Lever rule at 100 °C

The alloy is still in α + β; the tie line now ends on the α solvus at 4.8 and on the β solvus at 99.8 wt% Sn (length $99.8 - 4.8 = 95.0$):

$$W_\alpha = \frac{99.8 - 50}{99.8 - 4.8} = \frac{49.8}{95.0} = 0.524$$
$$W_\beta = \frac{50 - 4.8}{99.8 - 4.8} = \frac{45.2}{95.0} = 0.476$$

Explain the change with the solvus

The α solvus slopes to the left: the solubility of Sn in α falls from 18.3 wt% at 183 °C to 4.8 wt% at 100 °C. The α of the alloy (primary and eutectic) is therefore supersaturated as it cools and rejects the excess Sn as small particles of β inside its grains: this is precipitation in the solid state, which grows as the temperature decreases [Session 8 Slides 3-4]. The amount of β gained is

$$\Delta W_\beta = W_\beta(100\ ^\circ\text{C}) - W_\beta(183 - \Delta T) = 0.4758 - 0.3987 = 0.077$$

that is, 7.7 % of the mass of the alloy. The β side changes little (97.8 → 99.8 wt% Sn), so almost all the change comes from the α side. The microconstituents keep their names: primary α (now with fine β precipitates) and eutectic.

Key results
  • α (4.8 wt% Sn) and β (99.8 wt% Sn); $W_\alpha = 0.524$, $W_\beta = 0.476$.
  • β grows by 0.077 of the alloy between 183 − ΔT and 100 °C: it precipitates from the α, whose Sn solubility falls from 18.3 to 4.8 wt%.

Interpretation and checks

On cooling, the Pb-50 Sn alloy starts to solidify at 215 °C with α, holds 14.6 % α at 200 °C and 27.3 % just above 183 °C; the remaining 72.7 % of liquid, now of eutectic composition, solidifies at 183 °C as eutectic. Because 50 wt% Sn is close to E (61.9), the eutectic dominates the microstructure, and more than half of the α (54.6 %) is eutectic α.

Control cases: as $C_0 \to 18.3$ the eutectic fraction $W_E = (C_0 - 18.3)/43.6 \to 0$ (all primary α), and as $C_0 \to 61.9$, $W_E \to 1$ (fully eutectic). All fractions lie between 0 and 1, and each pair adds up to 1. Dimensional check: the lever rule is a ratio of two composition differences, (wt% Sn)/(wt% Sn), so the fractions are dimensionless, as they must be.

Common exam mistake: taking the primary α from the long tie line below 183 °C (0.601). That number is the total α (primary plus eutectic); the primary α must be read on the short tie line just above 183 °C, which ends at the eutectic liquid.

P06 — Building a eutectic diagram from a description and using it

Two metals A and B are completely soluble in the liquid state and partially soluble in the solid state, where they form a solid solution α rich in A and a solid solution β rich in B. Metal A melts at 900 °C and metal B at 700 °C. The system has a single invariant point, a eutectic at 550 °C, where the liquid contains 70 wt% B. At that temperature α can dissolve at most 20 wt% B and β at most 8 wt% A. At room temperature (20 °C) the solubilities fall to 5 wt% B in α and 3 wt% A in β. Assume that every boundary of the diagram is a straight line between the points given, and that cooling is slow (equilibrium).

(a) Draw the phase diagram (temperature against wt% B), label every region and write the invariant reaction.

(b) For an alloy of 85 wt% B: at which temperature does it start to solidify and what is the composition of the first solid? Give the phases, their compositions and their mass fractions at 600 °C.

(c) For the same alloy, give the microconstituents and their mass fractions just below 550 °C, and the mass fraction of each phase inside the eutectic.

(d) Which alloys of the system contain eutectic at room temperature? An alloy of 12 wt% B contains none: at which temperature does β appear in it, and how much β is there at 20 °C?

The composition axis is wt% B, so a content of A is turned into a content of B with $C_B = 100 - C_A$: 8 wt% A is 92 wt% B, and 3 wt% A is 97 wt% B. The lever rule uses ratios of composition differences, so the wt% can be used directly (the factor 1/100 that turns them into mass fractions cancels).

Model

  • Binary eutectic system with partial solid solubility [Session 7 Slides 26-27]: liquidus from each melting point down to E; solidus from each melting point to the end of the eutectic line; solvus from each end of the eutectic line down to room temperature.
  • Straight boundary through $(C_1, T_1)$ and $(C_2, T_2)$, as the statement imposes: $C = C_1 + (T - T_1)\dfrac{C_2 - C_1}{T_2 - T_1}$ (linear interpolation).
  • Lever rule on a horizontal tie line [Session 7 Slides 15-16]; microconstituents of a hypereutectic alloy: primary β and eutectic [Session 7 Slides 31-32]; precipitation of β when an alloy crosses the solvus [Session 8 Slides 3-4].

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Diagram and invariant reaction

Draw the boundaries from the points

The liquidus is the line above which only liquid exists; in a eutectic system it falls from the melting point of each metal to the eutectic point, so it is made of the two segments (0, 900)–(70, 550) and (70, 550)–(100, 700). The solidus is the line below which there is no liquid; it falls from each melting point to the corresponding end of the eutectic line: (0, 900)–(20, 550) for α and (100, 700)–(92, 550) for β. The horizontal eutectic line joins (20, 550) and (92, 550). Below it, the solvus lines give the limit of solubility in each solid solution: (20, 550)–(5, 20) for α and (92, 550)–(97, 20) for β [Session 7 Slides 26-27].

Label the regions

Above the liquidus: L. Between the A-side liquidus and the α solidus: α + L. Between the B-side liquidus and the β solidus: β + L. Left of the α solidus and α solvus: α. Right of the β solidus and β solvus: β. Below the eutectic line, between the two solvus lines: α + β. Every two-phase field lies between two one-phase fields of the phases it contains, as it must in a binary diagram.

Figure 1. The A-B diagram of Bank P06 drawn with straight lines: A melts at 900 °C and B at 700 °C; eutectic at 550 °C and 70 wt% B; at 550 °C α holds 20 wt% B and β 92 wt% B (8 wt% A); at 20 °C the solvus lines reach 5 and 97 wt% B. Dashed: the alloys of 85 wt% B (liquidus 625 °C) and 12 wt% B (solvus 267 °C).

Write the invariant reaction

At 550 °C a liquid of fixed composition gives two solids of fixed compositions; this is a eutectic reaction, and with three phases it has $F = 3 - P = 0$ degrees of freedom [Session 7 Slide 13]:

$$\text{cooling: } L\ (70\ \text{wt\% B}) \rightarrow \alpha\ (20\ \text{wt\% B}) + \beta\ (92\ \text{wt\% B}) \quad \text{at } 550\ ^\circ\text{C (eutectic)}$$

The diagram is thermodynamically sensible: both liquidus branches fall towards E, each solid solution is richest in solute at the eutectic temperature, and the eutectic composition lies between those of α and β.

Key results
  • Liquidus (0, 900)–(70, 550)–(100, 700); solidus (0, 900)–(20, 550) and (100, 700)–(92, 550); eutectic line (20, 550)–(92, 550); solvus (20, 550)–(5, 20) and (92, 550)–(97, 20).
  • Regions: L; α + L; β + L; α; β; α + β.
  • Cooling: L (70 wt% B) → α (20 wt% B) + β (92 wt% B) at 550 °C, eutectic.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Start of solidification and the tie line at 600 °C

Liquidus temperature of the alloy

On the straight B-side liquidus from (70, 550) to (100, 700), the temperature rises by $(700 - 550)/(100 - 70) = 150/30 = 5$ °C per wt% B. Writing the line and putting $C = 85$:

$$T = 550 + 5\,(C - 70) = 550 + 5\,(85 - 70) = 550 + 75 = 625\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

Composition of the first solid

At 625 °C the first crystal is the solid in equilibrium with the liquid, at the other end of the tie line, on the β solidus from (92, 550) to (100, 700). On that line the composition grows by $(100 - 92)/(700 - 550) = 8/150$ wt% B per °C:

$$C_\beta = 92 + (T - 550)\,\frac{8}{150} = 92 + (625 - 550)\,\frac{8}{150} = 92 + 4.0 = 96.0\ \text{wt\% B}$$

The first solid (β of 96 wt% B) is richer in B than the alloy, as the tie line requires.

Ends of the tie line at 600 °C

At 600 °C the alloy (85, 600) lies between the liquidus and the β solidus: β + L. The liquid end is found by isolating $C$ in the liquidus equation:

$$600 = 550 + 5\,(C_L - 70) \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_L - 70 = \frac{600 - 550}{5} = 10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_L = 80\ \text{wt\% B}$$
$$C_\beta = 92 + (600 - 550)\,\frac{8}{150} = 92 + 2.667 = 94.67\ \text{wt\% B}$$

Derive the lever rule and apply it

With W the mass fractions of the two phases, the total mass balance and the mass balance of B are

$$W_L + W_\beta = 1$$
$$W_L C_L + W_\beta C_\beta = C_0$$

Putting $W_L = 1 - W_\beta$ into the second equation and expanding:

$$(1 - W_\beta)\,C_L + W_\beta C_\beta = C_0$$
$$C_L + W_\beta\,(C_\beta - C_L) = C_0$$

so

$$W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_L}{C_\beta - C_L} \qquad W_L = 1 - W_\beta = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_L}$$

With a tie line of length $94.67 - 80 = 14.67$ wt% B [Session 7 Slide 16]:

$$W_\beta = \frac{85 - 80}{94.67 - 80} = \frac{5}{14.67} = 0.341$$
$$W_L = \frac{94.67 - 85}{94.67 - 80} = \frac{9.667}{14.67} = 0.659$$

Check of the B balance: $0.659 \times 80 + 0.341 \times 94.67 = 52.73 + 32.27 = 85.0$ wt% B.

Key results
  • Liquidus temperature 625 °C; first solid β of 96 wt% B.
  • At 600 °C: L of 80 wt% B and β of 94.7 wt% B; $W_L = 0.659$, $W_\beta = 0.341$.

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Microconstituents just below 550 °C

Phases just above 550 °C

The tie line now runs from the eutectic liquid ($C_E = 70$) to the β end of the eutectic line ($C_\beta = 92$), length 22 wt% B:

$$W_{\beta}(550 + \Delta T) = \frac{C_0 - C_E}{C_\beta - C_E} = \frac{85 - 70}{92 - 70} = \frac{15}{22} = 0.682$$
$$W_{L}(550 + \Delta T) = \frac{92 - 85}{22} = \frac{7}{22} = 0.318$$

Microconstituents just below 550 °C

The alloy is hypereutectic ($70 < 85 < 92$), so its primary phase is β [Session 7 Slide 31]. The β formed above 550 °C remains as primary β, and the liquid of 70 wt% B turns entirely into the eutectic microconstituent [Session 7 Slide 32]:

$$W_{\beta'} = 0.682 \qquad W_E = 0.318$$

Total phases just below 550 °C

Now α + β, on the tie line from 20 to 92 wt% B (length 72):

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{92 - 85}{92 - 20} = \frac{7}{72} = 0.0972$$
$$W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{85 - 20}{72} = \frac{65}{72} = 0.903$$

Phases inside the eutectic

The primary constituent is β, so every bit of α is in the eutectic: $W_{\alpha,E} = W_\alpha = 0.0972$. The β of the eutectic is the total β minus the primary β, with four figures:

$$W_{\beta,E} = W_\beta - W_{\beta'} = 0.9028 - 0.6818 = 0.221$$

Check with the make-up of the eutectic, which is $(92 - 70)/72 = 0.3056$ α: $0.3182 \times 0.3056 = 0.0972$ α, and $0.0972 + 0.221 = 0.318 = W_E$.

Key results
  • Primary β $W_{\beta'} = 0.682$; eutectic $W_E = 0.318$.
  • Total phases: $W_\alpha = 0.0972$, $W_\beta = 0.903$.
  • Inside the eutectic: α 0.0972 and β 0.221 of the alloy.

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Alloys with eutectic, and the 12 wt% B alloy

Range of alloys with eutectic

An alloy contains eutectic only if some of its liquid reaches the eutectic point at 550 °C, that is, if its vertical line crosses the eutectic line. The line runs from 20 to 92 wt% B, so the alloys with eutectic are those with $20 < C_0 < 92$ wt% B. An alloy of 12 wt% B solidifies completely as α before reaching 550 °C (it crosses the α solidus), so it has no eutectic.

Temperature at which β appears

Below its solidus the 12 wt% B alloy is single-phase α, until its vertical line meets the α solvus, the straight line from (20, 550) to (5, 20). Writing that line as temperature against composition and putting $C = 12$:

$$T = 20 + (C - 5)\,\frac{550 - 20}{20 - 5} = 20 + (12 - 5)\,\frac{530}{15} = 20 + 7 \times 35.33 = 267.3\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

Below 267.3 °C α cannot hold all its B, and β precipitates as fine particles inside the α grains and on their boundaries [Session 8 Slides 3-4].

Amount of β at 20 °C

At 20 °C the tie line runs from the α solvus (5 wt% B) to the β solvus (97 wt% B), length 92:

$$W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{12 - 5}{97 - 5} = \frac{7}{92} = 0.0761$$
$$W_\alpha = \frac{97 - 12}{92} = \frac{85}{92} = 0.924$$

All this β (7.61 % of the alloy) is precipitated β; none of it is eutectic.

Key results
  • Eutectic at room temperature for $20 < C_0 < 92$ wt% B.
  • The 12 wt% B alloy crosses the α solvus at 267.3 °C; at 20 °C it holds $W_\beta = 0.0761$ of precipitated β.

Interpretation and checks

The description fixes the diagram completely once straight lines are assumed: seven points, six boundaries and six regions. The 85 wt% B alloy starts to solidify at 625 °C with β of 96 wt% B, is two-thirds primary β at 550 °C, and the last 31.8 % of liquid becomes a eutectic that holds all the α of the alloy (9.72 %). The 12 wt% B alloy shows that a two-phase structure does not need a eutectic: it appears by precipitation when the solvus is crossed at 267.3 °C.

Control cases: for $C_0 \to 70$ the primary β $W_{\beta'} = (C_0 - 70)/22 \to 0$ and the alloy is all eutectic; for $C_0 \to 92$, $W_{\beta'} \to 1$. For $C_0 \to 5$ the solvus temperature tends to 20 °C, and for $C_0 \to 20$ to 550 °C. Part (a) · 0.75 points

Two candidate compositions and the choice

Derive the lever rule for the eutectic fraction

Just above $T_E$ the alloy holds a primary solid and liquid of composition $C_E$; that liquid becomes the eutectic, so $W_E = W_L(T_E + \Delta T)$. For a tie line from a solid of composition $C_s$ to the liquid $C_E$, the total mass balance and the Cu balance are

$$W_s + W_L = 1$$
$$W_s C_s + W_L C_E = C_0$$

Putting $W_s = 1 - W_L$ in the second equation and expanding:

$$(1 - W_L)\,C_s + W_L C_E = C_0$$
$$C_s + W_L\,(C_E - C_s) = C_0$$

hence

$$W_E = W_L = \frac{C_0 - C_s}{C_E - C_s}$$

Hypoeutectic candidate (solid α, $C_s = 8.8$)

$$0.55 = \frac{C_0 - 8.8}{28.1 - 8.8} = \frac{C_0 - 8.8}{19.3}$$
$$C_0 - 8.8 = 0.55 \times 19.3 = 10.615 \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_0 = 8.8 + 10.615 = 19.4\ \text{wt\% Cu}$$

Hypereutectic candidate (solid β, $C_s = 92$)

With $C_s = 92$ the same formula reads $W_E = (C_0 - 92)/(28.1 - 92) = (92 - C_0)/(92 - 28.1)$:

$$0.55 = \frac{92 - C_0}{92 - 28.1} = \frac{92 - C_0}{63.9}$$
$$92 - C_0 = 0.55 \times 63.9 = 35.145 \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_0 = 92 - 35.145 = 56.86\ \text{wt\% Cu}$$

Choose with the primary constituent

The eutectic fraction is the same function of $C_0$ on both sides of E, rising from 0 at an end of the eutectic line to 1 at E, so one value of $W_E$ always corresponds to two alloys. The primary constituent tells them apart: a hypoeutectic Ag-Cu alloy would show primary (Ag), a hypereutectic one primary (Cu) [Session 7 Slides 30-31]. The microanalysis finds (Cu) grains, so

$$C_0 = 56.86\ \text{wt\% Cu} \qquad (28.1 < 56.86 < 92\text{, hypereutectic})$$
Key results
  • Hypoeutectic candidate: $C_0 = 19.4$ wt% Cu (primary would be (Ag)).
  • Hypereutectic candidate: $C_0 = 56.86$ wt% Cu (primary would be (Cu)).
  • The primary grains are (Cu): the alloy is Ag-56.86 wt% Cu.

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Phases just below 779.1 °C and the make-up of the eutectic

Total phases with the lever rule

Below $T_E$ the liquid has gone and the alloy is α + β. With the lever rule of part (a) on the tie line from $C_\alpha = 8.8$ to $C_\beta = 92$ (length 83.2), carrying $C_0 = 56.855$:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{92 - 56.855}{92 - 8.8} = \frac{35.145}{83.2} = 0.422$$
$$W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{56.855 - 8.8}{83.2} = \frac{48.055}{83.2} = 0.578$$

Split the β between primary and eutectic

The primary β is 0.45 of the alloy (data). The rest of the β, and all of the α, are inside the eutectic:

$$W_{\beta,E} = W_\beta - W_{\beta'} = 0.5776 - 0.45 = 0.128$$
$$W_{\alpha,E} = W_\alpha = 0.422$$

Check: $W_{\alpha,E} + W_{\beta,E} = 0.422 + 0.128 = 0.550 = W_E$.

Make-up of the eutectic

The eutectic is the liquid of 28.1 wt% Cu split on the tie line 8.8–92; its α fraction is

$$\frac{C_\beta - C_E}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} = \frac{92 - 28.1}{83.2} = \frac{63.9}{83.2} = 0.768$$

so the eutectic is 76.8 % α and 23.2 % β whatever the alloy, and $0.55 \times 0.768 = 0.422$ reproduces $W_{\alpha,E}$. The lamellar matrix is mostly the Ag-rich phase.

Key results
  • α (8.8 wt% Cu) $W_\alpha = 0.422$; β (92 wt% Cu) $W_\beta = 0.578$.
  • Inside the eutectic: α 0.422 and β 0.128 of the alloy; the eutectic itself is 76.8 % α.

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Masses in the 3.0 kg casting

$$m_\alpha = 0.4224 \times 3.0\ \text{kg} = 1.267\ \text{kg} \qquad m_\beta = 0.5776 \times 3.0\ \text{kg} = 1.733\ \text{kg}$$

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Checking the certificate

Eutectic fraction of the certified alloy

Ag-50 wt% Cu is also hypereutectic ($28.1 < 50 < 92$), so its eutectic is

$$W_E(50) = \frac{92 - 50}{92 - 28.1} = \frac{42}{63.9} = 0.657$$

and its primary (Cu) is $1 - 0.657 = 0.343$.

Compare with the measurement

The micrograph shows 0.55 eutectic, not 0.657: about 11 points less eutectic and therefore more primary (Cu) than the certified alloy would have. Because $W_E$ falls as $C_0$ moves from E towards β, less eutectic means more Cu: the measurement corresponds to 56.86 wt% Cu, not 50. Within the hypotheses of the problem (equilibrium cooling, area fraction equal to mass fraction), the certificate is not consistent with the micrograph. The two alloys would also differ in their total phase fractions: α would be $42/83.2 = 0.505$ for the certified alloy, against 0.422 for the alloy of the micrograph.

Key results
  • Ag-50 wt% Cu would show $W_E = 0.657$ eutectic and 0.343 primary (Cu).
  • Measured 0.55: the certificate overstates the Ag content of the alloy (or understates its Cu); the micrograph points to 56.86 wt% Cu.

Interpretation and checks

A eutectic fraction alone never fixes the composition: it gives one hypoeutectic and one hypereutectic alloy, here 19.4 and 56.86 wt% Cu, and the nature of the primary grains decides between them. The alloy is hypereutectic: 45 % primary (Cu) dendrites in a matrix that, although it is 55 % of the alloy, is three-quarters Ag-rich α.

Control cases: with $W_E \to 1$ both candidates tend to $C_E = 28.1$ (fully eutectic), and with $W_E \to 0$ they tend to the ends of the eutectic line, 8.8 and 92 wt% Cu. All fractions lie between 0 and 1 and the masses add up to 3.0 kg. Dimensional check: the lever rule is a ratio of composition differences, (wt% Cu)/(wt% Cu), so it is dimensionless; a mass is a dimensionless fraction times kg, so it is in kg; and a mass of Cu needs the composition as a fraction (0.281), not as 28.1.

Common exam mistake (the trap of this problem): solving only on the side of the eutectic point one is used to (the hypoeutectic one) and answering 19.4 wt% Cu, an alloy that would show primary (Ag), not the (Cu) grains of the micrograph.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Phases, compositions and amounts just above the eutectoid temperature

Locate the alloy on the diagram and read the ends of the tie line

On cooling, the 0.45 wt% C austenite crosses the A3 line and ferrite starts to form at the austenite grain boundaries (Session 9, Slide 15). Just above 727 °C the point lies in the two-phase field α + γ. The horizontal tie line at 727 + ΔT °C ends on the ferrite boundary at $C_\alpha = 0.022$ wt% C and on the A3 line at the eutectoid point, $C_\gamma = C_E = 0.76$ wt% C (Session 9, Slides 4 and 17).

Derive the lever rule from the two mass balances

The phases are the only two places where the mass of the alloy can be, so the mass fractions add up to one (mass balance of the alloy):

$$W_\alpha + W_\gamma = 1$$

The carbon of the alloy is shared between the two phases (mass balance of carbon):

$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + W_\gamma C_\gamma = C_0$$

Substitute $W_\gamma = 1 - W_\alpha$ from the first balance into the second:

$$W_\alpha C_\alpha + (1 - W_\alpha) C_\gamma = C_0$$

Expand and group the terms in $W_\alpha$:

$$W_\alpha (C_\alpha - C_\gamma) = C_0 - C_\gamma$$

Divide by $(C_\alpha - C_\gamma)$ and change the sign of numerator and denominator:

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_\gamma - C_0}{C_\gamma - C_\alpha}, \qquad W_\gamma = 1 - W_\alpha = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\gamma - C_\alpha}$$

This is the lever rule of Session 7, Slides 15-21: each phase is weighted by the segment of the tie line on the opposite side of $C_0$.

Substitute the compositions

Tie line length: $C_\gamma - C_\alpha = 0.76 - 0.022 = 0.738$ wt% C.

$$W_\alpha = \frac{0.76 - 0.45}{0.76 - 0.022} = \frac{0.31}{0.738} = 0.4201$$
$$W_\gamma = \frac{0.45 - 0.022}{0.76 - 0.022} = \frac{0.428}{0.738} = 0.5799$$

Result: 42.0 % ferrite with 0.022 wt% C and 58.0 % austenite with 0.76 wt% C.

Checks: $0.4201 + 0.5799 = 1$. Carbon balance: $0.4201 \times 0.022 + 0.5799 \times 0.76 = 0.0092 + 0.4407 = 0.450$ wt% C $= C_0$.

Key results
  • Phases: ferrite α with 0.022 wt% C and austenite γ with 0.76 wt% C
  • $W_\alpha = 0.4201$ (42.0 %), $W_\gamma = 0.5799$ (58.0 %)

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Phases and microconstituents just below the eutectoid temperature

Phases: lever rule on the tie line from ferrite to cementite

Below 727 °C the point lies in the α + Fe₃C field (Session 9, Slide 9). The tie line at 727 − ΔT runs from $C_\alpha = 0.022$ to $C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 6.67$ wt% C, length $6.67 - 0.022 = 6.648$. With the lever rule of part (a), written for the pair α, Fe₃C (Session 9, Slide 18):

$$W_\alpha = \frac{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_0}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\alpha} = \frac{6.67 - 0.45}{6.67 - 0.022} = \frac{6.22}{6.648} = 0.9356$$
$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\alpha} = \frac{0.45 - 0.022}{6.648} = \frac{0.428}{6.648} = 0.0644$$

Microconstituents: the austenite of 727 + ΔT becomes pearlite

The proeutectoid (primary) ferrite formed above 727 °C does not change when the steel crosses the eutectoid line. The austenite present at 727 + ΔT has exactly the eutectoid composition, 0.76 wt% C, so all of it undergoes the eutectoid reaction γ (0.76) → α (0.022) + Fe₃C (6.67) and becomes pearlite (Session 9, Slides 10 and 18). The fractions are therefore those of part (a), which is the lever rule on the short tie line from $C_\alpha$ to $C_E$:

$$W_{\alpha_p} = \frac{C_E - C_0}{C_E - C_\alpha} = \frac{0.76 - 0.45}{0.738} = 0.4201, \qquad W_P = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_E - C_\alpha} = \frac{0.428}{0.738} = 0.5799$$

Result: two phases, ferrite (93.6 %, 0.022 wt% C) and cementite (6.4 %, 6.67 wt% C); two microconstituents, proeutectoid ferrite (42.0 %) and pearlite (58.0 %, overall 0.76 wt% C).

Checks: each pair adds up to 1. The ferrite phase (93.6 %) is larger than the proeutectoid ferrite (42.0 %) because the pearlite also contains ferrite; part (c) separates the two.

Key results
  • Phases: α (0.022 wt% C) $W_\alpha = 0.9356$; Fe₃C (6.67 wt% C) $W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 0.0644$
  • Microconstituents: proeutectoid ferrite $W_{\alpha_p} = 0.4201$; pearlite $W_P = 0.5799$ (0.76 wt% C)

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Mass of proeutectoid ferrite, eutectoid ferrite and cementite in the 8.0 kg bar

Split the ferrite phase into proeutectoid and eutectoid ferrite

The ferrite phase of part (b) is the sum of the proeutectoid ferrite and the ferrite of the pearlite lamellae (Session 9, Slide 18):

$$W_{\alpha} = W_{\alpha_p} + W_{\alpha_E}$$

Isolate the eutectoid ferrite:

$$W_{\alpha_E} = W_\alpha - W_{\alpha_p} = \frac{6.22}{6.648} - \frac{0.31}{0.738} = 0.9356 - 0.4201 = 0.5156$$

Convert mass fractions into masses

A mass fraction is the mass of the constituent divided by the mass of the bar, $W_i = m_i / m$, so $m_i = W_i\, m$ with $m = 8.0$ kg:

$$m_{\alpha_p} = 0.4201 \times 8.0\ \text{kg} = 3.360\ \text{kg}$$
$$m_{\alpha_E} = 0.5156 \times 8.0\ \text{kg} = 4.125\ \text{kg}$$
$$m_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 0.0644 \times 8.0\ \text{kg} = 0.515\ \text{kg}$$

(The products are computed with the unrounded fractions, 0.420054, 0.515566 and 0.064380.)

Check with the make-up of pearlite

Every pearlite has the make-up of the eutectoid steel, $W_\alpha = (6.67 - 0.76)/(6.67 - 0.022) = 5.91/6.648 = 0.8890$ ferrite and 0.1110 cementite (Session 9, Slide 13). The pearlite of the bar weighs $0.5799 \times 8.0 = 4.640$ kg, so it holds $0.8890 \times 4.640 = 4.125$ kg of ferrite and $0.1110 \times 4.640 = 0.515$ kg of cementite, the values above. Mass check: $3.360 + 4.125 + 0.515 = 8.000$ kg.

Key results
  • Proeutectoid ferrite: 3.360 kg
  • Eutectoid ferrite (inside the pearlite): 4.125 kg
  • Cementite: 0.515 kg (all of it inside the pearlite)

Faster cooling: displacement of the eutectoid point

Session 9, Slide 21: if the cooling rate increases, the eutectoid point moves towards lower temperatures and lower carbon contents. Interpretation and checks

The steel spends its last moments above 727 °C as a 42/58 mixture of ferrite and eutectoid austenite; the eutectoid reaction turns all of that austenite into pearlite without touching the ferrite, which is why the microconstituent fractions below 727 °C equal the phase fractions above it. Most of the bar is ferrite (93.6 % by mass), and the 6.4 % of hard cementite is entirely inside the pearlite.

Limiting cases: with $C_0 \to C_\alpha = 0.022$ the lever rule gives $W_{\alpha_p} \to 1$ (all ferrite); with $C_0 \to C_E = 0.76$ it gives $W_{\alpha_p} \to 0$ (all pearlite, the eutectoid steel of Session 9, Slide 13).

Dimensional check: the lever rule is a ratio of two compositions, (wt%)/(wt%), so it is dimensionless; a mass is a dimensionless fraction times kg, so it is in kg.

Common exam mistake: giving the 0.9356 of the long tie line as the "proeutectoid ferrite". That is the whole ferrite phase; the proeutectoid ferrite comes from the short tie line ending at 0.76 wt% C.

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Start of the transformation and phases at 780 °C

Read point b on the Acm line

From a (950 °C) down to b the steel is single-phase austenite with 1.10 wt% C ($W_\gamma = 1$). Point b lies on the straight Acm segment. Between its printed ends, the temperature grows linearly with the composition: the slope is $(1147 - 727)/(2.14 - 0.76) = 420/1.38 = 304.3$ °C per wt% C. At $C_0 = 1.10$:

$$T_b = 727 + (1.10 - 0.76) \times \frac{420}{1.38} = 727 + 0.34 \times 304.3 = 727 + 103.5 = 830.5\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

On the dashed 50 °C grid of the figure, b lies three fifths of the way from the 800 °C line to the 850 °C line: about 830 °C, in agreement.

Constituent that forms at b

Below Acm the solubility of carbon in austenite is lower than 1.10 wt% C, so the excess carbon leaves the austenite as proeutectoid cementite, Fe₃C, which forms along the boundaries of the austenite grains (Session 9, Slide 19). The austenite that remains becomes poorer in carbon, following the Acm line down towards 0.76 wt% C.

Composition of the austenite at 780 °C

At 780 °C the tie line starts on Acm. Invert the straight line $T = 727 + (C - 0.76)\times 420/1.38$ for $C$: subtract 727, multiply by $1.38/420$, add 0.76:

$$C_\gamma(780) = 0.76 + (780 - 727) \times \frac{1.38}{420} = 0.76 + 53 \times 0.003286 = 0.76 + 0.1741 = 0.934\ \text{wt\% C}$$

The other end of the tie line is cementite, $C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 6.67$ wt% C (a vertical line on the diagram, Session 9, Slide 4).

Lever rule, derived from the mass balances

Mass of the alloy: $W_\gamma + W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 1$. Carbon: $W_\gamma C_\gamma + W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = C_0$. Put $W_\gamma = 1 - W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}$ into the carbon balance:

$$C_\gamma + W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}(C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\gamma) = C_0$$

Subtract $C_\gamma$ and divide by $(C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\gamma)$:

$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = \frac{C_0 - C_\gamma}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\gamma}, \qquad W_\gamma = \frac{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_0}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\gamma}$$

Substitute at 780 °C

Tie line length: $6.67 - 0.934 = 5.736$ wt% C.

$$W_\gamma = \frac{6.67 - 1.10}{6.67 - 0.934} = \frac{5.57}{5.736} = 0.9711, \qquad W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = \frac{1.10 - 0.934}{5.736} = \frac{0.166}{5.736} = 0.0289$$

Result: at 780 °C, 97.1 % austenite with 0.934 wt% C and 2.9 % cementite with 6.67 wt% C. Check: $0.9711 \times 0.934 + 0.0289 \times 6.67 = 0.907 + 0.193 = 1.10$ wt% C.

Eutectoid reaction, proeutectoid cementite and pearlite in the forging

The eutectoid reaction

Tie line just above 727 °C

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Cementite inside the pearlite, and where the proeutectoid cementite sits

First way: subtract the proeutectoid cementite from the total

$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C},E} = W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C},p} = \frac{1.078}{6.648} - \frac{0.34}{5.91} = 0.16215 - 0.05753 = 0.1046$$
$$m_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C},E} = 0.10462 \times 2.5\ \text{kg} = 0.2616\ \text{kg}$$

Second way: the make-up of pearlite

Every pearlite has the composition of the eutectoid, 0.76 wt% C, so its cementite fraction is the lever rule of the eutectoid steel (Session 9, Slide 13):

$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}^{(P)} = \frac{0.76 - 0.022}{6.67 - 0.022} = \frac{0.738}{6.648} = 0.1110$$

Where the proeutectoid cementite sits

The proeutectoid cementite forms between b and 727 °C at the boundaries of the austenite grains, so after cooling it is a thin, continuous network around the pearlite colonies (Session 9, Slide 19).   • Eutectoid cementite: $W = 0.1046$ → 0.2616 kg (both ways)

Part (a) · 0.75 points

Lamellar constituent and the two candidate carbon contents

The lamellar constituent

Alternating lamellae of ferrite and cementite are pearlite, the product of the eutectoid reaction γ (0.76) → α (0.022) + Fe₃C (6.67) at 727 °C (Session 9, Slides 10 and 12). Pearlite always has the eutectoid composition, 0.76 wt% C, but it appears in both hypo- and hypereutectoid steels, so 88.0 % of pearlite alone does not tell on which side of 0.76 the steel lies.

Lever rule for the pearlite, derived once

Just above 727 °C the pearlite is still austenite of $C_E = 0.76$ wt% C, in equilibrium with a proeutectoid phase of composition $C_p$ ($C_\alpha$ or $C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}$). Mass balance: $W_P + W_p = 1$. Carbon balance: $W_P C_E + W_p C_p = C_0$. Substitute $W_p = 1 - W_P$:

$$W_P C_E + (1 - W_P) C_p = C_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad W_P (C_E - C_p) = C_0 - C_p \quad\Rightarrow\quad W_P = \frac{C_0 - C_p}{C_E - C_p}$$

Hypoeutectoid candidate ($C_p = C_\alpha$)

$$W_P = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_E - C_\alpha}$$

Multiply both sides by $(C_E - C_\alpha)$ and add $C_\alpha$:

$$C_0 = C_\alpha + W_P (C_E - C_\alpha) = 0.022 + 0.880 \times (0.76 - 0.022) = 0.022 + 0.880 \times 0.738 = 0.022 + 0.6494 = 0.671\ \text{wt\% C}$$

Hypereutectoid candidate ($C_p = C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}$)

$$W_P = \frac{C_0 - C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}}{C_E - C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}} = \frac{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_0}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_E}$$

Multiply by $(C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_E)$ and isolate $C_0$:

$$C_0 = C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - W_P (C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_E) = 6.67 - 0.880 \times 5.91 = 6.67 - 5.2008 = 1.469\ \text{wt\% C}$$

Result: both 0.671 wt% C (hypoeutectoid, $0.671 < 0.76$) and 1.469 wt% C (hypereutectoid, $0.76 < 1.469 < 2.14$) are steels with 88.0 % pearlite. Check: both lie inside their ranges, so both are physically possible.

Key results
  • Lamellar colonies: pearlite (α + Fe₃C lamellae, 0.76 wt% C overall)
  • Hypoeutectoid candidate: $C_0 = 0.671$ wt% C
  • Hypereutectoid candidate: $C_0 = 1.469$ wt% C

Part (b) · 0.75 points

Choice of the right carbon content and full austenitisation

What X is

In a hypoeutectoid steel the proeutectoid constituent is ferrite, which forms equiaxed grains of its own between the pearlite colonies (Session 9, Slide 14). In a hypereutectoid steel it is cementite, which forms a thin network along the boundaries of the former austenite grains around the pearlite (Session 9, Slide 19). The grain-boundary position alone does not decide, because proeutectoid ferrite also nucleates on the austenite grain boundaries (Session 9, Slide 15; Topic 4, section 14.2), and a large amount of it can outline them too. The hardness does decide: ferrite is a soft phase (Session 9, Slide 5) and would be softer than the pearlite, which contains 11 % of cementite, whereas cementite is hard and brittle (Session 9, Slide 8) and is harder than the pearlite. X is much harder than the colonies, so X is proeutectoid cementite, and its thin continuous network is the shape Slide 19 shows for it. The steel is hypereutectoid and

$$C_0 = 1.469\ \text{wt\% C}$$

The other candidate, 0.671 wt% C, would show 12.0 % of ferrite grains, not a grain-boundary network, and is rejected.

Lowest temperature of the single-phase austenite field

A hypereutectoid steel is fully austenitic above the Acm line (Session 9, Slides 4 and 11). On the straight Acm segment the temperature grows by $(1147 - 727)/(2.14 - 0.76) = 420/1.38 = 304.3$ °C per wt% C above the eutectoid point:

$$T_{Acm} = 727 + (1.469 - 0.76) \times 304.3 = 727 + 0.709 \times 304.3 = 727 + 215.8 = 942.8\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

Result: the bar must have been above about 943 °C to be entirely austenite. (Had the steel been the 0.671 wt% C candidate, it would have been fully austenitic above the A3 line, which at 0.671 wt% C lies only some 5 to 20 °C above 727 °C, depending on how its curve is read: the slides do not give A3 in numbers.)

Key results
  • X is much harder than the pearlite, so it is proeutectoid cementite (not ferrite): the steel is hypereutectoid, $C_0 = 1.469$ wt% C
  • Fully austenitic above about 943 °C (Acm line)

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Phases below 727 °C and where the cementite is

Phases: lever rule on the tie line from 0.022 to 6.67 wt% C

Length $6.67 - 0.022 = 6.648$ (Session 9, Slide 9 for the α + Fe₃C field):

$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\alpha} = \frac{1.469 - 0.022}{6.648} = \frac{1.447}{6.648} = 0.2177, \qquad W_\alpha = 1 - 0.2177 = 0.7823$$

Cementite inside the pearlite

The pearlite is the eutectoid steel, whose cementite fraction is (Session 9, Slide 13)

$$W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}^{(P)} = \frac{C_E - C_\alpha}{C_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} - C_\alpha} = \frac{0.738}{6.648} = 0.1110$$

so the cementite of the colonies, per unit mass of steel, is $W_P \times 0.1110 = 0.880 \times 0.1110 = 0.0977$. The rest of the cementite is X, the proeutectoid cementite, $W_X = 0.120$.

Check: $0.120 + 0.0977 = 0.2177 = W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}}$, the value of the long tie line: the two independent routes agree. The ferrite is all inside the pearlite: $0.880 \times (1 - 0.1110) = 0.7823 = W_\alpha$.

Key results
  • $W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} = 0.2177$, $W_\alpha = 0.7823$
  • Cementite in X (proeutectoid): 0.120; cementite in the pearlite: 0.0977

Part (d) · 0.75 points

Since $727 < 800 < 942.8$ °C, the point (1.469 wt% C, 800 °C) lies in the γ + Fe₃C field (Session 9, Slide 9). The tie line ends on Acm and on the cementite line. Inverting the straight Acm, $C = 0.76 + (T - 727)\times 1.38/420$:

Interpretation and checks

A pearlite fraction measured on a micrograph fixes the distance of the steel from the eutectoid, not the side: the same 88.0 % lies 0.089 wt% C below 0.76 (on the short ferrite side of the diagram) and 0.709 wt% C above it (on the long cementite side). The shape of the proeutectoid constituent, grains of ferrite or a network of cementite, is the second datum that removes the ambiguity, exactly as Slides 14 and 19 of Session 9 show it.

Limiting cases: $W_P \to 1$ makes both candidates collapse to $C_E = 0.76$ wt% C (the eutectoid steel); $W_P \to 0$ gives $C_\alpha = 0.022$ on one side and 6.67 wt% C on the other, which is outside the steels: below 2.14 wt% C the pearlite of a hypereutectoid steel never falls below $(6.67 - 2.14)/5.91 = 0.7665$.

Dimensional check: $C_0 = C_\alpha + W_P (C_E - C_\alpha)$ is wt% + (dimensionless)(wt%) = wt%; $T_{Acm}$ is °C + (wt%)(°C/wt%) = °C.

Derive the lever rule once

At 727 + ΔT the steel is a proeutectoid phase of composition $C_p$ plus austenite of $C_E = 0.76$. Mass balance: $W_p + W_\gamma = 1$. Carbon balance: $W_p C_p + W_\gamma C_E = C_0$. Substitute $W_\gamma = 1 - W_p$ into the carbon balance:

$$W_p (C_p - C_E) = C_0 - C_E \quad\Rightarrow\quad W_p = \frac{C_0 - C_E}{C_p - C_E} = \frac{C_E - C_0}{C_E - C_p}$$

Ranking by hardness

Part (c) · 0.75 points

Lowest carbon content for at least 50 % pearlite

Set up the inequality

For a hypoeutectoid steel the pearlite fraction is the lever rule of part (a):

$$W_P = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_E - C_\alpha} \geq 0.50$$

Multiply both sides by the positive length $(C_E - C_\alpha) = 0.738$ (the sense of the inequality is kept) and add $C_\alpha$:

$$C_0 \geq C_\alpha + 0.50\,(C_E - C_\alpha) = 0.022 + 0.50 \times 0.738 = 0.022 + 0.369 = 0.391\ \text{wt\% C}$$

Result: the hypoeutectoid steel needs at least 0.391 wt% C. S1 (0.25, 30.9 % pearlite) fails; S2 (0.60, 78.3 %) passes.

Hypereutectoid steels

For $C_0 > 0.76$, $W_P = (6.67 - C_0)/5.91$ decreases as $C_0$ grows, so its smallest value in the steel range is at $C_0 = 2.14$ wt% C (Session 9, Slide 4):

$$W_P(2.14) = \frac{6.67 - 2.14}{5.91} = \frac{4.53}{5.91} = 0.7665$$

That is above 0.50, so every hypereutectoid steel has more than 50 % pearlite.

Key results
  • Hypoeutectoid: $C_0 \geq 0.391$ wt% C (S1 fails, S2 meets it)
  • Every hypereutectoid steel meets it: the least pearlite, at 2.14 wt% C, is 0.7665

Direction of the shift for each element

Consequence for the microconstituents of S2

In both versions the eutectoid composition $C_E'$ is lower than 0.76, so the pearlite (which always has the eutectoid composition) carries less than 0.76 wt% C. The proeutectoid ferrite is $W_{\alpha_p} = (C_E' - C_0)/(C_E' - C_\alpha)$: a smaller $C_E'$ shrinks the numerator, so S2 has less proeutectoid ferrite and more pearlite than the plain 0.60 wt% C steel of part (a). Its total cementite fraction (the long tie line from ferrite to cementite) does not change when the eutectoid point moves; what changes is how the cementite is distributed: more of the soft proeutectoid ferrite is replaced by pearlite, which is harder than ferrite (it contains the hard cementite lamellae, Session 9, Slides 8 and 12).

If the eutectoid composition fell below 0.60 wt% C

Then $C_0 = 0.60 > C_E'$ and S2 would sit on the cementite side of the new eutectoid: on slow cooling it would form proeutectoid cementite at the austenite grain boundaries, followed by pearlite, like a hypereutectoid plain steel (Session 9, Slide 19).

Key results
  • Cr (favours α): eutectoid at higher temperature and lower %C
  • Mn (favours γ): eutectoid at lower temperature and lower %C
  • In both cases S2 is closer to the eutectoid: less proeutectoid ferrite, more pearlite, and the pearlite has less than 0.76 wt% C
  • If the eutectoid composition fell below 0.60 wt% C, S2 would behave as hypereutectoid: proeutectoid cementite + pearlite

Interpretation and checks

The three steels show the whole story of the Fe-Fe₃C steel corner: the pearlite fraction peaks at the eutectoid and falls on both sides, but the cementite fraction, which sets the hardness, grows steadily with the carbon content. S1 is a soft, mostly ferritic steel; S2 is mostly pearlite; S3 is the hardest and the least tough, because of its 10 % grain-boundary cementite network. Alloying moves the eutectoid point, so "hypo" and "hyper" are relative to the eutectoid of the actual alloy, not always to 0.76 wt% C.

Limiting cases: $C_0 \to 0.022$ gives $W_{\alpha_p} \to 1$ and $W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} \to 0$; $C_0 \to 0.76$ gives $W_P \to 1$ from both sides; $C_0 \to 6.67$ would give $W_{\text{Fe}_3\text{C}} \to 1$ (outside the steels).

Dimensional check: every lever rule is (wt%)/(wt%), dimensionless; the bound of part (c) is wt% + (dimensionless)(wt%) = wt%.

Part (b) · 1 point

Calculate the true stress and the true strain at fracture, and explain why $\sigma_T = \sigma(1+\varepsilon)$ cannot be used here

True stress at fracture with the measured area

The true stress is the load over the area that actually carries it at that instant [Session 11 Slide 37]. At fracture that area is the neck, $A_f$:

$$\sigma_{T,f} = \frac{F_f}{A_f} = \frac{17\,800\ \text{N}}{29.22\ \text{mm}^2} = 609.1\ \text{MPa}$$

Equivalently $\sigma_{T,f} = \sigma_f\,A_0/A_f = 354.1\ \text{MPa}\times1.720 = 609.1$ MPa [Session 11 Slide 38].

True strain at fracture: derivation from constant volume in the neck

The true strain adds each small elongation $dl$ divided by the current length $l$ [Session 11 Slide 37]. An antiderivative of $1/l$ is $\ln l$, and Barrow's rule with the limits substituted gives

$$\varepsilon_T = \int_{l_0}^{l}\frac{dl}{l} = \left[\ln l\right]_{l_0}^{l} = \ln l - \ln l_0 = \ln\frac{l}{l_0}$$

After necking the length between the marks no longer describes the strain of the neck, so the integral is applied to a thin slice of the neck itself. Plastic deformation keeps the volume of that slice constant: its initial length $l_0$ and area $A_0$ become $l$ and $A$ with $A_0 l_0 = A l$. Dividing both sides by $A\,l_0$:

$$\frac{l}{l_0} = \frac{A_0}{A} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \varepsilon_T = \ln\frac{A_0}{A}$$

which is the expression of the slide for the necked region [Session 11 Slide 38]. At the fracture surface $A = A_f$. With $A = \pi D^2/4$ the factor $\pi/4$ cancels in the ratio, and the logarithm of a square is twice the logarithm:

$$\varepsilon_{T,f} = \ln\frac{A_0}{A_f} = \ln\frac{d_0^2}{d_f^2} = 2\ln\frac{d_0}{d_f} = 2\ln\frac{8.00}{6.10} = 2\times0.2712 = 0.542$$

Why the uniform-deformation formulas fail here

The formulas $\sigma_T = \sigma(1+\varepsilon)$ and $\varepsilon_T = \ln(1+\varepsilon)$ of Slide 38 come from constant volume, $A_0 l_0 = A l$, applied to a gauge length that deforms uniformly, so that one strain $\varepsilon$ describes every cross-section: from $A_0 l_0 = A l$, $A = A_0 l_0/l = A_0/(1+\varepsilon)$ and $\sigma_T = F/A = (F/A_0)(1+\varepsilon) = \sigma(1+\varepsilon)$. After the maximum load the strain concentrates in the neck: the average strain of the gauge length (0.14) is much smaller than the local strain at the fracture surface. Using them anyway gives

$$\sigma_f\,(1 + 0.140) = 354.1\ \text{MPa}\times1.140 = 403.7\ \text{MPa} \qquad \ln(1 + 0.140) = 0.131$$

against 609.1 MPa and 0.542 from the measured neck: the area ratio of the neck is $A_0/A_f = 1.720$, not $1 + \varepsilon = 1.140$. That is why the slide switches to $F_i/A_i$ and $\ln(A_0/A)$ after necking [Session 11 Slide 38].

Key results
  • $\sigma_{T,f} = 609.1$ MPa
  • $\varepsilon_{T,f} = 0.542$
  • $\sigma_f(1 + EL/100) = 403.7$ MPa underestimates it by about a third

Interpretation and checks

  • Physical meaning. The engineering curve falls after the maximum (427.7 to 354.1 MPa) only because it divides by the original area; the metal in the neck is still hardening, and at fracture it carries 609.1 MPa. The slide states $\sigma_{\text{real}} > \sigma_{\text{eng}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\text{real}} < \varepsilon_{\text{eng}}$ [Session 11 Slide 37]. The stress inequality holds here (609.1 > 354.1 MPa). The strain inequality is written for the uniform deformation of the gauge length, where $\ln(1+\varepsilon) < \varepsilon$ (0.131 < 0.140); the 0.542 found above is not a counterexample, because it is the local strain of the neck, while 0.14 is the average over the whole gauge length.
  • Control checks. $\sigma_f < \sigma_{TS} < \sigma_{T,f}$; $0 < RA < 100$ %; if the specimen had broken without necking ($d_f \to d_0$), $RA \to 0$ and $\sigma_{T,f} \to \sigma_f$, the behaviour of a brittle material.
  • Dimensions. $F/A$ is N/m² = Pa; %EL and %RA are ratios of lengths and of areas, and $\ln(A_0/A_f)$ is the logarithm of a pure number, so both strains are dimensionless.
  • Common exam mistake. Dividing the fracture load by the original area and calling it the true fracture stress, or computing %RA with the diameters instead of their squares ($1 - 6.10/8.00 = 23.8$ %, wrong).

Read the modulus of elasticity and the yield strength, and derive and calculate the modulus of resilience

Modulus of elasticity from point A

The straight part passes through the origin and through A, and Hooke's law says its slope is $E$ [Session 11 Slide 12]:

$$E = \frac{\sigma_A - 0}{\varepsilon_A - 0} = \frac{310\ \text{MPa}}{0.0015} = 206\,667\ \text{MPa} = 206.7\ \text{GPa}$$

This agrees with the steel value of the slide's table, 207 GPa [Session 11 Slide 17].

Yield strength from point Y

The yield strength is the stress at the crossing of the curve with the offset line [Session 11 Slide 28]; the horizontal projection of Y ends at 450 on the stress axis:

$$\sigma_y = 450\ \text{MPa}$$

Check of the reading: on the offset line $\sigma = E(\varepsilon - 0.002)$, so the strain of Y must be $\varepsilon_Y = 0.002 + \sigma_y/E = 0.002 + 450/206\,667 = 0.004177$, which is where Y sits in the right panel (just to the right of the 0.004 gridline).

Elastic strain at the yield strength

Treating the elastic region as linear up to $\sigma_y$ (Slide 35) and isolating the strain from Hooke's law $\sigma_y = E\,\varepsilon_y$:

$$\varepsilon_y = \frac{\sigma_y}{E} = \frac{450\ \text{MPa}}{206\,667\ \text{MPa}} = 0.002177$$

Modulus of resilience as the area under the elastic line

The resilience is the energy absorbed per unit volume while the material deforms elastically, the area under the curve up to the yield point [Session 11 Slide 35]. In the elastic region $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, so

$$U_r = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y}\sigma\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y}E\,\varepsilon\,d\varepsilon$$

$E$ is a constant and leaves the integral; an antiderivative of $\varepsilon$ is $\varepsilon^2/2$ (its derivative is $2\varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$). Barrow's rule with the limits substituted:

$$U_r = E\left[\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\right]_0^{\varepsilon_y} = E\left(\frac{\varepsilon_y^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2}\right) = \frac{E\,\varepsilon_y^2}{2}$$

Replacing $\varepsilon_y = \sigma_y/E$: $U_r = \dfrac{E}{2}\,\dfrac{\sigma_y^2}{E^2} = \dfrac{\sigma_y^2}{2E}$, the formula of the slide, which is also the area of the triangle $\tfrac12\sigma_y\varepsilon_y$.

$$U_r = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E} = \frac{(450\ \text{MPa})^2}{2\times206\,667\ \text{MPa}} = \frac{202\,500}{413\,333}\ \text{MPa} = 0.490\ \text{MPa}$$

  • $\sigma_y = 450$ MPa, $\varepsilon_y = \sigma_y/E = 0.002177$
  • $U_r = 0.490$ MPa $= 0.490$ MJ/m³

Calculate the true stress and the true strain at point B, deriving the formulas you use, and say why they cannot be used after point U

True strain as a sum of small increments

The true strain adds each small elongation $dl$ divided by the length $l$ the specimen has at that moment [Session 11 Slide 37]:

$$\varepsilon_T = \int_{l_0}^{l}\frac{dl}{l}$$

An antiderivative of $1/l$ is $\ln l$. Barrow's rule with the limits substituted:

$$\varepsilon_T = \left[\ln l\right]_{l_0}^{l} = \ln l - \ln l_0 = \ln\frac{l}{l_0}$$

Since $\varepsilon = (l - l_0)/l_0$, the ratio is $l/l_0 = (l_0 + l - l_0)/l_0 = 1 + \varepsilon$, and

$$\varepsilon_T = \ln(1 + \varepsilon)$$

True stress from constant volume

Plastic deformation does not change the volume, and before necking the specimen deforms uniformly, so the volume of the gauge length is the same at every instant [Session 11 Slide 38]:

$$A_0\,l_0 = A_i\,l_i$$

Isolating the instantaneous area: $A_i = A_0\,l_0/l_i$. The true stress is the load over that area [Session 11 Slide 37]:

$$\sigma_T = \frac{F}{A_i} = \frac{F}{A_0\,l_0/l_i} = \frac{F}{A_0}\cdot\frac{l_i}{l_0} = \sigma\,(1 + \varepsilon)$$

using again $l_i/l_0 = 1 + \varepsilon$.

Values at point B

Point B is read at $\varepsilon_B = 0.08$, $\sigma_B = 560$ MPa:

$$\varepsilon_{T,B} = \ln(1 + 0.08) = \ln 1.08 = 0.0770$$
$$\sigma_{T,B} = \sigma_B\,(1 + \varepsilon_B) = 560\ \text{MPa}\times1.08 = 604.8\ \text{MPa}$$

As Slide 37 predicts, $\sigma_T > \sigma$ (604.8 > 560 MPa) and $\varepsilon_T < \varepsilon$ (0.0770 < 0.08). The cross-section at B has shrunk by the factor $A_i/A_0 = 1/1.08 = 0.926$.

Why not after point U

Point U is the maximum of the curve, the tensile strength (600 MPa), and necking follows it [Session 11 Slide 30]. From U on the deformation concentrates in the neck: the gauge length no longer has one area, $A_0 l_0 = A_i l_i$ with a single $A_i$ fails, and $\sigma(1+\varepsilon)$ and $\ln(1+\varepsilon)$ no longer describe the neck. After necking the slide uses the measured area, $\sigma_T = F_i/A_i$ and $\varepsilon_T = \ln(A_0/A) = 2\ln(D_0/D)$ [Session 11 Slide 38], so the diameter of the neck would have to be measured (for example at F).

  • $\varepsilon_{T,B} = \ln(1.08) = 0.0770$

  • Limiting case. $$d_f = d_0\,\sqrt{1 - RA} = 9.00\ \text{mm}\times\sqrt{0.870} = 9.00\ \text{mm}\times0.9327 = 8.39\ \text{mm}$$

Diameter at the maximum load from constant volume

Up to the maximum load the deformation is uniform and the volume of the gauge length does not change [Session 11 Slide 38]: $A_0 l_0 = A_u l_u$. Isolating the area and writing $l_u/l_0 = 1 + \varepsilon_u$:

$$A_u = \frac{A_0\,l_0}{l_u} = \frac{A_0}{1 + \varepsilon_u} = \frac{63.62\ \text{mm}^2}{1.080} = 58.90\ \text{mm}^2$$

and, with $A \propto d^2$, $d_u = d_0/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon_u} = 9.00\ \text{mm}/\sqrt{1.080} = 8.66$ mm.

True stress at the maximum load

The true stress is the load over the instantaneous area [Session 11 Slide 37]:

$$\sigma_{T,u} = \frac{F_{\max}}{A_u} = \frac{16\,222\ \text{N}}{58.90\ \text{mm}^2} = 275.4\ \text{MPa}$$

the same as $\sigma_{TS}(1 + \varepsilon_u) = 255\ \text{MPa}\times1.080 = 275.4$ MPa, as it must be, since both come from constant volume.

Key results
  • $l_f = 49.95$ mm, $A_f = 55.35$ mm², $d_f = 8.39$ mm
  • At the maximum load: $d = 8.66$ mm, $\sigma_T = 275.4$ MPa

Interpretation and checks

  • Consistency of the report. The neck diameter after fracture (8.39 mm) is smaller than the uniform diameter at the maximum load (8.66 mm): the neck kept thinning after the maximum, as it must [Session 11 Slide 30]. And $l_f = 49.95$ mm is longer than the length at the maximum, $45.00\times1.080 = 48.60$ mm, because the neck added plastic elongation even though the elastic part was recovered.
  • Order of magnitude. $E = 45$ GPa is the magnesium value of the slide's table [Session 11 Slide 17]; magnesium is hcp, with 6 slip systems against 12 for fcc and bcc [Session 11 Slide 25]. Extension (not on the slides): fewer slip systems usually mean less ductility, which fits the small %RA of 13 %.
  • Limiting case. If $RA \to 0$ (no neck), $d_f \to d_0$; if $\varepsilon_u \to 0$, $d_u \to d_0$ and $\sigma_T \to \sigma$.
  • Dimensions. $F = \sigma A_0$ is Pa·m² = N; $l_0(1 + \varepsilon)$ is m; $d_0\sqrt{1 - RA}$ is m; $F/A_u$ is Pa.
  • Common exam mistake. Getting the diameter after fracture as $d_0(1 - RA)$ = 7.83 mm: %RA is a reduction of the area, so the diameter scales with the square root.

Part (a) · 0.8 points

Does the rod deform plastically under the 9.5 kN load? Justify the answer.

Area of the cross-section, with the diameter in metres

The stress needs the initial area of the circular section. The diameter is first converted to metres, $d_0 = 8.0\ \text{mm} = 8.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}$, and then:

$$A_0 = \frac{\pi d_0^2}{4} = \frac{\pi\,(8.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2}{4} = \frac{\pi\times 6.4\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2}{4} = 5.027\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$$

Engineering stress produced by the force

The force is converted to newtons, $F = 9.5\ \text{kN} = 9.5\times10^{3}\ \text{N}$. The engineering stress is the force over the initial area [Session 11, Slide 9]:

$$\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0} = \frac{9.5\times10^{3}\ \text{N}}{5.027\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2} = 1.890\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa} = 189.0\ \text{MPa}$$

Compare the stress with the yield strength

The rod deforms plastically only if the stress exceeds the yield strength [Session 11, Slide 10]. Here

$$\sigma = 189.0\ \text{MPa} < \sigma_y = 280\ \text{MPa}$$

so the rod stays in the elastic zone: it does not deform plastically, and all its deformation is recovered when the load is removed. The yield strength is $280/189.0 = 1.48$ times the applied stress.

Key results
  • $A_0 = 5.027\times10^{-5}$ m²
  • $\sigma = 189.0$ MPa $< \sigma_y = 280$ MPa: elastic, no plastic deformation
  • Ratio $\sigma_y/\sigma = 1.48$

Part (b) · 1.2 points

Length and diameter of the rod while the load is applied.

Axial strain from Hooke's law

Part (a) showed that the rod is elastic, so Hooke's law applies [Session 11, Slide 12]. Isolating the strain from $\sigma = E\,\varepsilon_z$ (divide both sides by $E$):

$$\varepsilon_z = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{189.0\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}}{207\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa}} = 9.130\times10^{-4}$$

Length under the load

From the definition $\varepsilon_z = \Delta l/l_0$ [Session 11, Slide 9], multiplying both sides by $l_0$ gives the elongation:

$$\Delta l = \varepsilon_z\,l_0 = 9.130\times10^{-4}\times 350\ \text{mm} = 0.3196\ \text{mm}$$
$$l = l_0 + \Delta l = 350\ \text{mm} + 0.3196\ \text{mm} = 350.320\ \text{mm}$$

Transverse strain from Poisson's ratio

A longitudinal elastic stretch comes with a transverse contraction [Session 11, Slide 19]. From $\nu = -\varepsilon_x/\varepsilon_z$, multiplying both sides by $-\varepsilon_z$:

$$\varepsilon_x = -\nu\,\varepsilon_z = -0.31\times 9.130\times10^{-4} = -2.830\times10^{-4}$$

Diameter under the load

The transverse strain is the relative change of the diameter, $\varepsilon_x = \Delta d/d_0$, so

$$\Delta d = \varepsilon_x\,d_0 = -2.830\times10^{-4}\times 8.0\ \text{mm} = -2.264\times10^{-3}\ \text{mm}$$
$$d = d_0 + \Delta d = 8.0\ \text{mm} - 0.002264\ \text{mm} = 7.9977\ \text{mm}$$

The diameter shrinks by about 2.3 µm while the length grows by about 0.32 mm.

Key results
  • $\varepsilon_z = 9.130\times10^{-4}$, $\Delta l = 0.3196$ mm, $l = 350.320$ mm
  • $\varepsilon_x = -2.830\times10^{-4}$, $\Delta d = -2.264\times10^{-3}$ mm, $d = 7.9977$ mm

Interpretation and checks

  • The rod works at 189 MPa, two thirds of its yield strength, so the whole deformation is elastic and is recovered on unloading. If the same force acted on a rod of 6.0 mm, the stress would rise by $(8/6)^2$ to 336 MPa and the rod would yield: the check of part (a) must always come before Hooke's law.
  • Control case: with $\nu = 0$ the diameter would not change; with $\nu = 0.5$ (incompressible, Slide 19) the contraction would be $-4.57\times10^{-4}$, larger than the one found. The value 0.31 lies in the range 0.25 to 0.4 of metals in the slide.
  • SI check: $\sigma = F/A_0$ in N/m² = Pa; $\varepsilon = \sigma/E$ in Pa/Pa, dimensionless; $\Delta l = \varepsilon\,l_0$ and $\Delta d = \varepsilon_x d_0$ in metres.
  • Common mistake: using the radius in place of the diameter in $\pi d_0^2/4$, or forgetting the minus sign of the transverse strain and giving a diameter that grows under tension.

Length of the specimen after the 40 kN load is removed. Draw the loading and unloading path.

Split the total strain into its elastic and plastic parts

The strain recovered is therefore Hooke's law applied to the stress drop $\sigma_B - 0$:

$$\varepsilon_e = \frac{\sigma_B}{E} = \frac{353.7\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}}{69\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa}} = 5.126\times10^{-3}$$

Permanent strain

Isolating the plastic part from $\varepsilon_B = \varepsilon_e + \varepsilon_p$ (subtract $\varepsilon_e$ from both sides):

$$\varepsilon_p = \varepsilon_B - \varepsilon_e = 0.035 - 5.126\times10^{-3} = 0.02987$$

The permanent strain is measured on the initial gauge length, so

$$l_P = l_0\,(1 + \varepsilon_p) = 50.0\ \text{mm}\times(1 + 0.02987) = 51.494\ \text{mm}$$

  • Working stress $\sigma_w = \sigma_y/N$ [Session 11, Slide 29].
  • Hooke's law $\sigma = E\,\varepsilon$ [Session 11, Slide 12].
  • Modulus of resilience $U_r = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y}\sigma\,d\varepsilon$ [Session 11, Slide 35].
  • Hypotheses: uniform uniaxial stress; the weight of the rod is neglected against 2400 kg; linear elastic behaviour up to $\sigma_y$.

Part (a) · 0.8 points

Minimum diameter of the rod.

Load on the rod

The rod holds the mass at rest, so the axial force is its weight:

$$F = m\,g = 2400\ \text{kg}\times 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2 = 23\,544\ \text{N}$$

Largest stress allowed

The design keeps the stress at or below the working stress [Session 11, Slide 29]:

$$\sigma_w = \frac{\sigma_y}{N} = \frac{880\ \text{MPa}}{2.5} = 352\ \text{MPa} = 352\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}$$

Minimum area and diameter

The condition is $\sigma = F/A_0 \le \sigma_w$. Multiplying both sides by $A_0$ and dividing by $\sigma_w$ isolates the area:

$$A_0 \ge \frac{F}{\sigma_w} = \frac{23\,544\ \text{N}}{352\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}} = 6.689\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$$

From $A_0 = \pi d_0^2/4$: multiply by 4, divide by $\pi$ and take the square root:

$$d_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{4A_{\min}}{\pi}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\times 6.689\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2}{\pi}} = \sqrt{8.516\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2} = 9.228\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 9.23\ \text{mm}$$
Key results
  • $F = 23\,544$ N, $\sigma_w = 352$ MPa
  • $A_{\min} = 6.689\times10^{-5}$ m², $d_{\min} = 9.23$ mm

Part (b) · 0.6 points

The rod is made with the next whole millimetre, 10 mm. Find the real safety factor and the elongation of the rod under the load.

Stress with the 10 mm rod

$$A_0 = \frac{\pi\,(10\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2}{4} = 7.854\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2 \qquad \sigma = \frac{F}{A_0} = \frac{23\,544\ \text{N}}{7.854\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2} = 299.8\ \text{MPa}$$

Real safety factor

From $\sigma_w = \sigma_y/N$ with the working stress equal to the actual stress, isolating $N$:

$$N_{\text{real}} = \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma} = \frac{880\ \text{MPa}}{299.8\ \text{MPa}} = 2.94 \ge 2.5$$

Elongation

The rod is elastic ($\sigma < \sigma_y$), so Hooke's law gives the strain [Session 11, Slide 12], and $\Delta l = \varepsilon\,l_0$ the elongation:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{299.8\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}}{107\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa}} = 2.802\times10^{-3} \qquad \Delta l = 2.802\times10^{-3}\times 1200\ \text{mm} = 3.36\ \text{mm}$$
Key results
  • $A_0 = 7.854\times10^{-5}$ m², $\sigma = 299.8$ MPa
  • $N_{\text{real}} = 2.94$
  • $\varepsilon = 2.802\times10^{-3}$, $\Delta l = 3.36$ mm

Part (c) · 0.6 points

Derive the modulus of resilience of the alloy from its definition as an integral and evaluate it.

Definition and upper limit

The modulus of resilience is the elastic energy per unit volume stored up to the yield point, the area under the curve from 0 to $\varepsilon_y$ [Session 11, Slide 35]. The upper limit follows from Hooke's law at the yield point, $\sigma_y = E\,\varepsilon_y$, divided by $E$:

$$\varepsilon_y = \frac{\sigma_y}{E} = \frac{880\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}}{107\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa}} = 8.224\times10^{-3}$$

Integrate with Barrow's rule

In the elastic range $\sigma = E\,\varepsilon$ with $E$ constant, so $E$ leaves the integral. The antiderivative of $\varepsilon$ is $\varepsilon^2/2$, and Barrow's rule substitutes the limits:

$$U_r = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y}\sigma\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^{\varepsilon_y}E\,\varepsilon\,d\varepsilon = E\left[\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\right]_0^{\varepsilon_y} = E\left(\frac{\varepsilon_y^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2}\right) = \frac{E\,\varepsilon_y^2}{2}$$

Replacing $\varepsilon_y = \sigma_y/E$:

$$U_r = \frac{E}{2}\,\frac{\sigma_y^2}{E^2} = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{2E}$$
Figure 1. The modulus of resilience is the area under the elastic line from 0 to the yield strain.

Value

$$U_r = \frac{(880\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa})^2}{2\times 107\times10^{9}\ \text{Pa}} = \frac{7.744\times10^{17}\ \text{Pa}^2}{2.14\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}} = 3.619\times10^{6}\ \text{J/m}^3 = 3.619\ \text{MPa}$$
Key results
  • $\varepsilon_y = 8.224\times10^{-3}$
  • $U_r = 3.619\times10^{6}$ J/m³ $= 3.619$ MPa

Interpretation and checks

  • Rounding the diameter up from 9.23 mm to 10 mm raises the safety factor from 2.5 to 2.94: the area grows with the square of the diameter, $(10/9.228)^2 = 1.174$.
  • Control case: with $N = 1$ the minimum diameter would be $9.228\sqrt{1/2.5} = 5.84$ mm, the rod that just reaches yield; the diameter scales as $\sqrt{N}$.
  • The resilience 3.62 MJ/m³ is high because $\sigma_y$ is high and $E$ moderate, the property Slide 35 asks of spring alloys. At the working load each cubic metre of the 10 mm rod stores only $\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\varepsilon = 0.420$ MJ/m³, about 1/8.6 of $U_r$, since the energy goes with $\sigma^2$.
  • SI check: $F/\sigma_w$ in N/Pa = m²; $\sigma_y^2/E$ in Pa²/Pa = Pa = N/m² = J/m³, an energy per volume.
  • Common mistake: using the mass in kg as the force (forgetting $g$), or the yield strength in place of the working stress, which gives 5.84 mm and no margin.
