Problem 42

Particle Attracted to the Origin by $F = m\mu/r^\alpha$

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 29
Binet + Effective Potential

(Advanced problem.) A point particle $P$ of mass $m$ is attracted by the origin of coordinates $O$ of the inertial reference frame $S_0$ by a force with magnitude

$$ F = \frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha} $$

where $r$ is the distance between $O$ and $P$, and $\mu, \alpha$ are known constants.

Notation.

The official text writes $F = m\alpha/r^\beta$ (strength $\alpha$, exponent $\beta$) and asks for $\beta = 1, 2, 3, 4$. Here the strength is called $\mu$ and the exponent $\alpha$, so the cases "$\alpha = 1, 2, 3, 4$" below are the official $\beta = 1, 2, 3, 4$, and $\mu/h^2$ is the official $\alpha/h^2$.

  • (a)
    Show that the angular momentum vector and the mechanical energy of $P$ are conserved quantities of motion, and that the motion of $P$ occurs in a plane.
  • (b)
    Using polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in the plane of motion, find a second-order differential equation to solve for $u \equiv 1/r$ as a function of $\theta$ (i.e., $u = u(\theta)$).
  • (c)
    Find a first integral of this equation and discuss the limits of motion of $u$ as a function of $\mu/h^2$, $\alpha$, and the value of $u$ and $u' \equiv du/d\theta$ at $t = 0$, where $h$ is the magnitude of the angular momentum vector about the origin per unit mass. For $\alpha = 1, 2, 3,$ and $4$, determine under which conditions: (i) the particle escapes to infinity ($u = 0$); (ii) the particle reaches $r = 0$ ($u \to \infty$); (iii) the particle oscillates between two values of $u$.

Hint: try to solve first the problem with $\alpha = 2$, and then try the other values.

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

A point particle $P$ of mass $m$ is attracted by the origin $O$ of the inertial frame $S_0$ by a force of magnitude $m\mu/r^\alpha$, where $r$ is the distance from $O$ to $P$ (Problems.pdf, p. 29; the official text calls the strength $\alpha$ and the exponent $\beta$, see the notation note of the Problem Set). The statement asks to prove that the angular momentum and the mechanical energy are conserved and that the motion is planar, to find the second-order equation of $u$ as a function of $\theta$, and to discuss with a first integral when the particle escapes, reaches $O$ or oscillates, for the exponents 1, 2, 3 and 4. The solution follows the Newtonian approach of Notes.pdf, Sec. 7.1 to Sec. 7.7, and the derivation of the central force problem of Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.8.1 and Notes.pdf, Sec. 8.3, with the general exponent in place of the inverse square.

Change of Basis and Position Vector

Figure 2. Rotation by $\theta$ about $\mathbf k$ (out of the paper). The triangle of $\mathbf e_\theta$ has its opposite leg along $-\mathbf i$, which gives the minus sign in $\mathbf e_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\mathbf i + \cos\theta\,\mathbf j$.
Polar basis: rotation about k Law: elementary rotation about the third axis; change of basis matrix and its checks Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.14; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.2

View along $\mathbf k$, which points out of the paper (⊙) because it is the cross product of $\mathbf i$ (to the right) and $\mathbf j$ (up): a positive rotation about $\mathbf k$ is counterclockwise, as $\theta$ in the figure. $\mathbf e_r$ is $\mathbf i$ turned by $\theta$: in its right triangle the hypotenuse is 1, the adjacent leg $\cos\theta$ lies along $\mathbf i$ and the opposite leg $\sin\theta$ along $\mathbf j$. $\mathbf e_\theta$ is $\mathbf j$ turned by $\theta$: the adjacent leg $\cos\theta$ lies along $\mathbf j$ and the opposite leg $\sin\theta$ along minus $\mathbf i$, because the upward vector turned counterclockwise leans to the left (the minus sign).

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_r &= \cos\theta\,\mathbf i + \sin\theta\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{(right triangle of $\mathbf e_r$: adjacent leg on $\mathbf i$, opposite leg on $\mathbf j$)} \\ \mathbf e_\theta &= -\sin\theta\,\mathbf i + \cos\theta\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{(right triangle of $\mathbf e_\theta$: opposite leg on $-\mathbf i$, adjacent leg on $\mathbf j$)} \\ \mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_\theta &= \cos^2\theta\,\mathbf i\times\mathbf j - \sin^2\theta\,\mathbf j\times\mathbf i \quad &\text{(distributive law; $\mathbf i\times\mathbf i = \mathbf j\times\mathbf j = \mathbf 0$)} \\ &= \left(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta\right)\mathbf k = \mathbf k \quad &\text{(right-handed: $\mathbf j\times\mathbf i = -\mathbf k$)} \\ \det[{}_0R_1] &= 1\cdot\left(\cos\theta\cdot\cos\theta - \left(-\sin\theta\right)\sin\theta\right) = 1 \quad &\text{(expansion along the third row)} \\ \left([{}_0R_1][{}_0R_1]^T\right)_{11} &= \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 1)} \\ \left([{}_0R_1][{}_0R_1]^T\right)_{12} &= \cos\theta\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 2; row 3 is the unit row of $\mathbf k$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_1] = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \cr \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \cr 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det[{}_0R_1] = 1, \quad [{}_0R_1][{}_0R_1]^T = I}$$

Units: dimensionless direction cosines: $[1]$

Meaning: the columns of the matrix are $\mathbf e_r$, $\mathbf e_\theta$ and $\mathbf k$ written in $\mathcal B_0$; $\mathbf e_r$ follows the particle and $\mathbf e_\theta$ is perpendicular to it in the plane of the motion.

Position vector of the particle Law: position vector of a point particle; polar coordinates in the plane of the motion Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.2; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.3

In the plane of the motion the segment $OP$ is the hypotenuse $r$ of the right triangle whose leg along $\mathbf i$ is $r\cos\theta$ (adjacent to $\theta$) and whose leg along $\mathbf j$ is $r\sin\theta$ (opposite to $\theta$).

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P &= \overrightarrow{OP} \quad &\text{(definition, Eq. 2.2)} \\ &= r\,\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r$ is the unit vector from $O$ towards $P$)} \\ &= r\cos\theta\,\mathbf i + r\sin\theta\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r$ in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ \mathbf r_0^P\cdot\mathbf r_0^P &= r^2\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta \quad &\text{(components squared and added)} \\ &= r^2 \quad &\text{($\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = r\,\mathbf e_r = r\cos\theta\,\mathbf i + r\sin\theta\,\mathbf j, \qquad \lvert\mathbf r_0^P\rvert = r}$$

Units: $[r] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the position has only a radial component; the attraction acts along minus $\mathbf e_r$, that is along the line $PO$: it is a central force.

Kinematics

Derivatives of the polar unit vectors Law: derivative of a unit vector turning about a fixed axis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.25; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.26
$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\omega_{10} &= \dot\theta\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{($\mathcal B_1$ turns about the fixed $\mathbf k$ with the angle $\theta$)} \\ \mathbf k\times\mathbf e_r &= \cos\theta\,\mathbf j - \sin\theta\,\mathbf i = \mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{($\mathbf k\times\mathbf i = \mathbf j$, $\mathbf k\times\mathbf j = -\mathbf i$)} \\ \mathbf k\times\mathbf e_\theta &= -\sin\theta\,\mathbf j - \cos\theta\,\mathbf i = -\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(same products)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_r &= \boldsymbol\omega_{10}\times\mathbf e_r = \dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{(Eq. 1.62)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_\theta &= \boldsymbol\omega_{10}\times\mathbf e_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(Eq. 1.62)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf e}_r = \dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta, \qquad \dot{\mathbf e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_r}$$

Units: $[\dot\theta] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: both unit vectors turn with the particle at the rate $\dot\theta$; $\mathbf k$ is fixed.

Velocity and acceleration of the particle Law: time derivatives of the position vector in $S_0$; product rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.3; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.3
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(r\,\mathbf e_r\right) \quad &\text{(definition, Eq. 2.3)} \\ &= \dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\,\dot{\mathbf e}_r \quad &\text{(product rule)} \\ &= \dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{($\dot{\mathbf e}_r = \dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \ddot r\,\mathbf e_r + \dot r\,\dot{\mathbf e}_r + \dot r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + r\ddot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + r\dot\theta\,\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.4; product rule on each term)} \\ &= \ddot r\,\mathbf e_r + \dot r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + \dot r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + r\ddot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta - r\dot\theta^2\,\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(derivatives of the unit vectors substituted)} \\ &= \left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right)\mathbf e_r + \left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right)\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{(grouped along $\mathbf e_r$ and $\mathbf e_\theta$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = \dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta, \qquad \mathbf a_0^P = \left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right)\mathbf e_r + \left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right)\mathbf e_\theta}$$

Units: $[\dot r] = [r\dot\theta] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[\ddot r] = [r\dot\theta^2] = [\dot r\dot\theta] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: $\dot r$ is the rate at which $P$ moves away from $O$ and $r\dot\theta$ the speed at which it turns round $O$; these are the expressions of Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.3.

Angular momentum per unit mass in polar form Law: angular momentum about a point, per unit mass; cross products of the polar basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.48; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.7
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf h &= \mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf v_0^P \quad &\text{(Eq. 4.48 about $O$, divided by $m$)} \\ &= r\,\mathbf e_r\times\left(\dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta\right) \quad &\text{(position and velocity substituted)} \\ &= r\dot r\,\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_r + r^2\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{(distributive law)} \\ &= r^2\dot\theta\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_r = \mathbf 0$, $\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_\theta = \mathbf k$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf h = \frac{\mathbf H_{O,0}}{m} = r^2\dot\theta\,\mathbf k, \qquad h = r^2\dot\theta}$$

Units: $[r^2\dot\theta] = [\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: with $\mathbf k$ along $\mathbf H_{O,0}$ the rate $\dot\theta$ is positive: the particle turns counterclockwise about $\mathbf k$, never backwards.

Forces and Equations of Motion

The free-body diagram of $P$ shows a single force, the attraction towards $O$. $S_0$ is inertial, so no inertia force acts, and there is no constraint.

Figure 3. The only force on $P$ is the attraction towards $O$; its line of action passes through $O$, so its moment about $O$ is zero and $\mathbf H_{O,0}$ (out of the paper) is constant.
(a) Torque about O and conservation of the angular momentum Law: theorem of the angular momentum about a fixed point; moment of a central force Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.48; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.53; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.57; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.58; [Lecture 4, Slide 5]

The proof uses vectors of $S_0$ in any basis, not the polar coordinates, so it does not assume that the motion is planar. $O$ is fixed, so its velocity is zero.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{O,0} &= \mathbf r_0^P\times m\,\mathbf v_0^P \quad &\text{(definition, Eq. 4.48)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{O,0}}{dt}\right|_0 &= \mathbf v_0^P\times m\,\mathbf v_0^P + \mathbf r_0^P\times m\,\mathbf a_0^P \quad &\text{(product rule for the cross product; $O$ fixed)} \\ &= \mathbf r_0^P\times m\,\mathbf a_0^P \quad &\text{($\mathbf v_0^P\times\mathbf v_0^P = \mathbf 0$)} \\ &= \mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf F \quad &\text{(Newton's second law, Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4)} \\ &= r\,\mathbf e_r\times\left(-\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha}\,\mathbf e_r\right) \quad &\text{(position vector and force)} \\ &= -m\mu\,r^{1-\alpha}\left(\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_r\right) \quad &\text{(scalars taken out of the product)} \\ &= \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r\times\mathbf e_r = \mathbf 0$)} \\ \left[\mathbf H_{O,0}\right]_0^t &= \int_0^t\mathbf 0\,dt' = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf M_O = \mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf F = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf H_{O,0} = m\,\mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf v_0^P = \mathbf H_{O,0}(0) = \text{const}}$$

Units: $[\mathbf H_{O,0}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$; $[\mathbf M_O] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the line of action of the attraction always passes through $O$, so it has no moment about $O$: the three components of the angular momentum are conserved (three scalar laws), for any exponent.

(a) The motion is planar Law: the angular momentum is perpendicular to the position vector; scalar triple product Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.8.1; [Lecture 4, Slide 7]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P\cdot\mathbf H_{O,0} &= \mathbf r_0^P\cdot\left(\mathbf r_0^P\times m\,\mathbf v_0^P\right) \quad &\text{(definition of $\mathbf H_{O,0}$)} \\ &= m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\left(\mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf r_0^P\right) \quad &\text{(cyclic permutation of the triple product)} \\ &= 0 \quad &\text{($\mathbf r_0^P\times\mathbf r_0^P = \mathbf 0$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P(t)\cdot\mathbf H_{O,0} = 0 \ \text{for all } t, \ \text{with } \mathbf H_{O,0} \ \text{constant}: \ P \ \text{moves in the fixed plane through } O \ \text{perpendicular to } \mathbf H_{O,0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ on both sides of the zero

Meaning: the vector $\mathbf H_{O,0}$ is fixed by the previous box, and the position stays perpendicular to it: this is the plane $Oxy$ of the table, with $\mathbf k$ along $\mathbf H_{O,0}$. If $\mathbf H_{O,0}$ is zero the velocity is parallel to the position and the particle moves on a fixed line through $O$ (last limiting case).

(a) Elementary work of the attraction: an exact differential Law: elementary work of a force; potential energy exists when the work is an exact differential Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.14; Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.4
$$\begin{aligned} d\mathbf r_0^P &= dr\,\mathbf e_r + r\,d\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(differential of $r\,\mathbf e_r$, product rule)} \\ \mathbf e_r\cdot d\mathbf e_r &= \tfrac12\,d\left(\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf e_r\right) \quad &\text{(product rule on the dot product)} \\ &= \tfrac12\,d(1) = 0 \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r$ is a unit vector)} \\ \delta W_0 &= \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r_0^P \quad &\text{(Eq. 4.13)} \\ &= -\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha}\,\mathbf e_r\cdot\left(dr\,\mathbf e_r + r\,d\mathbf e_r\right) \quad &\text{(force and displacement substituted)} \\ &= -\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha}\,dr \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf e_r = 1$, $\mathbf e_r\cdot d\mathbf e_r = 0$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\delta W_0 = -m\mu\,r^{-\alpha}\,dr: \ \text{a function of } r \text{ alone times } dr, \ \text{an exact differential}}$$

Units: $[m\mu\,r^{-\alpha}\,dr] = [\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L^{\alpha+1}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^{-\alpha}}][\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: only the change of the distance to $O$ costs work; the work between two positions depends only on their distances to $O$, not on the path, so the attraction is conservative.

(a) Potential energy of the attraction Law: potential energy as minus the work from a reference position; antiderivative and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.14

The work of the previous box is integrated from a reference radius $r_*$, where the potential energy is zero, to the radius $r$; $s$ is the integration variable.

$$\begin{aligned} V_0(r) &= -\int_{r_*}^{r}\left(-m\mu\,s^{-\alpha}\right)ds \quad &\text{(Eq. 4.14 with the work of the previous box)} \\ &= m\mu\int_{r_*}^{r}s^{-\alpha}\,ds \quad &\text{(the two minus signs cancel)} \\ \alpha \ne 1:\quad V_0(r) &= m\mu\left[\frac{s^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right]_{r_*}^{r} \quad &\text{(antiderivative of $s^{-\alpha}$)} \\ &= m\mu\,\frac{r^{1-\alpha} - r_*^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha} \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \\ \alpha \gt 1,\ r_*\to\infty:\quad V_0(r) &= \frac{m\mu\,r^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha} \quad &\text{($r_*^{1-\alpha}\to 0$ because $1-\alpha \lt 0$)} \\ &= -\frac{m\mu}{(\alpha-1)\,r^{\alpha-1}} \quad &\text{(sign moved to the denominator)} \\ \alpha = 1:\quad V_0(r) &= m\mu\left[\ln s\right]_{r_*}^{r} \quad &\text{(antiderivative of $s^{-1}$)} \\ &= m\mu\ln\frac{r}{r_*} \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule; difference of logarithms)} \\ \alpha \gt 1:\quad \frac{dV_0}{dr} &= -\frac{m\mu}{\alpha-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)r^{-\alpha} \quad &\text{(derivative of the power $r^{1-\alpha}$)} \\ &= m\mu\,r^{-\alpha} \quad &\text{($(1-\alpha)/(\alpha-1) = -1$)} \\ \alpha = 1:\quad \frac{dV_0}{dr} &= \frac{m\mu}{r} \quad &\text{(derivative of the logarithm)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{V_0(r) = -\frac{m\mu}{(\alpha-1)\,r^{\alpha-1}} \ (\alpha = 2, 3, 4), \qquad V_0(r) = m\mu\ln\frac{r}{r_*} \ (\alpha = 1), \qquad \frac{dV_0}{dr} = m\mu\,r^{-\alpha}}$$

Units: $[m\mu\,r^{1-\alpha}] = [\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L^{\alpha+1}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^{1-\alpha}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$; the logarithm is of a ratio of lengths

Meaning: minus the derivative of the potential is the radial component of the force, as it must be. For the exponents above 1 the potential tends to zero far away; for the exponent 1 it grows without bound with $r$, which will forbid the escape.

(a) Conservation of the mechanical energy Law: theorem of the kinetic energy; the attraction derives from the potential of the previous box; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.11; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.29; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.31
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P\right) &= m\,\mathbf a_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P \quad &\text{(product rule on the dot product)} \\ &= \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v_0^P \quad &\text{(Newton's second law, Eq. 4.11)} \\ &= -\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha}\,\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf v_0^P \quad &\text{(force substituted)} \\ \mathbf r_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P &= \tfrac12\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf r_0^P\cdot\mathbf r_0^P\right) \quad &\text{(product rule on the dot product)} \\ &= \tfrac12\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\right) \quad &\text{($\mathbf r_0^P\cdot\mathbf r_0^P = r^2$)} \\ &= r\dot r \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf v_0^P &= \dot r \quad &\text{(divided by $r$; $\mathbf e_r$ is $\mathbf r_0^P/r$)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P\right) &= -m\mu\,r^{-\alpha}\,\dot r \quad &\text{(substituted)} \\ &= -\frac{dV_0}{dr}\,\dot r \quad &\text{(derivative of the potential, previous box)} \\ &= -\frac{d}{dt}V_0\big(r(t)\big) \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P + V_0\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(both terms on the left)} \\ \left[\tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P + V_0\right]_0^t &= 0 \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule between $0$ and $t$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{E_0 = \tfrac12 m\,\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P + V_0(r) = \text{const}}$$

Units: $[\tfrac12 m v^2] = [V_0] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the only force does work that depends on the distance to $O$ alone, so the kinetic energy gained when $P$ falls towards $O$ is exactly the potential energy lost: the mechanical energy is a second conserved quantity. This completes part (a).

Newton's second law in the plane of the motion Law: Newton's second law in an inertial frame, projected on the polar basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.5; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.6
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf a_0^P &= -\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha}\,\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(the only force; Eq. 4.4)} \\ \mathbf e_r:\ m\left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right) &= -\frac{m\mu}{r^\alpha} \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf e_r$)} \\ \mathbf e_\theta:\ m\left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf e_\theta$)} \\ \mathbf k:\ 0 &= 0 \quad &\text{(planar motion: no component along $\mathbf k$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = -\frac{\mu}{r^\alpha}, \qquad r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta = 0}$$

Units: $[\ddot r] = [r\dot\theta^2] = [\mu\,r^{-\alpha}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: two equations for the two unknown functions $r(t)$ and $\theta(t)$; with the exponent 2 they are Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.5 and Eq. 8.6.

Areal integral: the angular momentum per unit mass is constant Law: the transverse equation times r is an exact derivative; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.7; [Lecture 4, Slide 12]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\dot\theta\right) &= 2r\dot r\dot\theta + r^2\ddot\theta \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule on $r^2$)} \\ &= r\left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right) \quad &\text{(common factor $r$)} \\ &= 0 \quad &\text{(transverse equation)} \\ \left[r^2\dot\theta\right]_0^t &= 0 \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \\ \dot\theta &= \frac{h}{r^2} = h\,u^2 \quad &\text{(the constant is $h$, kinematics box; $u = 1/r$)} \end{aligned}$$

Erratum to Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.8.1, property 5

Property 5 of the list of properties of central force motion in Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.8.1 states that the particle $P$ can never reach $O$ unless the angular momentum $\mathbf H_{O,0}$ is zero. The list is written for any central force, but the property holds only under an extra hypothesis: the effective potential $W$ must grow without bound as $u$ grows, that is, the centrifugal barrier must dominate the attraction near $O$. For the power law of this problem that is the case for every exponent below 3 (gravity, the exponent 2 of Notes.pdf, Ch. 8, included) and for the exponent 3 with $K$ below 1. With nonzero angular momentum the particle does reach $O$, in a finite time, for the exponent 3 with $K$ of at least 1 and for the exponent 4 (boxes of part (c) and the box below). Zero angular momentum is not sufficient either: the radial motion of the previous box escapes for some data.

Two first integrals: angular momentum and energy Law: exact derivatives (product and chain rules); Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.58; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.31; Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.7; [Lecture 3, Slide 17]; [Lecture 4, Slide 7]

$$\boxed{r^2\dot\theta = h = a\,v_0, \qquad E = m\dot r^2 + \frac{m h^2}{2r^2} + mg\left(r - 2a\right) = \text{const}}$$

Units: $[h] = [\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-1}}]$; every term of $E$ $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the first is the angular momentum of $P_1$ about the vertical through the hole per unit mass, constant because the tension passes through the hole; the second is the mechanical energy of the pair: the tension does the power $-T\dot r$ on $P_1$ and $+T\dot r$ on $P_2$, which cancel, and the smooth stationary constraints do no work.

(a) and (b) Circular motion

Speed and tension for a circular motion of P1 Law: equations of motion with a constant radius Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Sec. 7.7

A circumference of $P_1$ about the hole has a constant radius, equal to its initial value $a$.

$$\begin{aligned} r &\equiv a, \quad \dot r = \ddot r = 0 \quad &\text{(constant radius)} \\ m\cdot 0 &= T - mg \quad &\text{(equation of $P_2$)} \\ T &= mg \quad &\text{(solved)} \\ \dot\theta &= \frac{h}{a^2} \quad &\text{(areal integral with $r = a$)} \\ &= \frac{v_0}{a} \quad &\text{($h = a v_0$)} \\ m\left(0 - a\dot\theta^2\right) &= -T \quad &\text{(equation of $P_1$ along $\mathbf e_r$)} \\ T &= m a\dot\theta^2 \quad &\text{(solved)} \\ &= \frac{m v_0^2}{a} \quad &\text{($\dot\theta = v_0/a$)} \\ \frac{m v_0^2}{a} &= mg \quad &\text{(the two values of $T$)} \\ v_0 &= \sqrt{a g} \quad &\text{(positive root)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\text{(a)}\ v_0 = \sqrt{a g}, \qquad \text{(b)}\ T = mg}$$

Units: $[\sqrt{ag}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[mg] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: $P_2$ hangs at rest, so the cord carries its weight; that weight is exactly the centripetal force that keeps $P_1$ on the circle of radius $a$.

(c) Reduction to quadratures for the speed of the statement

First integrals evaluated with the initial state Law: the first integrals with the initial data; energy integral solved for the radial rate Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Problems.pdf, p. 30
$$\begin{aligned} v_0^2 &= \frac{8ag}{3} \quad &\text{(statement)} \\ h^2 &= a^2v_0^2 = \frac{8a^3g}{3} \quad &\text{($h = a v_0$)} \\ \frac{E}{m} &= \dot r(0)^2 + \frac{h^2}{2a^2} + g\left(a - 2a\right) \quad &\text{(energy at $t = 0$)} \\ &= 0 + \frac{4ag}{3} - g a \quad &\text{($\dot r(0) = 0$; $h^2/(2a^2) = 4ag/3$)} \\ &= \frac{g a}{3} \quad &\text{(added)} \\ \dot r^2 &= \frac{E}{m} - \frac{h^2}{2r^2} - g\left(r - 2a\right) \quad &\text{(energy integral solved for $\dot r^2$)} \\ &= \frac{g a}{3} - \frac{4a^3g}{3r^2} - g r + 2g a \quad &\text{(values substituted)} \\ &= \frac{7g a}{3} - g r - \frac{4a^3g}{3r^2} \quad &\text{(constant terms added)} \\ &= \frac{g\left(7a r^2 - 3r^3 - 4a^3\right)}{3r^2} \quad &\text{(common denominator $3r^2$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{h^2 = \frac{8a^3g}{3}, \qquad \dot r^2 = \frac{g\,P(r)}{3r^2}, \qquad P(r) = -3r^3 + 7a r^2 - 4a^3}$$

Units: $[g\,P/r^2] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^3}][\mathrm{L^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\dot r^2]$

Meaning: the radial motion obeys a one-dimensional energy equation: the radial rate is known from the radius alone.

Quadrature for the time Law: separation of variables in the energy integral; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44

The sign of the radial rate is fixed by the radial acceleration at the start, where the rate is zero.

$$\begin{aligned} \ddot r(0) &= \tfrac12\left(-g + \frac{h^2}{a^3}\right) \quad &\text{(equation of motion free of $T$ with $r\dot\theta^2 = h^2/r^3$, at $r = a$)} \\ &= \tfrac12\left(-g + \frac{8g}{3}\right) \quad &\text{($h^2 = 8a^3g/3$)} \\ &= \frac{5g}{6} \gt 0 \quad &\text{(added: $r$ starts to grow)} \\ \frac{dr}{dt} &= +\sqrt{\frac{g}{3}}\,\frac{\sqrt{P(r)}}{r} \quad &\text{(positive root while $r$ grows)} \\ \frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{P(r)}} &= \sqrt{\frac{g}{3}}\,dt \quad &\text{(separation of variables)} \\ \int_a^r\frac{r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{P(r')}} &= \sqrt{\frac{g}{3}}\int_0^t dt' \quad &\text{(integrated from the initial state)} \\ &= \sqrt{\frac{g}{3}}\;t \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule on the right side)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{t = \sqrt{\frac{3}{g}}\int_a^r\frac{r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{P(r')}} \ \ (r \text{ growing}), \qquad t = \frac{\tau_r}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{3}{g}}\int_r^{2a}\frac{r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{P(r')}} \ \ (r \text{ decreasing from } 2a)}$$

Units: $[\sqrt{3/g}\;r\,dr/\sqrt{P}] = [\mathrm{T\,L^{-1/2}}][\mathrm{L^2}][\mathrm{L^{-3/2}}] = [\mathrm{T}]$

Meaning: the integrand is improper at the turning radii, where $P$ has simple roots (next part), but it is integrable; after the outer turning point the root changes sign and the motion repeats with the radial period $\tau_r$, twice the time from $a$ to $2a$.

Quadrature for the angle Law: areal integral with the time element of the previous box; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 8.7
$$\begin{aligned} d\theta &= \frac{h}{r^2}\,dt \quad &\text{(areal integral, $\dot\theta = h/r^2$)} \\ &= \frac{h}{r^2}\,\sqrt{\frac{3}{g}}\,\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{P(r)}} \quad &\text{($dt$ of the previous box)} \\ &= h\sqrt{\frac{3}{g}}\,\frac{dr}{r\sqrt{P(r)}} \quad &\text{(one power of $r$ cancelled)} \\ h\sqrt{\frac{3}{g}} &= \sqrt{\frac{8a^3g}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{g}} \quad &\text{(product of the square roots)} \\ &= \sqrt{8a^3} = 2\sqrt2\,a^{3/2} \quad &\text{($g$ and 3 cancel)} \\ \theta - 0 &= 2\sqrt2\,a^{3/2}\int_a^r\frac{dr'}{r'\sqrt{P(r')}} \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule from $\theta(0) = 0$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\theta(r) = 2\sqrt2\,a^{3/2}\int_a^r\frac{dr'}{r'\sqrt{P(r')}} \ \ (r \text{ growing}), \qquad \theta(t) = \int_0^t\frac{h}{r(t')^2}\,dt' \ \ (\text{all } t)}$$

Units: $[a^{3/2}\,dr/(r\sqrt P)] = [\mathrm{L^{3/2}}][\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{L^{-1}}][\mathrm{L^{-3/2}}] = [1]$

Meaning: the angle grows monotonically, since the angular rate is always positive; together with the quadrature for the time it gives the orbit of $P_1$ and, through the cord, the height of $P_2$.

(d) Oscillation between a and 2a

Figure 5. The square of the radial rate is proportional to $-3x^3 + 7x^2 - 4 = -(x-1)(x-2)(3x+2)$, with $x = r/a$: it is non-negative only for $1 \le x \le 2$, so $P_1$ oscillates between $r = a$ and $r = 2a$.
Band of radii and turning points Law: the square of the radial rate cannot be negative; radial acceleration at the turning points Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28; Problems.pdf, p. 30

With $x = r/a$ the polynomial is factored with the roots of the hint.

$$\begin{aligned} P(r) &= a^3\left(-3x^3 + 7x^2 - 4\right) \quad &\text{($r = a x$; $a^3$ factored out)} \\ \left(x - 1\right)\left(x - 2\right) &= x^2 - 3x + 2 \quad &\text{(product)} \\ \left(x^2 - 3x + 2\right)\left(3x + 2\right) &= 3x^3 - 7x^2 + 4 \quad &\text{(product)} \\ -3x^3 + 7x^2 - 4 &= -\left(x - 1\right)\left(x - 2\right)\left(3x + 2\right) \quad &\text{(minus the previous row: roots $1$, $2$, $-2/3$)} \\ P \ge 0 &\iff \left(x - 1\right)\left(x - 2\right) \le 0 \quad &\text{($\dot r^2 \ge 0$; $3x + 2 \gt 0$ for $x \gt 0$)} \\ &\iff 1 \le x \le 2 \quad &\text{(a product of two factors is not positive between their roots)} \\ \ddot r &= -\frac{g}{2} + \frac{h^2}{2r^3} \quad &\text{(equation of motion free of $T$, $r\dot\theta^2 = h^2/r^3$, divided by 2)} \\ &= -\frac{g}{2} + \frac{4a^3g}{3r^3} \quad &\text{($h^2 = 8a^3g/3$)} \\ \ddot r\big|_{r = a} &= -\frac{g}{2} + \frac{4g}{3} \quad &\text{(inner turning point)} \\ &= \frac{5g}{6} \gt 0 \quad &\text{(added)} \\ \ddot r\big|_{r = 2a} &= -\frac{g}{2} + \frac{g}{6} \quad &\text{(outer turning point: $4a^3/(3\cdot 8a^3) = 1/6$)} \\ &= -\frac{g}{3} \lt 0 \quad &\text{(added)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{a \le r(t) \le 2a; \ \text{the radial rate vanishes at } r = a \ (\ddot r = 5g/6 \gt 0) \text{ and at } r = 2a \ (\ddot r = -g/3 \lt 0): \ P_1 \text{ oscillates between them}}$$

Units: $x$ dimensionless; $[\ddot r] = [g] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: at both ends of the band the radial rate vanishes and the radial acceleration points back into the band, so $r$ reverses in a finite time at each end. At the outer end the hanging length $2a - r$ is zero: $P_2$ arrives at the hole with zero velocity, exactly at the limit allowed by the cord, and goes down again.

(e) Tension of the cord

Tension from Newton's law for P2 Law: equation of P2 along the vertical; radial acceleration from the equation free of the tension Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Problems.pdf, p. 30
$$\begin{aligned} T &= m\left(g + \ddot r\right) \quad &\text{(equation of $P_2$ along $\mathbf k$)} \\ \ddot r &= -\frac{g}{2} + \frac{4a^3g}{3r^3} \quad &\text{(previous box)} \\ T &= m\left(g - \frac{g}{2} + \frac{4a^3g}{3r^3}\right) \quad &\text{(substituted)} \\ &= mg\left(\frac12 + \frac{4a^3}{3r^3}\right) \quad &\text{(common factor $mg$)} \\ T(a) &= mg\left(\frac12 + \frac43\right) \quad &\text{(inner end of the band)} \\ &= \frac{11}{6}\,mg \quad &\text{(added)} \\ T(2a) &= mg\left(\frac12 + \frac{4}{24}\right) \quad &\text{(outer end of the band)} \\ &= \frac23\,mg \quad &\text{(added)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: the expression of the statement. The tension decreases with $r$ and is smallest at the outer end, where it is still positive: the cord stays tense during the whole motion, as assumed.

(f) Rough plane

Friction force on P1 Law: Coulomb friction of a sliding particle, opposite to its velocity relative to the (fixed) plane Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 6.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 6.21; [Lecture 3, Slide 18]

The friction lies in the plane, so the vertical equation of $P_1$ is unchanged and the normal reaction is still the weight. The plane is at rest in $S_0$, so the relative velocity is the velocity of $P_1$.

$$\begin{aligned} N_1 &= mg \quad &\text{(equation of $P_1$ along $\mathbf k$)} \\ \lvert\mathbf v_0^{P_1}\rvert &= \sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2} \quad &\text{(orthonormal polar basis)} \\ \mathbf R &= -\mu N_1\,\frac{\mathbf v_0^{P_1}}{\lvert\mathbf v_0^{P_1}\rvert} \quad &\text{(Eq. 6.21, $P_1$ sliding)} \\ &= -\mu mg\,\frac{\dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} \quad &\text{($N_1$ and the velocity substituted)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf R = -\mu mg\,\frac{\dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}}}$$

Units: $[\mu mg] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$; the fraction is dimensionless

Meaning: a force of constant magnitude opposite to the velocity of $P_1$; it adds no unknown while $P_1$ slides.

Equations of motion on the rough plane Law: Newton's second law with the friction added; elimination of the tension Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Sec. 6.6; [Lecture 3, Slide 18]
$$\begin{aligned} P_1,\ \mathbf e_r:\ m\left(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\right) &= -T - \mu mg\,\frac{\dot r}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf e_r$)} \\ P_1,\ \mathbf e_\theta:\ m\left(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\right) &= -\mu mg\,\frac{r\dot\theta}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf e_\theta$)} \\ P_2,\ \mathbf k:\ m\ddot r &= T - mg \quad &\text{(unchanged)} \\ 2\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 + g + \mu g\,\frac{\dot r}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} &= 0 \quad &\text{(first plus third, divided by $m$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\begin{array}{l} 2\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 + g + \mu g\,\dfrac{\dot r}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} = 0 \cr r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta + \mu g\,\dfrac{r\dot\theta}{\sqrt{\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2}} = 0 \cr T = m\left(g + \ddot r\right), \quad N_1 = mg \end{array}}$$

Units: every term of the first two equations $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the two equations for $r$ and $\theta$ hold while $P_1$ slides and the cord is tense; the tension and the normal reaction follow afterwards.

Angular momentum and energy on the rough plane Law: transverse equation times r; theorem of the kinetic energy for both particles (power of the forces) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.11; Notes.pdf, Sec. 6.6; [Lecture 3, Slide 17]
