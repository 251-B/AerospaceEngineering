Problem 10

Conservative Force and Potential I

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 9
Work & Potential Energy

A particle of mass $m$ moves under a force $\mathbf{F} = -c x^3\mathbf{i}$, where $c$ is a positive constant. Find the potential energy function. If the particle starts from rest at $x = -a$, what is its velocity when it reaches $x = 0$? In the subsequent motion, where does it stop again?

Worked Solution [ Click to Expand ]

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The particle $P$ moves along a fixed straight line, the axis $Ox$ of an inertial frame. The force of the statement depends only on the abscissa, so the problem is one-dimensional and conservative: the answers follow from the potential energy and from the conservation of mechanical energy (Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.5).

Change of Basis and Position Vector

The view of the figure looks along $\mathbf k$, which points out of the paper (⊙): $\mathbf i$ to the right, $\mathbf j$ up. The particle is drawn at a point of negative abscissa, with the force that pulls it towards $O$.

Figure 1. Fixed basis $\mathcal B_0$ ($\mathbf k$ out of the paper) and position vector $\mathbf r_0^P = x\,\mathbf i$; for negative $x$ the force $-c x^3\mathbf i$ points along $+\mathbf i$, towards $O$..
The only basis and its change of basis matrix Law: change of basis matrix between two bases; orthonormality and unit determinant Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.14; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15

The problem needs one basis only, $\mathcal B_0$, attached to the inertial frame. The matrix that writes $\mathcal B_0$ in itself has as columns the components of $\mathbf i$, $\mathbf j$, $\mathbf k$ in $\mathcal B_0$, so it is the identity; it is checked like any rotation matrix.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf i &= 1\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(first column: components of $\mathbf i$ in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ \mathbf j &= 0\,\mathbf i + 1\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(second column)} \\ \mathbf k &= 0\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 1\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(third column)} \\ \det[{}_0R_0] &= 1\cdot(1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0) - 0 + 0 = 1 \quad &\text{(expansion along the first row)} \\ [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T &= I\,I = I \quad &\text{(the identity is symmetric and equal to its square)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_0] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det[{}_0R_0] = 1, \quad [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T = I}$$

Units: every entry is a direction cosine, dimensionless: $[1]$

Meaning: no rotation is involved; $\mathcal B_0$ is orthonormal and right-handed, and every vector of the problem is written in it.

Derivatives of the unit vectors of the fixed basis Law: derivative of a vector of constant modulus that rotates with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62

The basis $\mathcal B_0$ is attached to $S_0$, so its angular velocity relative to $S_0$ is the zero vector.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\mathbf i}{dt} &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf i \quad &\text{(derivative of a unit vector)} \\ &= \mathbf 0 \times \mathbf i \quad &\text{($\mathcal B_0$ does not rotate in $S_0$)} \\ &= (0\cdot 0 - 0\cdot 0)\,\mathbf i + (0\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0)\,\mathbf j + (0\cdot 0 - 0\cdot 1)\,\mathbf k = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(components of the vector product written out)} \\ \frac{d\mathbf j}{dt} &= \mathbf 0 \times \mathbf j = \mathbf 0, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf k}{dt} = \mathbf 0 \times \mathbf k = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(same product with the other two vectors)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf i}{dt} = \frac{d\mathbf j}{dt} = \frac{d\mathbf k}{dt} = \mathbf 0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$, every component zero

Meaning: the unit vectors of $\mathcal B_0$ are constant, so only the coordinate $x$ changes in time.

Position vector of the particle Law: position vector of a point in Cartesian components Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.2; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.5
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P &= \overrightarrow{OP} \quad &\text{(definition of the position vector)} \\ &= x\,\mathbf i + y\,\mathbf j + z\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(Cartesian components in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ &= x\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{(constraints $y = 0$, $z = 0$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = x\,\mathbf i}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: $x$ is a signed distance: negative to the left of $O$, where the particle starts.

Kinematics

Velocity and acceleration of the particle Law: velocity and acceleration as time derivatives of the position vector in $S_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(x\,\mathbf i\right) \quad &\text{(definition of velocity)} \\ &= \dot x\,\mathbf i + x\,\frac{d\mathbf i}{dt} \quad &\text{(product rule)} \\ &= \dot x\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{($\mathbf i$ is constant)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot x\,\mathbf i\right) \quad &\text{(definition of acceleration)} \\ &= \ddot x\,\mathbf i + \dot x\,\frac{d\mathbf i}{dt} = \ddot x\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{(product rule; $\mathbf i$ is constant)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = \dot x\,\mathbf i, \qquad \mathbf a_0^P = \ddot x\,\mathbf i}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$ and $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: velocity and acceleration lie along the line of motion; the sign of the rate of $x$ tells the sense of motion.

Forces and Equations of Motion

The free-body diagram shows the applied force along $\mathbf i$ and the resultant $\mathbf R_\perp$ of the forces normal to the axis. No inertia forces appear because $S_0$ is inertial.

Figure 2. Free-body diagram. Only $\mathbf F$ has a component along $\mathbf i$; $\mathbf R_\perp$ is drawn as an unknown, and the $\mathbf j$, $\mathbf k$ equations give $R_y = R_z = 0$..
Newton's second law projected on the fixed basis Law: Newton's second law in an inertial frame, projected on $\mathcal B_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.5
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf a_0^P &= \mathbf F + \mathbf R_\perp \quad &\text{(sum of the forces on $P$)} \\ m\,\ddot x\,\mathbf i &= -c\,x^3\,\mathbf i + R_y\,\mathbf j + R_z\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(kinematics and forces in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ \mathbf i:\quad m\,\ddot x &= -c\,x^3 \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf i$)} \\ \mathbf j, \mathbf k:\quad 0 &= R_y, \qquad 0 = R_z \quad &\text{(dot products with $\mathbf j$ and $\mathbf k$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{m\,\ddot x = -c\,x^3, \qquad \mathbf R_\perp = \mathbf 0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{-2}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^3}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the force always points towards $O$ (positive for negative $x$, negative for positive $x$) and grows with the cube of the distance; the forces normal to the axis balance each other (the guide carries the weight, if there is one).

The force is conservative: potential energy function Law: in one dimension a force that depends only on position derives from a potential, defined by its work Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.16; [Lecture 2, Slide 14]

The component $F_x(x)$ depends only on $x$, so the elementary work is an exact differential and the potential exists. The reference is taken at the origin.

$$\begin{aligned} V_0(x) &= -\int_0^x F_x(x')\,dx' \quad &\text{(definition of the potential, reference $V_0(0) = 0$)} \\ &= -\int_0^x \left(-c\,x'^3\right)dx' = c\int_0^x x'^3\,dx' \quad &\text{(component of the force of the statement)} \\ &= c\left[\frac{x'^4}{4}\right]_0^x \quad &\text{(antiderivative of $x'^3$)} \\ &= c\left(\frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{0^4}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x^4 \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \\ -\frac{dV_0}{dx} &= -\frac{1}{4}\,c\cdot 4x^3 = -c\,x^3 = F_x \quad &\text{(check: the inversion of the definition returns the force)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{V_0(x) = \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x^4}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^{-2}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^4}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, an energy

Meaning: an even, convex well with its minimum at $O$, flatter than a parabola near the origin and steeper far from it.

The potential well and the energy level of the release are drawn below; the turning points are where the level meets the curve.

Figure 3. Potential well and energy level of the release. The motion is allowed where $V_0 \le E_0$, between $-a$ and $+a$; the kinetic energy is largest at the origin..
First integral: conservation of mechanical energy Law: the equation of motion multiplied by the velocity is an exact time derivative Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.21; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.22; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.26
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\ddot x + c\,x^3 &= 0 \quad &\text{(equation of motion along $\mathbf i$)} \\ \dot x\left(m\,\ddot x + c\,x^3\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(multiplication by $\dot x$)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x^2\right) &= m\,\dot x\,\ddot x \quad &\text{(chain rule: derivative of $\tfrac12 m u^2$ with $u = \dot x$)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}V_0(x(t)) &= \frac{dV_0}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{dt} = c\,x^3\,\dot x \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x^2 + \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x^4\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(sum of the two derivatives above)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x^2 + \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x^4 = \text{const}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ for both terms

Meaning: kinetic plus potential energy stays constant; the speed at each position follows from the position alone.

Mechanical energy of the release Law: the constant of the energy integral is fixed by the initial conditions Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.26; Problems.pdf, p. 9
$$\begin{aligned} E_0 &= \frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x(0)^2 + \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x(0)^4 \quad &\text{(energy integral at $t = 0$)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}m\cdot 0^2 + \frac{1}{4}\,c\,(-a)^4 \quad &\text{(released from rest at $x = -a$)} \\ &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \quad &\text{($(-a)^4 = a^4$, even power)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^{-2}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^4}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: all the energy is potential at the release; it turns into kinetic energy as the particle falls into the well.

Answers and Checks

Velocity on reaching the origin Law: conservation of mechanical energy between the release point and the origin Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; [Lecture 2, Slide 20]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x^2 + \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x^4 &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \quad &\text{(energy integral with the energy of the release)} \\ \frac{1}{2}m\,\dot x^2 + 0 &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \quad &\text{(at $x = 0$)} \\ \dot x^2 &= \frac{c\,a^4}{2m} \quad &\text{(multiplication by $2/m$)} \\ |\dot x| &= a^2\sqrt{\frac{c}{2m}} \quad &\text{(square root; $a^4 = (a^2)^2$ with $a^2 \gt 0$)} \\ \dot x &\gt 0 \quad &\text{(sign: see the sentence below)} \end{aligned}$$

Sign: at the release the velocity is zero and the force at $x = -a$ is $c\,a^3\,\mathbf i$, along $+\mathbf i$, so the particle starts moving towards positive $x$. The velocity can vanish again only where the kinetic energy vanishes, that is where the potential equals $E_0$, which happens only at $x = \pm a$; between $-a$ and $0$ it cannot change sign, so it is positive at the origin.

$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P(x = 0) = a^2\sqrt{\frac{c}{2m}}\ \mathbf i}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L^2}]\left([\mathrm{M\,L^{-2}\,T^{-2}}]/[\mathrm{M}]\right)^{1/2} = [\mathrm{L^2}][\mathrm{L^{-1}\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the origin is the bottom of the well, so the speed there is the largest speed of the motion.

Point where the particle stops again Law: turning points: the kinetic energy vanishes where the potential equals the mechanical energy Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{4}\,c\,x_s^4 &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \quad &\text{(zero kinetic energy at the stop $x_s$)} \\ x_s^4 - a^4 &= 0 \quad &\text{(division by $c/4 \gt 0$)} \\ \left(x_s^2 - a^2\right)\left(x_s^2 + a^2\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(difference of squares)} \\ \left(x_s - a\right)\left(x_s + a\right)\left(x_s^2 + a^2\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(difference of squares again)} \\ x_s &= \pm a \quad &\text{($x_s^2 + a^2 \gt 0$ has no real root)} \\ x_s &= +a \quad &\text{(the particle crossed $O$ moving towards positive $x$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{x_s = +a}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the motion is allowed only where the potential does not exceed $E_0$, the interval from $-a$ to $+a$; the particle stops at $+a$, is pushed back by the force $-c\,a^3\,\mathbf i$ and oscillates periodically between $-a$ and $+a$.

Independent check: integration of the equation of motion in the variable x Law: chain rule on the acceleration, separation of variables and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.22; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.24
$$\begin{aligned} \ddot x &= \frac{d\dot x}{dt} = \frac{d\dot x}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{dt} = v\,\frac{dv}{dx} \quad &\text{(chain rule, with $v = \dot x$ as a function of $x$)} \\ m\,v\,dv &= -c\,x^3\,dx \quad &\text{(equation of motion times $dx$)} \\ m\int_0^{v_1} v\,dv &= -c\int_{-a}^{0} x^3\,dx \quad &\text{(from the release, $v = 0$ at $x = -a$, to the origin, $v = v_1$)} \\ m\left[\frac{v^2}{2}\right]_0^{v_1} &= -c\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_{-a}^{0} \quad &\text{(antiderivatives)} \\ \frac{1}{2}m\,v_1^2 &= -c\left(0 - \frac{a^4}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \\ v_1^2 &= \frac{c\,a^4}{2m} \quad &\text{(same value as with the energy integral)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_1 = a^2\sqrt{\frac{c}{2m}}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the work of the force from $-a$ to $0$ is all converted into kinetic energy, which confirms the result without the potential.

Limiting cases and symmetry Law: behaviour of the results for limiting data; parity of the potential Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28
$$\begin{aligned} a \to 0:\quad E_0 &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,a^4 \to 0, \qquad v_1 = a^2\sqrt{\frac{c}{2m}} \to 0 \quad &\text{(released at the equilibrium, it stays at rest)} \\ c \to 0:\quad v_1 &\to 0 \quad &\text{(no force, no motion from rest)} \\ m \to \infty:\quad v_1 &\to 0 \quad &\text{(infinite inertia)} \\ V_0(-x) &= \frac{1}{4}\,c\,(-x)^4 = V_0(x) \quad &\text{(even potential, so the stops are symmetric about $O$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\text{the turning points are } \pm a \text{ and the speed at } O \text{ scales as } a^2}$$

Units: $E_0$ in $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, $v_1$ in $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; each limit keeps these dimensions

Meaning: unlike a linear spring (speed proportional to $a$), the cubic force gives a speed at the origin proportional to $a^2$.

Forces and Equations of Motion

The free-body diagram of the figure above shows the conservative force along $\mathbf i$ (its sign depends on the position) and the resultant of the normal forces. $S_0$ is inertial: no inertia forces.

Force derived from the potential Law: in one dimension the force is minus the derivative of the potential; chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.16; [Lecture 2, Slide 14]

Write $u$ for the inner function $x^2 - a^2$, whose derivative is $2x$.

$$\begin{aligned} V &= -\frac{c}{2}\,u^2, \qquad u = x^2 - a^2 \quad &\text{(composition of functions)} \\ \frac{dV}{dx} &= \frac{dV}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx} = -\frac{c}{2}\cdot 2u\cdot 2x \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ &= -2c\,x\left(x^2 - a^2\right) \quad &\text{(back to $x$)} \\ F_x &= -\frac{dV}{dx} = 2c\,x\left(x^2 - a^2\right) \quad &\text{(force from the potential)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf F = 2c\,x\left(x^2 - a^2\right)\mathbf i}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^{-2}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{L^2}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: for $0 \lt x \lt a$ the force is negative (towards the origin) and for $x \gt a$ it is positive (away from it): inside the barriers the force pulls towards $O$, outside them it pushes away from $O$ (symmetrically for negative $x$).

Equation of motion and energy integral Law: Newton's second law along $\mathbf i$; multiplication by the velocity and chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.21; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.26; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\ddot x\,\mathbf i &= F_x\,\mathbf i + R_y\,\mathbf j + R_z\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(second law in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ m\,\ddot x &= -\frac{dV}{dx}, \qquad R_y = R_z = 0 \quad &\text{(components along $\mathbf i$, $\mathbf j$, $\mathbf k$)} \\ \dot x\left(m\,\ddot x + \frac{dV}{dx}\right) &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 m\dot x^2\right) + \frac{dV}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt} = 0 \quad &\text{(multiplication by $\dot x$; chain rule twice)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 m\dot x^2 + V(x)\right) &= 0 \quad &\text{(the bracket is a first integral)} \\ \frac12 m\dot x^2 &= E - V(x) \ge 0 \quad &\text{(kinetic energy is non-negative)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{m\,\ddot x = 2c\,x\left(x^2 - a^2\right), \qquad \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 = E - V(x), \qquad V(x) \le E}$$

Problem 12

Conservative Force and Potential III

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 9
Oscillation PeriodOscillations - Lecture 6

A particle moving under a conservative force oscillates between $x_1$ and $x_2$. Show that the period of oscillation is:

$$ \tau = 2\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \sqrt{\frac{m}{2[V(x_2) - V(x)]}}\,dx $$

where $V(x)$ is the potential energy function. In particular, if $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2(x^2 - bx^4)$, show that the period for oscillations of amplitude $a$ is:

$$ \tau = \frac{2}{\omega_0}\int_{-a}^a \frac{dx}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\sqrt{1 - b(a^2 + x^2)}} $$

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The particle moves along the axis $Ox$ of an inertial frame under a conservative force, so its mechanical energy is constant and the velocity is a known function of the position. The time between two positions is then a quadrature: this is the method that Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44 applies to the pendulum and [Lecture 6, Slide 7] to the harmonic oscillator. The second part applies the general formula to an anharmonic potential.

Duration of the outward leg Law: change of the variable of integration from $t$ to $x$; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44; [Lecture 6, Slide 7]

The particle leaves $x_1$ at the instant $t_A$ and reaches $x_2$ at $t_B$.

$$\begin{aligned} t_B - t_A &= \int_{t_A}^{t_B} dt \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule: antiderivative $t$)} \\ &= \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dx}{\dot x} \quad &\text{(change of variable, $x(t_A) = x_1$, $x(t_B) = x_2$)} \\ &= \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]/m}} \quad &\text{(positive root on this leg)} \\ &= \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]}}\,dx \quad &\text{(the reciprocal of a square root is the square root of the reciprocal)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{t_B - t_A = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]}}\,dx}$$

Meaning: each element of length is crossed in the time length over speed; summing them gives the duration of the leg.

Duration of the return leg Law: change of variable with the negative root; reversal of the limits of a definite integral Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44

The particle leaves $x_2$ at $t_B$ and is back at $x_1$ at $t_C$.

$$\begin{aligned} t_C - t_B &= \int_{x_2}^{x_1}\frac{dx}{\dot x} \quad &\text{(change of variable, $x(t_B) = x_2$, $x(t_C) = x_1$)} \\ &= \int_{x_2}^{x_1}\frac{dx}{-\sqrt{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]/m}} \quad &\text{(negative root on this leg)} \\ &= \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]/m}} \quad &\text{(reversing the limits changes the sign)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{t_C - t_B = t_B - t_A}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T}]$

Meaning: the way back takes as long as the way out: at every point the speed is the same on both legs, only the sense is opposite.

Period of the oscillation Law: periodicity of the motion; sum of the two legs Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44; [Lecture 6, Slide 7]

At $t_C$ the particle is again at $x_1$ with zero velocity, the same state as at $t_A$; the equation of motion is of second order, so the same state is followed by the same motion and the time $t_C - t_A$ is the period.

$$\begin{aligned} \tau &= t_C - t_A = \left(t_C - t_B\right) + \left(t_B - t_A\right) \quad &\text{(the period is the sum of the two legs)} \\ &= 2\left(t_B - t_A\right) \quad &\text{(the two legs last the same)} \\ &= 2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]}}\,dx \quad &\text{(duration of the outward leg)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\tau = 2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]}}\,dx}$$

Meaning: the first formula of the statement is proved; it holds for any potential with two simple turning points.

$$\begin{aligned} V(x_2) - V(x) &\approx k_2\left(x_2 - x\right) \quad &\text{(first-order expansion about $x_2$)} \\ \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(x_2) - V(x)\right]}} &\approx \sqrt{\frac{m}{2k_2}}\left(x_2 - x\right)^{-1/2} \quad &\text{(integrand near $x_2$)} \\ \int_{x_2 - \delta}^{x_2}\left(x_2 - x\right)^{-1/2}dx &= \left[-2\left(x_2 - x\right)^{1/2}\right]_{x_2 - \delta}^{x_2} \quad &\text{(antiderivative; check: its derivative is the integrand)} \\ &= -2\cdot 0 - \left(-2\sqrt\delta\right) = 2\sqrt\delta \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\text{the period integral is finite at a simple turning point}}$$

Meaning: although the particle stops at the turning points, it spends only a finite time near them; the same holds at $x_1$.

Meaning: for $b$ positive the potential has two maxima at $x^2$ equal to $1/(2b)$; only amplitudes below them give an oscillation. For $b$ zero or negative every amplitude does.

$$\begin{aligned} V(a) - V(x) &= \frac12 m\omega_0^2\left[\left(a^2 - x^2\right) - b\left(a^4 - x^4\right)\right] \quad &\text{(difference of the two values, grouped by powers)} \\ a^4 - x^4 &= \left(a^2 - x^2\right)\left(a^2 + x^2\right) \quad &\text{(difference of squares)} \\ V(a) - V(x) &= \frac12 m\omega_0^2\left(a^2 - x^2\right)\left[1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)\right] \quad &\text{(common factor $a^2 - x^2$)} \\ \frac{m}{2\left[V(a) - V(x)\right]} &= \frac{m}{m\omega_0^2\left(a^2 - x^2\right)\left[1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)\right]} = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2\left(a^2 - x^2\right)\left[1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)\right]} \quad &\text{(the mass cancels)} \\ \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\left[V(a) - V(x)\right]}} &= \frac{1}{\omega_0\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,\sqrt{1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)}} \quad &\text{(both brackets positive for $\lvert x\rvert \lt a$: for $b \gt 0$, $b(a^2+x^2) \lt 2ba^2 \lt 1$; for $b \le 0$ the bracket is at least 1)} \\ \tau &= 2\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{dx}{\omega_0\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,\sqrt{1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)}} \quad &\text{(period formula with $x_1 = -a$, $x_2 = a$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\tau = \frac{2}{\omega_0}\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,\sqrt{1 - b\left(a^2 + x^2\right)}}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T}]$: the integral is a length over a length, dimensionless, and $1/\omega_0$ is a time

Meaning: the second formula of the statement is proved; the factor with $b$ measures how far the oscillation is from harmonic.

Figure 2. For $b > 0$ the potential lies below the harmonic parabola, so the particle moves more slowly and the period grows with the amplitude; oscillations need $2ba^2 < 1$..
Harmonic limit: the period does not depend on the amplitude Law: trigonometric substitution in a definite integral; Barrow's rule Source: [Lecture 6, Slide 7]; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.13

For $b$ equal to zero the potential is that of the harmonic oscillator with stiffness $m\omega_0^2$. Substitute $x = a\sin\varphi$: when $x$ runs from $-a$ to $a$, $\varphi$ runs from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$, where $\cos\varphi$ is not negative.

$$\begin{aligned} dx &= a\cos\varphi\,d\varphi \quad &\text{(differential of the substitution)} \\ \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} &= \sqrt{a^2\left(1 - \sin^2\varphi\right)} = a\cos\varphi \quad &\text{(Pythagorean identity; $\cos\varphi \ge 0$ on the interval)} \\ \int_{-a}^{a}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} &= \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{a\cos\varphi\,d\varphi}{a\cos\varphi} = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\varphi \quad &\text{(substitution with the new limits)} \\ &= \left[\varphi\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{\pi}{2} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \pi \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \\ \tau &= \frac{2}{\omega_0}\,\pi \quad &\text{(the second formula with $b$ equal to zero)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{b = 0:\quad \tau = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T}]$

Meaning: the result of the course for the harmonic oscillator (isochronous: independent of $a$) is recovered.

Effect of the amplitude: first-order correction Law: same substitution in the exact integral; first-order expansion of a power for small anharmonicity; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.44; [Lecture 6, Slide 7]

With the same substitution, $a^2 + x^2$ becomes $a^2(1 + \sin^2\varphi)$. Write $\epsilon$ for $b\,a^2(1 + \sin^2\varphi)$, small when $b\,a^2$ is small.

$$\begin{aligned} \tau &= \frac{2}{\omega_0}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - b\,a^2\left(1 + \sin^2\varphi\right)}} \quad &\text{(exact; the factor $a\cos\varphi$ cancels as before)} \\ \left(1 - \epsilon\right)^{-1/2} &\approx 1 + \frac{\epsilon}{2} \quad &\text{(first-order expansion, derivative of the power at $\epsilon = 0$ equal to $1/2$)} \\ \int\cos 2\varphi\,d\varphi &= \frac12\int\cos u\,du = \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{2} \quad &\text{(substitution $u = 2\varphi$, $du = 2\,d\varphi$)} \\ \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin^2\varphi\,d\varphi &= \left[\frac{\varphi}{2} - \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{4}\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \left(\frac{\pi}{4} - 0\right) - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4} - 0\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad &\text{(antiderivative from $\sin^2\varphi = (1 - \cos 2\varphi)/2$; Barrow's rule)} \\ \tau &\approx \frac{2}{\omega_0}\left[\pi + \frac{b\,a^2}{2}\left(\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right] \quad &\text{(term by term, with $\int d\varphi = \pi$)} \\ &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}\left(1 + \frac{3}{4}\,b\,a^2\right) \quad &\text{(common factor $\pi$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\tau \approx \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}\left(1 + \frac34\,b\,a^2\right)\quad (b\,a^2 \ll 1)}$$

Units: $[b\,a^2] = [1]$, so $[\tau] = [\mathrm{T}]$

Meaning: for $b$ positive (a softening force, weaker than the linear one far from $O$) the period grows with the amplitude and tends to infinity as $2b\,a^2$ tends to 1, where the turning points reach the maxima of the potential; for $b$ negative (hardening) it decreases.

Problem 13

Conservative Force and Potential IV

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 9
Quadratures & Trajectory

A particle of mass $m$ moves along the $Ox$ axis under the potential:

$$ V(x) = mgx\left[\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^2 - 3\right] $$

Compute the evolution of the particle as a function of time, when it is released from $x_0 = a$ with an initial velocity $v_0 = \sqrt{8ga}$ directed along the positive $Ox$ axis.

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

A particle of mass $m$ moves along the axis $Ox$ of an inertial frame under a force that derives from the potential of the statement. The motion is one-dimensional, so the mechanical energy is a first integral and the time law follows from one quadrature, as in Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.5 (one-dimensional conservative motion). The integral given in the tip of the statement is used, and it is verified by differentiation before it is used.

Hypotheses and degrees of freedom:

  • The particle is kept on the axis $Ox$ by a smooth guide (or moves on it freely). Change of Basis and Position Vector

Only one basis is needed: $\mathcal B_0$ is fixed in the inertial frame. The boxes below state this as a (trivial) change of basis, give the position vector and the derivatives of the unit vectors from the angular velocity of the basis.

Basis of the problem and its change of basis matrix Law: definition of the change of basis matrix; orthogonality and unit determinant Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.14; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15

The motion takes place on one fixed straight line, so the basis in which the position is written is the fixed basis itself: every column of the matrix is a fixed unit vector written in $\mathcal B_0$.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf i &= 1\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(first column)} \\ \mathbf j &= 0\,\mathbf i + 1\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(second column)} \\ \mathbf k &= \mathbf i \times \mathbf j \quad &\text{(right-handed basis; $\mathbf k$ points out of the paper ⊙ in the figure)} \\ \det[{}_0R_0] &= 1\cdot(1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0) = 1 \quad &\text{(expansion along the first row)} \\ [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T &= I\,I = I \quad &\text{(product of the identity with itself)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_0] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det[{}_0R_0] = 1,\qquad [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T = I}$$

Units: $[1]$ for every entry

Meaning: no rotation is involved; every vector of the problem has one component, along $\mathbf i$.

Position vector and dimensionless coordinate Law: definition of the position vector in the fixed basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.2; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.5
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P &= \overrightarrow{OP} \quad &\text{(definition of the position vector)} \\ &= x\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(the particle stays on the axis $Ox$)} \\ &= a\,y\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{(dimensionless coordinate, $x$ is $a$ times $y$)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: the initial position is the point $x = a$, which turns out to be the bottom of the potential well.

Derivatives of the unit vectors Law: derivative of a unit vector of constant magnitude, $\dot{\mathbf u} = \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf u$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62

The basis $\mathcal B_0$ does not rotate with respect to $S_0$, so its angular velocity is the zero vector.

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} &= 0\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(fixed basis)} \\ \dot{\mathbf i} &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf i = \mathbf 0 \times \mathbf i = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(every component of $\boldsymbol\omega$ is zero)} \\ \dot{\mathbf j} &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf j = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(same reason)} \\ \dot{\mathbf k} &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf k = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(same reason)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: only the coordinate changes in time; the unit vectors are constants.

Velocity and acceleration of the particle Law: definitions of velocity and acceleration in $S_0$; product rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.3; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7

Conservative force from the potential Law: force of a one-dimensional potential, minus the derivative of the potential; chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.16; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.15
$$\begin{aligned} V &= mgx\left(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - 3\right) = mg\left(\frac{x^3}{a^2} - 3x\right) \quad &\text{(potential of the statement, expanded)} \\ \frac{dV}{dx} &= mg\left(\frac{3x^2}{a^2} - 3\right) \quad &\text{(derivative of a power, term by term)} \\ F_x &= -\frac{dV}{dx} = -3mg\left(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - 1\right) \quad &\text{(force from the potential)} \\ &= -3mg\left(y^2 - 1\right) \quad &\text{(dimensionless coordinate)} \\ V &= mga\left(y^3 - 3y\right) \quad &\text{(the potential in terms of $y$: $x^3/a^2$ is $a\,y^3$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf F = -3mg\left(y^2 - 1\right)\mathbf i,\qquad V = mga\left(y^3 - 3y\right)}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$ for the force; $[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ for the potential

Meaning: between the points $x = -a$ and $x = a$ the force pushes toward $+\mathbf i$; outside that interval it pushes toward $-\mathbf i$.

Equilibrium points and their stability Law: equilibrium where the force vanishes; stable at a minimum of the potential, unstable at a maximum Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.32; Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.6; [Lecture 2, Slide 22]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dV}{dy} &= mga\left(3y^2 - 3\right) = 3mga\left(y^2 - 1\right) \quad &\text{(derivative term by term)} \\ 3mga\left(y^2 - 1\right) &= 0 \iff y = 1 \ \text{or}\ y = -1 \quad &\text{(difference of squares, $(y - 1)(y + 1)$)} \\ \frac{d^2V}{dy^2} &= 6mga\,y \quad &\text{(second derivative)} \\ V(1) &= mga(1 - 3) = -2mga,\quad \left.\frac{d^2V}{dy^2}\right|_{1} = 6mga \gt 0 \quad &\text{(local minimum: stable)} \\ V(-1) &= mga(-1 + 3) = 2mga,\quad \left.\frac{d^2V}{dy^2}\right|_{-1} = -6mga \lt 0 \quad &\text{(local maximum: unstable)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{x = a:\ \text{stable, } V = -2mga;\qquad x = -a:\ \text{unstable, } V = 2mga}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ for every value of the potential

Meaning: the particle starts at the bottom of a well whose left wall ends at the barrier top $x = -a$; to the right the potential grows without bound.

Equation of motion Law: Newton's second law in the inertial frame, projected on $\mathbf i$, $\mathbf j$ and $\mathbf k$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.21
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf a_0^P &= \mathbf F + \mathbf R_\perp \quad &\text{(Newton's second law; $\mathbf R_\perp = R_y\,\mathbf j + R_z\,\mathbf k$)} \\ 0 &= R_y, \qquad 0 = R_z \quad &\text{(dot products with $\mathbf j$ and $\mathbf k$)} \\ m\,a\,\ddot y &= -3mg\left(y^2 - 1\right) \quad &\text{(projection on $\mathbf i$, with the kinematics above)} \\ \ddot y &= -\frac{3g}{a}\left(y^2 - 1\right) \quad &\text{(division by $m\,a$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\ddot y + \frac{3g}{a}\left(y^2 - 1\right) = 0,\qquad y(0) = 1,\quad \dot y(0) = \sqrt{8g/a}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-2}}]$ on both sides

Meaning: a non-linear equation of second order; it is integrated once with the energy, then by quadrature.

Mechanical energy of the motion Law: conservation of mechanical energy of a particle under a conservative force Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.26; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + V(x)\right) &= \dot x\left(m\ddot x + \frac{dV}{dx}\right) = 0 \quad &\text{(chain rule on both terms; equation of motion)} \\ E &= \tfrac{1}{2}m\dot x^2 + V(x) \quad &\text{(definition: kinetic plus potential energy, constant)} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}m(8ga) + mga\left(1^3 - 3\cdot 1\right) \quad &\text{(initial state, $y$ equal to 1)} \\ &= 4mga - 2mga = 2mga \quad &\text{(arithmetic)} \\ E - V(-a) &= 2mga - 2mga = 0 \quad &\text{(comparison with the barrier top)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{E = 2mga = V(-a)}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the energy equals exactly the height of the barrier: the motion is the separatrix, and the particle can reach the top of the barrier only in the limit of infinite time.

First integral in factorised form Law: energy equation solved for the squared speed; factorisation of a cubic by one known root Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28
$$\begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2}m a^2\dot y^2 &= E - V = 2mga - mga\left(y^3 - 3y\right) \quad &\text{(energy equation with $\dot x$ equal to $a\dot y$)} \\ \dot y^2 &= \frac{2g}{a}\left(2 + 3y - y^3\right) \quad &\text{(multiplication by $2/(m a^2)$)} \\ y^3 - 3y - 2\big|_{y = -1} &= -1 + 3 - 2 = 0 \quad &\text{(so $y + 1$ is a factor)} \\ (y + 1)(y^2 - y - 2) &= y^3 - y^2 - 2y + y^2 - y - 2 = y^3 - 3y - 2 \quad &\text{(the quotient, checked by expansion)} \\ y^2 - y - 2 &= (y + 1)(y - 2) \quad &\text{(roots $-1$ and $2$ of the quadratic)} \\ 2 + 3y - y^3 &= -(y + 1)^2(y - 2) = (y + 1)^2(2 - y) \quad &\text{(change of sign of the cubic)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot y^2 = \frac{2g}{a}\,(y + 1)^2\,(2 - y)}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-2}}]$ on both sides

Meaning: the allowed region is every $y$ up to 2, past the barrier top too; $x = 2a$ is a simple root (a turning point reached in finite time), while $x = -a$ is a double root: the speed vanishes there, but a particle coming from the right reaches it only as $t \to \infty$, so it never goes beyond it.

Verification of the integral of the tip Law: an antiderivative is checked by differentiating it; chain rule Source: Problems.pdf, p. 9

Let $u$ be the square root of the quantity $(2 - y)/3$. For $y$ between $-1$ and $2$ it lies between 0 and 1, where the inverse hyperbolic tangent is defined.

$$\begin{aligned} u^2 &= \frac{2 - y}{3} \quad &\text{(definition of $u$)} \\ 2u\,du &= -\frac{dy}{3} \implies \frac{du}{dy} = -\frac{1}{6u} \quad &\text{(differential of both sides)} \\ \frac{d}{dy}\left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{arctanh}u\right] &= -\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\,\frac{1}{1 - u^2}\,\frac{du}{dy} \quad &\text{(chain rule; the derivative of arctanh is $1/(1 - u^2)$)} \\ &= \frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\,\frac{1}{1 - u^2}\,\frac{1}{6u} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt 3\,u\,(1 - u^2)} \quad &\text{(substitution of $du/dy$)} \\ 1 - u^2 &= \frac{3 - 2 + y}{3} = \frac{y + 1}{3} \quad &\text{(definition of $u$)} \\ 3\sqrt 3\,u\,(1 - u^2) &= 3\sqrt 3\,\frac{\sqrt{2 - y}}{\sqrt 3}\,\frac{y + 1}{3} = (y + 1)\sqrt{2 - y} \quad &\text{(product of the factors)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\int\frac{dy}{(y + 1)\sqrt{2 - y}} = -\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{arctanh}\sqrt{\frac{2 - y}{3}} + C}$$

Units: dimensionless

Meaning: the tip of the statement is correct on the whole allowed interval of $y$ between $-1$ and $2$.

Outgoing leg: from the bottom of the well to the turning point Law: separation of variables and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Problems.pdf, p. 9

On this leg the velocity is positive (it starts positive and can change sign only where the right-hand side of the first integral vanishes), and the factor $y + 1$ is positive, so the positive square root is taken. $\lambda$ and $\alpha$ are defined in the table.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dt} &= \sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}}\,(y + 1)\sqrt{2 - y} \quad &\text{(positive root of the first integral)} \\ \int_1^{y}\frac{dy'}{(y' + 1)\sqrt{2 - y'}} &= \sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}}\int_0^{t}dt' \quad &\text{(separation of variables, from $y(0)$ equal to 1)} \\ \left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{arctanh}\sqrt{\frac{2 - y'}{3}}\right]_1^{y} &= \sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}}\,\bigl[t'\bigr]_0^{t} \quad &\text{(antiderivatives)} \\ -\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{arctanh}\sqrt{\frac{2 - y}{3}} + \frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{arctanh}\frac{1}{\sqrt 3} &= \sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}}\,(t - 0) \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule; at $y'$ equal to 1 the root is $1/\sqrt 3$)} \\ \operatorname{arctanh}\sqrt{\frac{2 - y}{3}} &= -\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}}\,t + \operatorname{arctanh}\frac{1}{\sqrt 3} \quad &\text{(multiplication by $-\sqrt 3/2$)} \\ \frac{\sqrt 3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2g}{a}} &= \sqrt{\frac{3\cdot 2g}{4a}} = \sqrt{\frac{3g}{2a}} = \lambda \quad &\text{(simplification of the rate)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\operatorname{arctanh}\sqrt{\frac{2 - y}{3}} = -\sqrt{\frac{3g}{2a}}\,t + \operatorname{arctanh}\frac{1}{\sqrt 3},\qquad 0 \le t \le t_1}$$

Units: $[\lambda\,t] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = [1]$; both sides are dimensionless

Meaning: as time grows the left side decreases from its initial value toward zero: the particle climbs the right wall of the well and slows down.

Time to reach the turning point Law: the speed vanishes at the right end of the allowed region Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28
$$\begin{aligned} 2 - y(t_1) &= 0 \implies x(t_1) = 2a \quad &\text{(simple root of the first integral)} \\ \operatorname{arctanh}0 &= 0 = -\lambda\,t_1 + \alpha \quad &\text{(outgoing leg at $t_1$)} \\ t_1 &= \frac{\alpha}{\lambda} = \sqrt{\frac{2a}{3g}}\operatorname{arctanh}\frac{1}{\sqrt 3} \quad &\text{(solution for $t_1$)} \\ \alpha &= \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 + 1/\sqrt 3}{1 - 1/\sqrt 3} = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(2 + \sqrt 3\right) \approx 0.6585 \quad &\text{(logarithmic form of arctanh; $(\sqrt3 + 1)^2/2$ is $2 + \sqrt 3$)} \\ F_x(2a) &= -3mg\left(2^2 - 1\right) = -9mg \lt 0 \quad &\text{(force at the turning point)} \end{aligned}$$

Problem 14

Conservative Force and Potential V

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 9
Types of Motion

A particle of mass $m$ is moving along the $Ox$ axis under the force $\mathbf{F}(x) = mK x e^{x/a}\mathbf{i}$, where $K$ and $a$ are known positive constants. The initial position of the particle is $x = a$ and the initial velocity is $\mathbf{v} = -v_0\mathbf{i}$. Describe the possible types of motion of the particle as a function of $v_0$.

Worked Solution [ Click to Expand ]

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The particle moves along the axis $Ox$ of an inertial frame under a force that depends only on its abscissa, so the force is conservative and the mechanical energy is constant. The types of motion follow from the shape of the potential and from the level of the energy fixed by the initial velocity (Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.5 and Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.6; [Lecture 2, Slide 20]).

Equation of motion Law: Newton's second law in an inertial frame, projected on $\mathcal B_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.5

Potential energy by integration by parts Law: potential of a one-dimensional force, defined by its work; integration by parts and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.16; [Lecture 2, Slide 14]

The force depends only on $x$, so the potential exists. The reference is the initial point, $V(a) = 0$. In the integral take the factor $x'$ to differentiate and the exponential to integrate: the exponential is integrated with the substitution of the next rows.

$$\begin{aligned} V(x) &= -\int_a^x mK x'\,e^{x'/a}\,dx' \quad &\text{(definition of the potential, reference at $x' = a$)} \\ \int_a^x a\,e^{x'/a}\,dx' &= a^2\int_1^{x/a} e^{u}\,du = a^2\left[e^u\right]_1^{x/a} \quad &\text{(substitution $u = x'/a$, $du = dx'/a$; limits $1$ and $x/a$)} \\ \int_a^x x'\,e^{x'/a}\,dx' &= \left[a\,x'\,e^{x'/a}\right]_a^x - \int_a^x a\,e^{x'/a}\,dx' \quad &\text{(integration by parts; $a\,e^{x'/a}$ has derivative $e^{x'/a}$ by the chain rule)} \\ &= \left(a\,x\,e^{x/a} - a^2 e\right) - a^2\left[e^u\right]_1^{x/a} \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule on the first term; the substitution above for the second)} \\ &= a\,x\,e^{x/a} - a^2 e - a^2 e^{x/a} + a^2 e = a\left(x - a\right)e^{x/a} \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule; the constants cancel)} \\ V(x) &= -mKa\left(x - a\right)e^{x/a} = mKa\left(a - x\right)e^{x/a} \quad &\text{(times $-mK$)} \\ -\frac{dV}{dx} &= -mKa\left[-e^{x/a} + \left(a - x\right)\frac{1}{a}e^{x/a}\right] = mK x\,e^{x/a} \quad &\text{(check by the product and chain rules)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{V(x) = mKa\left(a - x\right)e^{x/a}}$$

Meaning: the potential is positive to the left of $a$ and negative to its right; its derivative returns the force of the statement.

Shape of the potential: the barrier at the origin Law: equilibrium where the force vanishes; stability from the second derivative; limits at infinity Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.32; Notes.pdf, Sec. 4.6; [Lecture 2, Slide 22]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dV}{dx} &= -mK x\,e^{x/a} = 0 \iff x = 0 \quad &\text{(the exponential never vanishes)} \\ \frac{dV}{dx} &\gt 0 \ \text{for}\ x \lt 0, \qquad \frac{dV}{dx} \lt 0 \ \text{for}\ x \gt 0 \quad &\text{(sign of minus $x$; increasing, then decreasing)} \\ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} &= -mK\left(e^{x/a} + x\,\frac{1}{a}e^{x/a}\right) = -mK\,e^{x/a}\left(1 + \frac{x}{a}\right) \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule on the exponential)} \\ V(0) &= mKa^2, \qquad \left.\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}\right|_0 = -mK \lt 0 \quad &\text{(a maximum: unstable equilibrium, the barrier)} \\ \lim_{x\to-\infty}V &= \lim_{u\to+\infty} mKa^2\left(1 + u\right)e^{-u} = 0^+ \quad &\text{(with $u = -x/a$; the exponential decays faster than any polynomial grows)} \\ \lim_{x\to+\infty}V &= -\infty \quad &\text{(the factor $a - x$ tends to $-\infty$ and the exponential to $+\infty$)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: a single barrier of height $mKa^2$ at the origin; to its left the potential stays positive and decays to zero, to its right it falls without bound.

Answers and Checks

Case 1: initial speed below the critical one Law: turning point where the potential equals the energy; direction of the force there Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.16

On the segment from $0$ to $a$ the potential is continuous and strictly decreasing, from $mKa^2$ down to $0$; the energy lies strictly between these two values, so the potential takes the value $E$ at exactly one point $x^*$ of the segment.

$$\begin{aligned} 0 \lt v_0 \lt a\sqrt{2K} &\Rightarrow 0 \lt E \lt mKa^2 = V(0) \quad &\text{(energy below the barrier top)} \\ mKa\left(a - x^*\right)e^{x^*/a} &= \frac12 m v_0^2 \iff \left(1 - \frac{x^*}{a}\right)e^{x^*/a} = \frac{v_0^2}{2Ka^2} \quad &\text{(turning point; division by $mKa^2$)} \\ F_x(x^*) &= mK x^*\,e^{x^*/a} \gt 0 \quad &\text{(at the turning point the force points to $+\mathbf i$)} \\ x \gt x^*:\quad \frac12 m\dot x^2 &= E - V(x) \gt E - V(x^*) = 0 \quad &\text{(the potential decreases to the right of $x^*$: no other stop)} \\ x \ge a:\quad \dot x &\ge v_0 \quad &\text{(there the potential is not positive)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_0 \lt a\sqrt{2K}:\ \text{stops at } x^* \in (0, a),\ \text{turns back and moves towards } +\infty}$$

Units: both sides of the turning-point equation are dimensionless

Meaning: the particle decelerates before reaching the origin, is reflected by the barrier and then runs away to the right faster and faster, since the potential falls without bound.

Case 2: initial speed equal to the critical one Law: energy integral; Taylor expansion of the potential with remainder about the barrier top; separation of variables and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; [Lecture 6, Slide 15]

Now the energy equals the barrier top. On the segment from $0$ to $a$ the potential is below $V(0)$ except at $0$, so the velocity stays negative and does not vanish before the origin. Expand the potential about $0$ with the remainder at an intermediate point $\xi$ between $0$ and $x$; since $\xi$ lies between $0$ and $a$, the factor $e^{\xi/a}(1 + \xi/a)$ is at most $2e$.

$$\begin{aligned} V(0) - V(x) &= -V'(0)\,x - \frac12 V''(\xi)\,x^2 = \frac12 mK\,e^{\xi/a}\left(1 + \frac{\xi}{a}\right)x^2 \quad &\text{(Taylor with remainder; $V'(0) = 0$)} \\ &\le e\,mK\,x^2 \quad &\text{(bound of the factor)} \\ \dot x^2 &= \frac{2}{m}\left[V(0) - V(x)\right] \le 2eK\,x^2 \quad &\text{(energy integral with $E = V(0)$)} \\ t(x) &= \int_x^a\frac{dx'}{\lvert\dot x\rvert} \ge \frac{1}{\sqrt{2eK}}\int_x^a\frac{dx'}{x'} \quad &\text{(time from $a$ to $x$; the speed is at most $\sqrt{2eK}\,x'$)} \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2eK}}\left[\ln x'\right]_x^a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2eK}}\ln\frac{a}{x} \quad &\text{(antiderivative; Barrow's rule)} \\ \lim_{x\to 0^+}t(x) &= +\infty \quad &\text{(the logarithm grows without bound)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_0 = a\sqrt{2K}:\ \text{the particle tends to } x = 0 \text{ but needs an infinite time to reach it}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-2}}]^{-1/2} = [\mathrm{T}]$ for the factor in front of the logarithm

Meaning: the particle creeps up to the unstable equilibrium at the barrier top. The linearised equation there, $\ddot x$ equal to $K x$, gives the exponential approach $x$ proportional to $e^{-\sqrt K\,t}$ on the separatrix.

Case 3: initial speed above the critical one Law: energy integral; lower bound of the speed Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.28

For every $x$ up to $a$ the potential is at most its maximum $V(0)$.

$$\begin{aligned} v_0 \gt a\sqrt{2K} &\Rightarrow E \gt V(0) \ge V(x)\ \text{for}\ x \le a \quad &\text{(energy above the barrier top)} \\ \dot x^2 &= v_0^2 - \frac{2V(x)}{m} \ge v_0^2 - 2Ka^2 \gt 0 \quad &\text{(energy integral and the bound on the potential)} \\ \dot x(x = 0) &= -\sqrt{v_0^2 - 2Ka^2} \quad &\text{(at the barrier top; negative sign kept from the start)} \\ \lim_{x\to-\infty}\dot x^2 &= v_0^2 - 0 = v_0^2 \quad &\text{(the potential tends to zero on the left)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_0 \gt a\sqrt{2K}:\ \text{crosses } x = 0 \text{ with speed } \sqrt{v_0^2 - 2Ka^2} \text{ and moves towards } -\infty,\ \lvert\dot x\rvert \to v_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Change of Basis and Position Vector

The only basis is $\mathcal B_0$. In the view of the figure $\mathbf i$ points down the page and $\mathbf j$ to the right, so $\mathbf k$, the cross product of $\mathbf i$ and $\mathbf j$ (right-hand rule), points out of the paper (⊙).

The only basis and its change of basis matrix Law: change of basis matrix between two bases; orthonormality and unit determinant Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.14; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15

The matrix that writes $\mathcal B_0$ in itself has as columns the components of $\mathbf i$, $\mathbf j$, $\mathbf k$ in $\mathcal B_0$, so it is the identity; it is checked like any rotation matrix.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf i &= 1\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(first column: components of $\mathbf i$ in $\mathcal B_0$)} \\ \mathbf j &= 0\,\mathbf i + 1\,\mathbf j + 0\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(second column)} \\ \mathbf k &= 0\,\mathbf i + 0\,\mathbf j + 1\,\mathbf k \quad &\text{(third column)} \\ \det[{}_0R_0] &= 1\cdot(1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 0) - 0 + 0 = 1 \quad &\text{(expansion along the first row)} \\ [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T &= I\,I = I \quad &\text{(the identity is symmetric and equal to its square)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_0] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \cr 0 & 1 & 0 \cr 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det[{}_0R_0] = 1, \quad [{}_0R_0][{}_0R_0]^T = I}$$

Units: every entry is a direction cosine, dimensionless: $[1]$

Meaning: no rotation is involved; $\mathcal B_0$ is orthonormal and right-handed, and the weight has a positive component along its first axis.

Kinematics

Velocity and acceleration of the ball Law: velocity and acceleration as time derivatives of the position vector in $S_0$; derivative of a unit vector of a basis that does not rotate Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7

Forces and Equations of Motion

The free-body diagram shows the weight and the drag, drawn for a ball that moves down. The ball is free, so no reaction acts, and $S_0$ is inertial, so no inertia force acts.

Figure 2. Forces on the ball while it falls. The weight acts along $\mathbf i$ and the drag, opposite to the downward velocity, along $-\mathbf i$ with magnitude $\tfrac12\rho S C_D v^2$; they balance at the terminal speed..
Forces on the ball Law: weight in a uniform gravity field; quadratic drag opposite to the velocity relative to the air Source: Problems.pdf, p. 9; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4

The drag points along minus the unit vector of the velocity. The sign box below proves that the velocity component is positive for every positive time, so that unit vector is $\mathbf i$.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf F_g &= m\,g\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{(gravity points down, along $+\mathbf i$)} \\ \mathbf F_D &= -D\,\frac{\mathbf v_0^P}{|\mathbf v_0^P|} = -\tfrac12\rho v^2 S C_D\,\frac{v\,\mathbf i}{|v|} \quad &\text{(drag opposite to the velocity; law of the statement)} \\ &= -\tfrac12\rho S C_D\,v^2\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{($v \gt 0$ during the fall, so $v/|v| = 1$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf F_g = m g\,\mathbf i, \qquad \mathbf F_D = -\tfrac12\rho S C_D\,v^2\,\mathbf i}$$

Units: $\mathbf F_g$: $[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$; $\mathbf F_D$: $[\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}][\mathrm{L^2}][1][\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the weight is constant; the drag grows with the square of the speed and points up while the ball falls.

Newton's second law projected on the fixed basis Law: Newton's second law in an inertial frame; projection on $\mathcal B_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.5

The ball is free: the constraint reaction is the zero vector. In a general motion the drag would be minus the factor $\tfrac12\rho S C_D$ times the speed times the velocity vector, so its horizontal components are proportional to the horizontal rates.

$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf a_0^P &= \mathbf F_g + \mathbf F_D + \mathbf R, \quad \mathbf R = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(second law in $S_0$; no constraint)} \\ \mathbf j, \mathbf k:\quad m\,\ddot y &= -\tfrac12\rho S C_D\,|\mathbf v_0^P|\,\dot y, \qquad m\,\ddot z = -\tfrac12\rho S C_D\,|\mathbf v_0^P|\,\dot z \quad &\text{(dot products with $\mathbf j$ and $\mathbf k$)} \\ y(t) &= z(t) = 0 \quad &\text{(zero initial data; the zero functions solve both equations; uniqueness)} \\ \mathbf i:\quad m\,\frac{dv}{dt} &= m g - \tfrac12\rho S C_D\,v^2 \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf i$)} \\ \frac{dv}{dt} &= g - \frac{\rho S C_D}{2m}\,v^2 \quad &\text{(division by $m$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{dv}{dt} = g - \frac{\rho S C_D}{2m}\,v^2, \qquad v(0) = 0, \quad s(0) = 0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$ on both sides: $\frac{[\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}][\mathrm{L^2}]}{[\mathrm{M}]}[\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$; the reaction $\mathbf R$ would be $[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: one first-order equation for the one unknown $v$; the position follows from a second integration.

Terminal speed and the equation written with it Law: the terminal state is the motion with zero acceleration, where the net force vanishes Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Problems.pdf, p. 9
$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= g - \frac{\rho S C_D}{2m}\,v_t^2 \quad &\text{(zero acceleration at the constant speed $v_t$)} \\ v_t^2 &= \frac{2 m g}{\rho S C_D} \quad &\text{(solution for the square of the speed)} \\ v_t &= +\sqrt{\frac{2 m g}{\rho S C_D}} \quad &\text{(positive root: downward velocity)} \\ \frac{\rho S C_D}{2m} &= \frac{g}{v_t^2} \quad &\text{(first line rearranged: $\tfrac{\rho S C_D}{2m}\,v_t^2 = g$, divided by $v_t^2$)} \\ \frac{dv}{dt} &= g - \frac{g}{v_t^2}\,v^2 = \frac{g}{v_t^2}\left(v_t^2 - v^2\right) \quad &\text{(substitution in the equation of motion; common factor)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2 m g}{\rho S C_D}}, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{g}{v_t^2}\left(v_t^2 - v^2\right)}$$

Units: $[v_t] = \left(\frac{[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]}{[\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}][\mathrm{L^2}]}\right)^{1/2} = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; the rate equation $\frac{[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]}{[\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}]}[\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: heavy, small and streamlined balls have a high terminal speed; the acceleration is downward while the speed is below it and vanishes at it.

Sign of the velocity during the fall Law: uniqueness of the solution of a first-order differential equation with a smooth right-hand side Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.6; [Lecture 6, Slide 3]

The constant functions equal to $v_t$ and to minus $v_t$ solve the rate equation (they make the right-hand side vanish). Two different solutions cannot cross, so a solution that starts between them stays between them.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dv}{dt}\Big|_{t = 0} &= \frac{g}{v_t^2}\left(v_t^2 - 0\right) = g \gt 0 \quad &\text{(initial condition $v(0) = 0$)} \\ -v_t \lt v(t) &\lt v_t \quad &\text{(no crossing of the two constant solutions)} \\ \frac{dv}{dt} &= \frac{g}{v_t^2}\left(v_t^2 - v^2\right) \gt 0 \quad &\text{(positive right-hand side inside that interval)} \\ 0 &\lt v(t) \lt v_t \quad \text{for } t \gt 0 \quad &\text{(the speed grows from zero)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{0 \lt v(t) \lt v_t \quad \text{for every } t \gt 0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$ for $v$ and $v_t$; $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$ for the initial acceleration

Meaning: the ball always falls, so the drag always points up as written, and the ratio of $v$ to $v_t$ stays inside the interval where the inverse hyperbolic tangent is defined.

Particular solution for the centre of mass

Steady motion of the centre of mass Law: general solution of a linear equation as homogeneous plus particular solution; method of undetermined coefficients for a harmonic forcing Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.7; Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.4; [Lecture 6, Slide 3]; [Lecture 6, Slide 11]

The homogeneous solution is the free oscillation at $\omega_0$ (Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.7); the statement assumes it has died out. The forcing is harmonic at $\omega$, so the particular solution is sought as the same harmonic with an unknown amplitude, for $\omega \neq \omega_0$.

$$\begin{aligned} x_0^G &= x_h + x_p,\quad x_h = C_1\cos\omega_0 t + C_2\sin\omega_0 t \to 0 \quad &\text{(general solution; homogeneous part damped out, hypothesis)} \\ x_p &= a\cos\omega t \quad &\text{(trial function at the forcing frequency)} \\ \dot x_p &= -a\omega\sin\omega t \quad &\text{(chain rule: derivative of $\cos u$ times the derivative of $u = \omega t$)} \\ \ddot x_p &= -a\omega^2\cos\omega t \quad &\text{(chain rule again)} \\ -a\omega^2\cos\omega t + \omega_0^2 a\cos\omega t &= \omega_0^2\ell\cos\omega t \quad &\text{(substitution in the equation of $x_0^G$)} \\ a\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right) &= \omega_0^2\ell \quad &\text{(coefficients of $\cos\omega t$ equated)} \\ a &= \frac{\omega_0^2\ell}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2} \quad &\text{(division, valid for $\omega \neq \omega_0$)} \\ y_p &= b\sin\omega t,\quad \ddot y_p = -b\omega^2\sin\omega t \quad &\text{(same trial for the second component; chain rule twice)} \\ b\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right) &= \omega_0^2\ell \Rightarrow b = a \quad &\text{(coefficients of $\sin\omega t$ equated)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{x_0^G(t) = \frac{\omega_0^2\ell}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\cos\omega t,\qquad y_0^G(t) = \frac{\omega_0^2\ell}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\sin\omega t,\qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}}$$

Meaning: in steady state $G$ describes a circle about $O$ at the rate of the disk, and stays on the line through $O$ along the unbalance direction: the position vector of $G$ is the amplitude times $\mathbf i_1$.

Position vectors of the airplane and of the wheel; length of the gear Law: position vector in Cartesian components; composition of a function of the abscissa with the prescribed motion Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.2; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.5; Problems.pdf, p. 31

Meaning: the wheel rises and falls with the bumps at the angular frequency $\Omega$, which grows with the taxiing speed; the gear is compressed when its length is below $L$.

Kinematics

Velocities and accelerations Law: velocity and acceleration as time derivatives in $S_0$; chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(c\,t\,\mathbf i + y\,\mathbf j\right) = c\,\mathbf i + \dot y\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{(constant $c$ and constant unit vectors)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \ddot y\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{(derivative once more)} \\ \dot y_b &= h\cos\left(\Omega t\right)\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Omega t\right) = h\,\Omega\cos\Omega t \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \dot\ell_s &= \dot y - \dot y_b = \dot y - h\,\Omega\cos\Omega t \quad &\text{(rate of the length of the gear)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = c\,\mathbf i + \dot y\,\mathbf j, \qquad \mathbf a_0^P = \ddot y\,\mathbf j, \qquad \dot y_b = h\,\Omega\cos\Omega t}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$ for $\mathbf v_0^P$, $c$, $\dot y$, $\dot y_b$ and $\dot\ell_s$; $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$ for $\mathbf a_0^P$

Meaning: the horizontal motion is uniform, so all the acceleration is vertical; the wheel moves vertically faster when the airplane taxies faster.

Forces and Equations of Motion

The free-body diagram of $P$ shows the weight, the force of the spring and the force of the damper (both along the gear), and the net horizontal force (engine thrust, acting on the airframe, minus the rolling resistance transmitted by the gear). $S_0$ is inertial, so no inertia force acts.

Figure 2. Forces on the airplane. The spring and the damper act along the vertical gear; the horizontal resultant vanishes because the taxiing speed is constant..
Forces on the airplane Law: linear spring and linear damper along the gear; weight Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.19; [Lecture 6, Slide 5]

The spring pushes $P$ up when it is compressed (length below $L$) and pulls it down when it is stretched; the damper opposes the rate of change of the length of the gear.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf F_g &= -m g\,\mathbf j \quad &\text{(weight)} \\ \mathbf F_k &= -k\left(\ell_s - L\right)\mathbf j = -k\left(y - y_b - L\right)\mathbf j \quad &\text{(spring: minus the stiffness times the elongation)} \\ \mathbf F_\lambda &= -\lambda\,\dot\ell_s\,\mathbf j = -\lambda\left(\dot y - \dot y_b\right)\mathbf j \quad &\text{(damper: minus its constant times the rate of elongation)} \\ \mathbf F_x &= F_x\,\mathbf i \quad &\text{(net horizontal force: engine thrust minus the rolling resistance transmitted by the gear, unknown)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf F_g = -m g\,\mathbf j, \quad \mathbf F_k = -k\left(y - y_b - L\right)\mathbf j, \quad \mathbf F_\lambda = -\lambda\left(\dot y - \dot y_b\right)\mathbf j, \quad \mathbf F_x = F_x\,\mathbf i}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$ for every force: $[k][\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{M\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}]$, $[\lambda][\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,T^{-1}}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the road acts on the airplane only through the gear: through the spring by the position of the wheel and through the damper by its vertical speed.

Newton's second law and the equation about the static equilibrium Law: Newton's second law in an inertial frame; projection on $\mathcal B_0$; change of variable to the static equilibrium Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.5; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.32
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf a_0^P &= \mathbf F_g + \mathbf F_k + \mathbf F_\lambda + \mathbf F_x \quad &\text{(second law with every force)} \\ \mathbf i:\quad 0 &= F_x \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf i$: no horizontal acceleration)} \\ \mathbf j:\quad m\ddot y &= -m g - k\left(y - y_b - L\right) - \lambda\left(\dot y - \dot y_b\right) \quad &\text{(dot product with $\mathbf j$)} \\ y_e &= L - \frac{m g}{k} \quad &\text{(static equilibrium on flat ground: $0 = -m g - k(y_e - L)$)} \\ y &= y_e + z, \quad \dot y = \dot z, \quad \ddot y = \ddot z \quad &\text{(new unknown $z$; $y_e$ is constant)} \\ m\ddot z + \lambda\dot z + k z &= k\,y_b + \lambda\,\dot y_b \quad &\text{(substitution: the weight and $k(y_e - L)$ cancel)} \\ \ddot z + 2\gamma\dot z + \omega_0^2 z &= \omega_0^2 h\sin\Omega t + 2\gamma h\,\Omega\cos\Omega t \quad &\text{(division by $m$; $k/m = \omega_0^2$, $\lambda/m = 2\gamma$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\ddot z + 2\gamma\dot z + \omega_0^2 z = \omega_0^2 h\sin\Omega t + 2\gamma h\,\Omega\cos\Omega t, \qquad y = L - \frac{m g}{k} + z, \qquad F_x = 0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$ for every term of the scalar equation ($[\gamma] = [\Omega] = [\omega_0] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$, $[h] = [\mathrm{L}]$); $[y_e] = [\mathrm{L}]$; $[F_x] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: about its static position the airplane is a damped harmonic oscillator forced at the frequency of the bumps; the weight only lowers the equilibrium by the static deflection $mg/k$.

Answers and Checks

(a) Resonance without damping

Forced response without damping Law: particular solution of the forced oscillator by undetermined coefficients Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.4; [Lecture 6, Slide 3]; [Lecture 6, Slide 11]

With the damping negligible the equation is the undamped one, and the general solution is the free oscillation at $\omega_0$ plus a particular solution at the frequency of the forcing.

$$\begin{aligned} \ddot z + \omega_0^2 z &= \omega_0^2 h\sin\Omega t \quad &\text{(equation with $\gamma = 0$)} \\ z_p &= A\sin\Omega t, \quad \dot z_p = A\Omega\cos\Omega t, \quad \ddot z_p = -A\Omega^2\sin\Omega t \quad &\text{(trial function; chain rule twice)} \\ \left(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2\right)A\sin\Omega t &= \omega_0^2 h\sin\Omega t \quad &\text{(substitution)} \\ A &= \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2} \quad &\text{(coefficients equated, $\Omega \neq \omega_0$)} \\ z &= C_1\cos\omega_0 t + C_2\sin\omega_0 t + A\sin\Omega t \quad &\text{(free plus forced oscillation)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{z_p = \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2}\sin\Omega t, \qquad \Omega = \kappa c}$$

Units: $[A] = [\mathrm{T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}]/[\mathrm{T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L}]$; $C_1$, $C_2$ and $z$ in $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the forced amplitude grows without limit as the frequency of the bumps approaches the natural frequency of the airplane on its gear.

Resonant taxiing speed Law: resonance: the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency; particular solution with a factor $t$ Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.4; [Lecture 6, Slide 3] (trial with a factor $t$, as for a double root; extension of the damped analysis of Sec. 5.4.1)

At equal frequencies the trial of the previous box solves the homogeneous equation and fails; the trial with a factor $t$ is used, with the product rule and the chain rule.

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega &= \omega_0 \Leftrightarrow \kappa c = \sqrt{k/m} \quad &\text{(denominator of the amplitude equal to zero)} \\ z_p &= D\,t\cos\omega_0 t \quad &\text{(trial function with a factor $t$)} \\ \dot z_p &= D\cos\omega_0 t - D\omega_0 t\sin\omega_0 t \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule)} \\ \ddot z_p &= -2D\omega_0\sin\omega_0 t - D\omega_0^2 t\cos\omega_0 t \quad &\text{(the same rules once more)} \\ \ddot z_p + \omega_0^2 z_p &= -2D\omega_0\sin\omega_0 t = \omega_0^2 h\sin\omega_0 t \quad &\text{(the terms in $t\cos\omega_0 t$ cancel)} \\ D &= -\frac{\omega_0 h}{2} \quad &\text{(coefficients of $\sin\omega_0 t$ equated)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{c_{\text{res}} = \frac{1}{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\ \ (\text{numerically } \sqrt{k/m} \text{ in m/s}), \qquad z_p = -\frac{\omega_0 h}{2}\,t\cos\omega_0 t}$$

Units: $[c_{\text{res}}] = [\mathrm{L}][\mathrm{T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[D] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}][\mathrm{L}]$, so $D\,t$ and $z_p$ are in $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: at this speed one bump arrives every natural period of the airplane on its gear, and without damping the oscillation grows linearly in time.

Contact requires the force of the gear on the airplane to stay compressive, that is, to push $P$ upward. Near the undamped resonance the amplitude grows until this condition fails, and from then on the hypothesis of permanent contact breaks down: the wheel would have to pull on the runway.

Permanent contact of the wheel Law: unilateral contact: the runway can only push the wheel; the massless gear transmits the contact force to $P$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.4; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.23; Problems.pdf, p. 31
$$\begin{aligned} N &= k\left(L - \ell_s\right) + \lambda\left(\dot y_b - \dot y\right) \ge 0 \quad &\text{(upward component of $\mathbf F_k + \mathbf F_\lambda$: compressive gear)} \\ \ell_s - L &= y_e + z - y_b - L = z - y_b - \frac{m g}{k} \quad &\text{(box of the position vectors; $y = y_e + z$ with $y_e = L - m g/k$)} \\ N &= m g - k\left(z - y_b\right) - \lambda\left(\dot z - \dot y_b\right) \quad &\text{(substitution; $\dot y = \dot z$ because $y_e$ is constant)} \\ \lambda = 0,\ \Omega = \omega_0:\quad N &= m g - k\left(z_h - \frac{\omega_0 h}{2}\,t\cos\omega_0 t - h\sin\omega_0 t\right) \quad &\text{(undamped resonance: $z = z_h + z_p$ of the previous box)} \\ \frac{\omega_0 h}{2}\,t &\gt \frac{g}{\omega_0^2} + h + \max|z_h| \ \Rightarrow\ N \lt 0 \ \text{where}\ \cos\omega_0 t = -1 \quad &\text{(the secular term exceeds every bounded term; $k/m = \omega_0^2$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{N = m g - k\left(z - y_b\right) - \lambda\left(\dot z - \dot y_b\right) \ge 0 \ \text{required; it fails at undamped resonance once}\ t \gt \frac{2}{\omega_0 h}\left(\frac{g}{\omega_0^2} + h + \max|z_h|\right)}$$

Units: $[N] = [m g] = [k][\mathrm{L}] = [\lambda][\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$; $\left[\frac{g}{\omega_0^2}\right] = [\mathrm{L}]$; $\left[\frac{2}{\omega_0 h}\right][\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{T}]$

Meaning: the static deflection $m g/k$ is the margin of the contact: while the dynamic compression stays below it the wheel keeps touching the runway; at undamped resonance the growing oscillation exhausts this margin in finite time, so the linear model of part (a) is valid only for the first cycles.

(b) Amplitude at very high taxiing speed

Forced amplitude for a very high speed Law: limit of the forced amplitude when the forcing frequency is much larger than the natural one Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.4.1; [Lecture 6, Slide 13]

Without damping the free part keeps the amplitude fixed by the initial conditions and does not depend on the speed; the amplitude asked for is that of the forced part.

$$\begin{aligned} |A| &= \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\left|\omega_0^2 - \kappa^2 c^2\right|} \quad &\text{(previous box with $\Omega = \kappa c$)} \\ &= \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\kappa^2 c^2 - \omega_0^2} = \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\kappa^2 c^2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \omega_0^2/(\kappa^2 c^2)} \quad &\text{($\kappa c \gt \omega_0$; factor $\kappa^2 c^2$ taken out)} \\ &\approx \frac{\omega_0^2 h}{\kappa^2 c^2} = \frac{k\,h}{m\,\kappa^2 c^2} \quad &\text{($\omega_0^2/(\kappa^2 c^2) \ll 1$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{|A| \approx \frac{k\,h}{m\,\kappa^2 c^2}\ \ (\text{numerically } \frac{k}{m c^2} \text{ in metres}), \qquad A \lt 0}$$

Units: $\frac{[\mathrm{M\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}]}{[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L^{-2}}][\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}]} = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the airplane cannot follow fast bumps: its forced amplitude decays as the inverse square of the speed and moves in opposition to the road, as a free mass shaken through a soft spring.

(c) Landing gear with damping

Transient: the three damping regimes Law: characteristic equation of the damped oscillator; underdamped, critical and overdamped solutions Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.22; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.23; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.26; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.30; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.31; [Lecture 6, Slide 8]; [Lecture 6, Slide 9]; [Lecture 6, Slide 10]
$$\begin{aligned} p^2 + 2\gamma p + \omega_0^2 &= 0 \Rightarrow p = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} \quad &\text{(trial $e^{pt}$ in the homogeneous equation)} \\ \gamma \lt \omega_0:\quad z_h &= a\,e^{-\gamma t}\cos\left(\omega t + \phi\right), \quad \omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2} \quad &\text{(underdamped: complex roots)} \\ \gamma = \omega_0:\quad z_h &= \left(a + b\,t\right)e^{-\gamma t} \quad &\text{(critical: double root)} \\ \gamma \gt \omega_0:\quad z_h &= A_+ e^{-\gamma_+ t} + A_- e^{-\gamma_- t}, \quad \gamma_\pm = \gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} \quad &\text{(overdamped: two negative real roots)} \\ \gamma = \frac{\lambda}{2m} \lesseqgtr \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \ &\Leftrightarrow\ \lambda \lesseqgtr 2\sqrt{k m} \quad &\text{(definitions of $\gamma$ and $\omega_0$; multiplication by $2m$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\lambda \lt 2\sqrt{k m}:\ \text{underdamped}; \quad \lambda = 2\sqrt{k m}:\ \text{critical}; \quad \lambda \gt 2\sqrt{k m}:\ \text{overdamped}; \quad z_h \to 0 \text{ in the three cases}}$$

Units: $[p] = [\gamma] = [\gamma_\pm] = [\omega] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$; $a$, $A_\pm$, $z_h$ in $[\mathrm{L}]$, $b$ in $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[\lambda] = [\sqrt{k m}] = [\mathrm{M\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: with any damping the free oscillation dies out: oscillating within the time $1/\gamma$ when underdamped, fastest without oscillation when critical, slowly (time $1/\gamma_-$) when overdamped; afterwards only the forced response remains.

Steady response with damping Law: complex representation of the forced oscillator; the imaginary part of a complex solution is a solution Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.18; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.35; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.37; [Lecture 6, Slide 11]

The forcing is the imaginary part of $(\omega_0^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\gamma\Omega)\,h\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}$, because $\mathrm{Im}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} = \sin\Omega t$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,(\mathrm{i}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}) = \cos\Omega t$. The complex equation is solved and its imaginary part taken.

$$\begin{aligned} \ddot{\tilde z} + 2\gamma\dot{\tilde z} + \omega_0^2\tilde z &= \left(\omega_0^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\gamma\Omega\right)h\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t} \quad &\text{(complex equation)} \\ \tilde z &= H\,h\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}, \quad \dot{\tilde z} = \mathrm{i}\Omega\,\tilde z, \quad \ddot{\tilde z} = -\Omega^2\tilde z \quad &\text{(trial at the forcing frequency; chain rule)} \\ \left(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\gamma\Omega\right)H &= \omega_0^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\gamma\Omega \quad &\text{(substitution; division by $h\,e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}$)} \\ |H|^2 &= \frac{\omega_0^4 + 4\gamma^2\Omega^2}{\left(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2\right)^2 + 4\gamma^2\Omega^2} \quad &\text{(modulus of a quotient: quotient of the moduli)} \\ z_p &= \mathrm{Im}\,\tilde z = |H|\,h\sin\left(\Omega t + \arg H\right) \quad &\text{(imaginary part; $H = |H|e^{\mathrm{i}\arg H}$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{z_p = |H|\,h\sin\left(\Omega t + \arg H\right), \qquad |H| = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_0^4 + 4\gamma^2\Omega^2}{\left(\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2\right)^2 + 4\gamma^2\Omega^2}}, \qquad \Omega = \kappa c}$$

Units: $[\omega_0^4] = [\gamma^2\Omega^2] = [\mathrm{T^{-4}}]$, so $H$ is dimensionless and $z_p$, $\tilde z$ are in $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the steady amplitude is the height of the bumps times the factor $|H|$, finite for every speed; it is the same formula in the three damping regimes, which differ only in the transient.

How the results of (a) and (b) change Law: value of the amplitude factor at resonance, its maximum and its high-speed limit Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.41; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.46; [Lecture 6, Slide 14]

With the ratios $r = \Omega/\omega_0$ and $\zeta = \gamma/\omega_0$ (notation of this solution; critical damping is $\zeta = 1$) and $u = r^2$, the amplitude factor is a function of $u$ whose derivative is taken with the quotient rule. Unlike the force-driven peak of Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.46, which disappears for $\gamma \ge \omega_0/\sqrt2$, the base-excitation factor $|H|$ keeps a maximum for every damping ($u_p \gt 0$); the analysis of $|H|$ is an extension built with the method of Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.4.

$$\begin{aligned} |H|^2 &= \frac{1 + 4\zeta^2 u}{\left(1 - u\right)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u} \quad &\text{(numerator and denominator divided by $\omega_0^4$)} \\ r = 1:\quad |H|^2 &= \frac{1 + 4\zeta^2}{4\zeta^2} = 1 + \frac{1}{4\zeta^2} \quad &\text{(finite at the former resonance)} \\ \frac{d|H|^2}{du} &= \frac{4\zeta^2\left[(1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u\right] - \left(1 + 4\zeta^2 u\right)\left[-2(1 - u) + 4\zeta^2\right]}{\left[(1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u\right]^2} \quad &\text{(quotient rule; chain rule on $(1 - u)^2$)} \\ &= \frac{2\left(1 - u - 2\zeta^2 u^2\right)}{\left[(1 - u)^2 + 4\zeta^2 u\right]^2} \quad &\text{(numerator expanded: the terms in $\zeta^2$, $\zeta^2 u$ and $\zeta^4 u$ cancel)} \\ u_p &= \frac{\sqrt{1 + 8\zeta^2} - 1}{4\zeta^2} \gt 0 \quad &\text{(positive root of $2\zeta^2 u^2 + u - 1 = 0$: the derivative changes from positive to negative there, a maximum)} \\ \left.\left(2\zeta^2 u^2 + u - 1\right)\right|_{u = 0} &= -1, \quad \left.\left(2\zeta^2 u^2 + u - 1\right)\right|_{u = 1} = 2\zeta^2 \gt 0 \ \Rightarrow\ u_p \lt 1 \quad &\text{(change of sign between $u = 0$ and $u = 1$, so its positive root lies below 1)} \\ u = 2:\quad |H|^2 &= \frac{1 + 8\zeta^2}{1 + 8\zeta^2} = 1 \quad &\text{(the same value for every damping)} \\ \Omega \gg \omega_0,\ \Omega \gg \frac{\omega_0^2}{2\gamma}:\quad |H| &\approx \frac{2\gamma\Omega}{\Omega^2} = \frac{2\gamma}{\Omega} = \frac{\lambda}{m\kappa c} \quad &\text{(the damping terms dominate the numerator, $\Omega^4$ the denominator)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{|H(\omega_0)| = \sqrt{1 + \frac{\omega_0^2}{4\gamma^2}}, \qquad \Omega_p = \omega_0\sqrt{u_p} \lt \omega_0, \qquad |H(\sqrt2\,\omega_0)| = 1, \qquad |H| \approx \frac{\lambda}{m\kappa c}\ \text{at very high speed}}$$

Units: $r$, $\zeta$, $u$, $u_p$ and $|H|$ dimensionless; $[\Omega_p] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$; $\frac{[\mathrm{M\,T^{-1}}]}{[\mathrm{M}][\mathrm{L^{-1}}][\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]} = [1]$

Meaning: damping removes the infinite resonance and moves the peak below the natural frequency; below $\sqrt2\,\omega_0$ it reduces the transmitted amplitude, above it increases it, and at very high speed the amplitude decays only as the inverse of the speed, through the force the damper transmits.

Choice of the damping and effect of the payload Law: critical damping gives the shortest response time; dependence of the damping ratio on the mass Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.3.3; Notes.pdf, Eq. 5.23

The choice is a design judgement built on the results above: the critical value kills the transient fastest without oscillation (Notes.pdf, Sec. 5.3.3) and keeps the resonant peak low; more damping would lower the peak only slightly further while transmitting more of the fast bumps. The damper is fixed hardware, so its ratio to the critical value changes with the mass.

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda_c &= 2 m\omega_0 = 2m\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = 2\sqrt{k m} \quad &\text{(critical: $\gamma = \omega_0$, with $\gamma = \lambda/(2m)$)} \\ \zeta &= \frac{\gamma}{\omega_0} = \frac{\lambda}{2m}\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{k m}} \propto m^{-1/2} \quad &\text{(damping ratio of a given damper)} \\ c_{\text{res}} &= \frac{1}{\kappa}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \propto m^{-1/2} \quad &\text{(resonant speed of part (a))} \\ \lambda &= 2\sqrt{k\,m_{\max}} \Rightarrow \zeta = \sqrt{\frac{m_{\max}}{m}} \ge 1 \quad &\text{(damper critical at the largest mass)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\lambda = 2\sqrt{k\,m_{\max}}: \ \text{critical at full load, slightly overdamped for lighter loads}}$$

Units: $[\lambda_c] = [\mathrm{M\,T^{-1}}]$; $\zeta$ dimensionless; $[c_{\text{res}}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $m$ and $m_{\max}$ in $[\mathrm{M}]$

Meaning: yes, the payload matters: a heavier airplane has a lower resonant speed and a smaller damping ratio. A damper sized at the critical value of the largest mass never lets the transient oscillate; a lighter airplane is then overdamped, with a slower return and somewhat more transmission at high speed, which is the price of never being underdamped.
