Problem 01

Rocket Helical Trajectory

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 6
MIT OCW BenchmarkIn-Class Session 2

An out-of-control rocket traces an upward helical trajectory described in Cartesian coordinates for $t > 0$ by:

$$ x = a\cos(\omega t) \tag{1} $$ $$ y = a\sin(\omega t) \tag{2} $$ $$ z = v_z t \tag{3} $$

where $a$, $v_z$, and $\omega$ are constants.

  • (a)
    Express the position, velocity, and acceleration vectors in the Cartesian $x, y, z$ system using unit vectors $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$.
  • (b)
    Express the position, velocity, and acceleration vectors in the cylindrical coordinate system using unit vectors $\mathbf{e}_R, \mathbf{e}_\theta, \mathbf{e}_z$.
  • (c)
    Express the velocity and acceleration vectors using $s, \rho$ and the vector basis $\mathbf{e}_t, \mathbf{e}_n, \mathbf{e}_b$.
  • (d)
    Compute the modulus of the velocity vector.
  • (e)
    Express the evolution of the arc-length coordinate as a function of time.
  • (f)
    Write down the trajectory as a function of the arc-length coordinate.
  • (g)
    Where is the center of curvature for this curve when the particle is located at $(x, y, z) = (a, 0, 0)$?

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The rocket is modelled as a point particle $P$. Its three Cartesian coordinates are given as functions of time, so the motion is prescribed: the problem is purely kinematic and has no forces section.

ItemDefinition
Fixed frame$S_0 = \{O;\ \mathbf i_0, \mathbf j_0, \mathbf k_0\}$, origin at the launch point, $\mathbf k_0$ vertical, along the axis of the helix
Fixed basis$B_0 = \{\mathbf i_0, \mathbf j_0, \mathbf k_0\}$ (Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.1)
Cylindrical basis$B_C = \{\mathbf e_R, \mathbf e_\theta, \mathbf e_z\}$, obtained from $B_0$ by a rotation of angle $\theta$ about $\mathbf k_0$
Intrinsic basis$B_F = \{\mathbf e_t, \mathbf e_n, \mathbf e_b\}$ (tangent, principal normal, binormal; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.13)
Cartesian coordinates$x = a\cos(\omega t)$, $y = a\sin(\omega t)$, $z = v_z t$ (statement)
Cylindrical coordinates$R = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$, $z$ (Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.23)
Arc length$s$, measured along the helix from the point reached at $t = 0$
Data$a$ [m] radius of the helix, $\omega$ [rad/s] angular frequency, $v_z$ [m/s] vertical speed, $t$ [s] time
Hypotheses$a \gt 0$, $\omega \gt 0$, $v_z \gt 0$ constant; the law of the statement is taken to hold for $t \ge 0$ so that part (g), at $t = 0$, is the limit $t \to 0^+$
Degrees of freedom3 Cartesian coordinates minus 2 relations of the helix ($x = a\cos(\omega z/v_z)$, $y = a\sin(\omega z/v_z)$) leave 1; it is fixed by the time law

The position vector is first written in $B_0$, then in $B_C$; the intrinsic basis is built from the velocity in the kinematics section.

Change of Basis and Position Vector

The view along $\mathbf k_0$ (out of the paper) shows the rotation that takes $B_0$ into $B_C$; the right triangle of $\mathbf e_R$ gives its components.

Figure 1. View along $\mathbf k_0$ (out of the paper, $\odot$). The cylindrical basis is the fixed basis turned counter-clockwise by $\theta = \omega t$; the triangles give the components of $\mathbf e_R$ and $\mathbf e_\theta$, and the leftward leg of $\mathbf e_\theta$ explains its minus sign.
Cylindrical radius and azimuth of the helix Law: coordinate transformation of cylindrical coordinates Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.23; [Lecture 1, Slide 17]
$$\begin{aligned} R &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \quad &\text{(definition of the cylindrical radius)} \\ &= \sqrt{a^2\cos^2(\omega t) + a^2\sin^2(\omega t)} \quad &\text{(statement, Eqs. (1) and (2))} \\ &= a\sqrt{\cos^2(\omega t) + \sin^2(\omega t)} \quad &\text{(common factor $a^2$, with $a \gt 0$)} \\ &= a \quad &\text{(Pythagorean identity)} \\ \tan\theta &= \frac{y}{x} = \frac{a\sin(\omega t)}{a\cos(\omega t)} = \tan(\omega t) \quad &\text{(definition of the azimuth)} \\ \theta &= \omega t \quad &\text{($x$ and $\cos\theta$ have the same sign, so the branch is the one of $\omega t$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{R = a, \qquad \theta = \omega t, \qquad z = v_z t}$$

Units: $[R] = [a] = [\mathrm{L}]$; $[\theta] = [\omega][t] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = 1$; $[z] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the rocket stays on a cylinder of radius $a$ about $\mathbf k_0$, turns about it at the constant rate $\omega$ and climbs at the constant rate $v_z$.

Unit vectors of the cylindrical basis and the change of basis matrix Law: components of a unit vector with a known angle; simple rotation about the third axis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.24; [Lecture 1, Slide 7]

In the view of the figure $\mathbf k_0$ points out of the paper (⊙), $\mathbf i_0$ to the right and $\mathbf j_0$ up. A positive $\theta$ turns $\mathbf i_0$ towards $\mathbf j_0$, counterclockwise, as the right-hand rule about $\mathbf k_0$ requires.

  • $\mathbf e_R$ is the hypotenuse (length 1) of the right triangle whose adjacent leg lies along $\mathbf i_0$ and whose opposite leg lies along $\mathbf j_0$; the angle at $O$ is $\theta$.
  • $\mathbf e_\theta$ is $\mathbf e_R$ turned a further right angle counterclockwise. Its triangle has the angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf e_\theta$ and $\mathbf j_0$: the adjacent leg lies along $\mathbf j_0$, the opposite leg along the negative $\mathbf i_0$ direction, which is the origin of the minus sign.
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_R &= \cos\theta\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin\theta\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(adjacent leg $\cos\theta$ on $\mathbf i_0$, opposite leg $\sin\theta$ on $\mathbf j_0$)} \\ \mathbf e_\theta &= -\sin\theta\,\mathbf i_0 + \cos\theta\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(adjacent leg $\cos\theta$ on $\mathbf j_0$, opposite leg $\sin\theta$ pointing to $-\mathbf i_0$ for $0 \lt \theta \lt \pi$)} \\ \mathbf e_z &= \mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(the rotation axis is unchanged)} \\ \det[{}_0R_C] &= 1\cdot(\cos\theta\cos\theta - (-\sin\theta)\sin\theta) \quad &\text{(expansion along the third row, whose only entry is 1 at $(3,3)$)} \\ &= \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \quad &\text{(Pythagorean identity)} \\ ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{11} &= \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 1)} \\ ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{12} &= \cos\theta\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 2)} \\ ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{22} &= \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \quad &\text{(row 2 times row 2)} \\ ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{13} &= ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{23} = 0, \quad ([{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T)_{33} = 1 \quad &\text{(third row is $(0, 0, 1)$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_C] = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\omega t & -\sin\omega t & 0 \\ \sin\omega t & \cos\omega t & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det[{}_0R_C] = 1, \quad [{}_0R_C][{}_0R_C]^T = I}$$

Units: every entry is a cosine or a sine, dimensionless: $[1]$

Meaning: the columns are $\mathbf e_R$, $\mathbf e_\theta$, $\mathbf e_z$ written in $B_0$; the matrix is a proper rotation, so $B_C$ is orthonormal and right-handed at every instant.

The position vector is built in two steps: from $O$ to the foot $Q$ of $P$ on the plane of $\mathbf i_0$ and $\mathbf j_0$, then up from $Q$ to $P$.

Figure 2. The position vector is built in two steps: from $O$ to the foot $Q$ on the horizontal plane ($a\,\mathbf e_R$) and then up to $P$ ($v_z t\,\mathbf k_0$).
Position vector in the fixed and in the cylindrical basis Law: position vector of a point in Cartesian and in cylindrical components Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.5; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.28; [Lecture 1, Slide 12]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P &= \overrightarrow{OQ} + \overrightarrow{QP} \quad &\text{(vector sum through the foot $Q$ on the horizontal plane)} \\ \overrightarrow{OQ} &= a\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + a\sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(triangle $OQ$: hypotenuse $a$, adjacent leg $a\cos\omega t$, opposite leg $a\sin\omega t$)} \\ &= a\left(\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0\right) = a\,\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(first column of $[{}_0R_C]$)} \\ \overrightarrow{QP} &= v_z t\,\mathbf k_0 = v_z t\,\mathbf e_z \quad &\text{(height $z$ of the statement)} \\ \mathbf r_0^P &= a\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + a\sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0 + v_z t\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(sum in $B_0$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = a\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + a\sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0 + v_z t\,\mathbf k_0 = a\,\mathbf e_R + v_z t\,\mathbf e_z}$$

Units: $[a] = [\mathrm{L}]$ and $[v_z t] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: in $B_C$ the position has no $\mathbf e_\theta$ component: the turning of the rocket is carried entirely by the direction of $\mathbf e_R$.

Kinematics

(a) Velocity and acceleration in the fixed basis

Velocity and acceleration in Cartesian components Law: definition of velocity and acceleration; the vectors of $B_0$ are constant in $S_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7; [Lecture 1, Slide 13]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega t) &= -\sin(\omega t)\,\frac{d(\omega t)}{dt} = -\omega\sin(\omega t) \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t) &= \cos(\omega t)\,\frac{d(\omega t)}{dt} = \omega\cos(\omega t) \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ \mathbf v_0^P &= \dot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \dot y\,\mathbf j_0 + \dot z\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.6: $\mathbf i_0$, $\mathbf j_0$, $\mathbf k_0$ constant in $S_0$)} \\ &= -a\omega\sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + a\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0 + v_z\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(the two derivatives above; $\dot z$ is $v_z$)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \ddot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \ddot y\,\mathbf j_0 + \ddot z\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.7)} \\ &= -a\omega\cdot\omega\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + a\omega\cdot(-\omega\sin(\omega t))\,\mathbf j_0 + 0\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(chain rule again; $v_z$ constant)} \\ &= -a\omega^2\left(\cos(\omega t)\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin(\omega t)\,\mathbf j_0\right) \quad &\text{(common factor)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = a\cos\omega t\,\mathbf i_0 + a\sin\omega t\,\mathbf j_0 + v_z t\,\mathbf k_0,\quad \mathbf v_0^P = -a\omega\sin\omega t\,\mathbf i_0 + a\omega\cos\omega t\,\mathbf j_0 + v_z\,\mathbf k_0,\quad \mathbf a_0^P = -a\omega^2\cos\omega t\,\mathbf i_0 - a\omega^2\sin\omega t\,\mathbf j_0}$$

Units: $[a\omega] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$, $[v_z] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[a\omega^2] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the acceleration is horizontal and points from $P$ to the axis of the helix; the vertical motion is uniform.

(b) Velocity and acceleration in the cylindrical basis

Time derivatives of the cylindrical unit vectors Law: derivative of a vector of constant modulus that rotates with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ (Poisson) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62; Notes.pdf, Eqs. 2.25-2.27; [Lecture 1, Slide 10]

$B_C$ turns about $\mathbf k_0$ at the rate of $\theta$, which is $\omega$, so its angular velocity is $\omega\,\mathbf k_0$.

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\omega_{C} &= \dot\theta\,\mathbf k_0 = \omega\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(rotation about $\mathbf k_0$, positive by the right-hand rule)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_R &= \omega\,\mathbf k_0\times(\cos\theta\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin\theta\,\mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(Eq. 1.62)} \\ &= \omega\cos\theta\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0) + \omega\sin\theta\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(distributive property)} \\ &= \omega\cos\theta\,\mathbf j_0 - \omega\sin\theta\,\mathbf i_0 \quad &\text{($\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0 = \mathbf j_0$, $\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0 = -\mathbf i_0$)} \\ &= \omega\,\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{(second column of $[{}_0R_C]$)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_\theta &= \omega\,\mathbf k_0\times(-\sin\theta\,\mathbf i_0 + \cos\theta\,\mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(Eq. 1.62)} \\ &= \omega\left(-\sin\theta\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0) + \cos\theta\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0)\right) \quad &\text{(distribute $\mathbf k_0\times$ over the two terms)} \\ &= \omega\left(-\sin\theta\,\mathbf j_0 - \cos\theta\,\mathbf i_0\right) \quad &\text{($\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0 = \mathbf j_0$, $\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0 = -\mathbf i_0$)} \\ &= -\omega\,\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(first column of $[{}_0R_C]$)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_z &= \omega\,\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf k_0 = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(parallel vectors)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf e}_R = \omega\,\mathbf e_\theta, \qquad \dot{\mathbf e}_\theta = -\omega\,\mathbf e_R, \qquad \dot{\mathbf e}_z = \mathbf 0}$$

Units: $[\omega] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$, the rate of change of a dimensionless unit vector

Meaning: each unit vector of the horizontal plane turns at the rate $\omega$, always perpendicular to itself.

Velocity and acceleration in cylindrical components Law: definition of velocity and acceleration; product rule for a scalar times a vector Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.64; Notes.pdf, Eqs. 2.28-2.30; [Lecture 1, Slide 19]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(a\,\mathbf e_R + v_z t\,\mathbf e_z\right) \quad &\text{(definition of velocity)} \\ &= \dot a\,\mathbf e_R + a\,\dot{\mathbf e}_R + v_z\,\mathbf e_z + v_z t\,\dot{\mathbf e}_z \quad &\text{(product rule, Eq. 1.64)} \\ &= 0 + a\omega\,\mathbf e_\theta + v_z\,\mathbf e_z + \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{($a$ constant; derivatives of the unit vectors)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(a\omega\,\mathbf e_\theta + v_z\,\mathbf e_z\right) \quad &\text{(definition of acceleration)} \\ &= a\omega\,\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta + v_z\,\dot{\mathbf e}_z \quad &\text{(product rule; $a\omega$ and $v_z$ constant)} \\ &= a\omega(-\omega\,\mathbf e_R) + \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(derivatives of the unit vectors)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = a\,\mathbf e_R + v_z t\,\mathbf e_z, \qquad \mathbf v_0^P = a\omega\,\mathbf e_\theta + v_z\,\mathbf e_z, \qquad \mathbf a_0^P = -a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_R}$$

Units: $[a\omega] = [v_z] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$; $[a\omega^2] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: projecting the Cartesian result of part (a) on $\mathbf e_R$ gives the same centripetal acceleration; the cylindrical form shows at once that it has no $\mathbf e_\theta$ or $\mathbf e_z$ part.

(d) Modulus of the velocity

The speed is computed before part (c) because the intrinsic basis needs it.

Speed of the rocket Law: modulus of a vector in an orthonormal basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.40; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16
$$\begin{aligned} v &= \sqrt{\mathbf v_0^P\cdot\mathbf v_0^P} \quad &\text{(definition of the modulus)} \\ &= \sqrt{(a\omega)^2 + v_z^2} \quad &\text{(components $0$, $a\omega$, $v_z$ in the orthonormal $B_C$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v = \dot s = \sqrt{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2} \equiv V \quad (\text{constant})}$$

Units: $\sqrt{[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]^2} = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the rocket travels its path at a constant speed, the hypotenuse of the horizontal speed $a\omega$ and the vertical speed $v_z$.

(c) Intrinsic basis and the velocity and acceleration with $s$ and $\rho$

The unit tangent lies on the plane tangent to the cylinder; the figure folds that plane down onto the paper, with $\mathbf e_\theta$ to the right and $\mathbf e_z$ up.

Figure 3. Fold-down of the plane tangent to the cylinder. The velocity triangle (legs $a\omega$ and $v_z$, hypotenuse $V$) fixes the helix angle $\gamma$; $\mathbf e_b$ is $\mathbf e_t$ turned a right angle; $\mathbf e_n$ points into the paper, toward the axis.
Unit tangent vector Law: the unit tangent is the derivative of the position with respect to the arc length; chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.9; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16; [Lecture 1, Slide 25]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_t &= \frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{ds} = \frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{dt}\,\frac{dt}{ds} \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.9 and chain rule)} \\ &= \frac{\mathbf v_0^P}{V} \quad &\text{($ds/dt$ is the speed $V$)} \\ &= \frac{a\omega}{V}\,\mathbf e_\theta + \frac{v_z}{V}\,\mathbf e_z \quad &\text{(velocity of part (b))} \\ \cos\gamma &= \frac{a\omega}{V}, \quad \sin\gamma = \frac{v_z}{V} \quad &\text{(triangle: hypotenuse $V$, adjacent leg $a\omega$ on $\mathbf e_\theta$, opposite leg $v_z$ on $\mathbf e_z$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf e_t = \frac{a\omega\,\mathbf e_\theta + v_z\,\mathbf e_z}{\sqrt{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2}} = \cos\gamma\,\mathbf e_\theta + \sin\gamma\,\mathbf e_z}$$

Units: $[a\omega]/[V] = [v_z]/[V] = 1$, a unit vector

Meaning: the tangent climbs at the constant helix angle $\gamma$ above the horizontal direction $\mathbf e_\theta$.

Principal normal and radius of curvature Law: definition of the normal vector and of the curvature Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.11; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.12; [Lecture 1, Slide 26]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\mathbf e_t}{dt} &= \frac{a\omega}{V}\,\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta + \frac{v_z}{V}\,\dot{\mathbf e}_z \quad &\text{(product rule; $a\omega/V$ and $v_z/V$ constant)} \\ &= \frac{a\omega}{V}(-\omega\,\mathbf e_R) + \mathbf 0 = -\frac{a\omega^2}{V}\,\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(derivatives of the unit vectors)} \\ \frac{d\mathbf e_t}{ds} &= \frac{d\mathbf e_t}{dt}\,\frac{dt}{ds} = -\frac{a\omega^2}{V^2}\,\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(chain rule, $dt/ds$ is $1/V$)} \\ \frac{1}{\rho} &= \left|\frac{d\mathbf e_t}{ds}\right| = \frac{a\omega^2}{V^2} \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.12)} \\ \rho &= \frac{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2}{a\omega^2} = a + \frac{v_z^2}{a\omega^2} \quad &\text{(inverse; $V^2$ written out)} \\ \mathbf e_n &= \frac{d\mathbf e_t/ds}{|d\mathbf e_t/ds|} = -\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.11)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf e_n = -\mathbf e_R, \qquad \rho = a + \frac{v_z^2}{a\omega^2}}$$

Units: $[v_z^2]/[a\omega^2] = [\mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}]/[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the normal points horizontally to the axis of the helix; the osculating circle is larger than the cylinder because the climb straightens the path.

Binormal vector Law: the binormal completes a right-handed basis; vector product in an orthonormal right-handed basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.42; [Lecture 1, Slide 27]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_b &= \mathbf e_t\times\mathbf e_n = \frac{1}{V}\left(a\omega\,\mathbf e_\theta + v_z\,\mathbf e_z\right)\times(-\mathbf e_R) \quad &\text{(definition of $\mathbf e_b$)} \\ &= \frac{1}{V}\left(-a\omega\,(\mathbf e_\theta\times\mathbf e_R) - v_z\,(\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_R)\right) \quad &\text{(distributive property)} \\ &= \frac{1}{V}\left(-a\omega\,(-\mathbf e_z) - v_z\,\mathbf e_\theta\right) \quad &\text{($\mathbf e_\theta\times\mathbf e_R = -\mathbf e_z$, $\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf e_R = \mathbf e_\theta$, cyclic order $R, \theta, z$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf e_b = \frac{-v_z\,\mathbf e_\theta + a\omega\,\mathbf e_z}{\sqrt{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2}} = -\sin\gamma\,\mathbf e_\theta + \cos\gamma\,\mathbf e_z}$$

Units: dimensionless, a unit vector

Meaning: the binormal leans back from the vertical by the helix angle; it is constant in $B_C$ but turns with $\mathbf e_\theta$ in $S_0$.

Change of basis from the cylindrical to the intrinsic basis Law: change of basis matrix (columns are the new vectors in the old basis); orthonormality Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.16
$$\begin{aligned} \det[{}_CR_F] &= -(-1)\left(\frac{a\omega}{V}\frac{a\omega}{V} - \left(-\frac{v_z}{V}\right)\frac{v_z}{V}\right) \quad &\text{(expansion along row 1: only entry $-1$ at $(1,2)$, cofactor sign $-1$)} \\ &= \frac{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2}{V^2} = 1 \quad &\text{(definition of $V$)} \\ (RR^T)_{11} &= 0 + 1 + 0 = 1 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 1)} \\ (RR^T)_{22} &= \frac{a^2\omega^2}{V^2} + 0 + \frac{v_z^2}{V^2} = 1 \quad &\text{(row 2 times row 2)} \\ (RR^T)_{33} &= \frac{v_z^2}{V^2} + 0 + \frac{a^2\omega^2}{V^2} = 1 \quad &\text{(row 3 times row 3)} \\ (RR^T)_{12} &= (RR^T)_{13} = 0 \quad &\text{(row 1 has its only entry where rows 2 and 3 have zero)} \\ (RR^T)_{23} &= \frac{a\omega\,v_z}{V^2} + 0 - \frac{v_z\,a\omega}{V^2} = 0 \quad &\text{(row 2 times row 3)} \\ \det[{}_0R_F] &= \det[{}_0R_C]\,\det[{}_CR_F] = 1 \quad &\text{(composition, Eq. 1.16)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_CR_F] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ a\omega/V & 0 & -v_z/V \\ v_z/V & 0 & a\omega/V \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det[{}_CR_F] = 1, \quad [{}_CR_F][{}_CR_F]^T = I}$$

Units: dimensionless entries

Meaning: $B_F$ is a proper orthonormal basis; it is $B_C$ turned so that its first vector follows the climbing tangent.

Velocity and acceleration with $s$ and $\rho$ in the intrinsic basis Law: intrinsic components of velocity and acceleration Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.17; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.18; [Lecture 1, Slide 28]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^P &= \dot s\,\mathbf e_t \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.16)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= \ddot s\,\mathbf e_t + \frac{\dot s^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.17)} \\ \ddot s &= \frac{dV}{dt} = 0 \quad &\text{($V$ constant)} \\ \frac{\dot s^2}{\rho} &= \frac{V^2\,a\omega^2}{V^2} = a\omega^2 \quad &\text{($\rho$ of the normal box)} \\ \mathbf a_0^P &= a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_n = -a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_R \quad &\text{(agrees with part (b))} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = \dot s\,\mathbf e_t = V\,\mathbf e_t, \qquad \mathbf a_0^P = \ddot s\,\mathbf e_t + \frac{\dot s^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n = \frac{V^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n = a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_n}$$

Units: $[V^2/\rho] = [\mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}]/[\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: no tangential acceleration (uniform speed): all the acceleration turns the velocity, and it has no binormal component, as Eq. 2.17 requires.

(e) and (f) Arc length and trajectory in terms of $s$

Arc length as a function of time Law: the arc length is the integral of the speed Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16; [Lecture 1, Slide 24]
$$\begin{aligned} s(t) &= \int_0^t \dot s\,dt' = \int_0^t V\,dt' \quad &\text{(origin of $s$ at $t = 0$)} \\ &= V\left[t'\right]_0^t \quad &\text{(antiderivative of a constant)} \\ &= V(t - 0) \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{s(t) = \sqrt{a^2\omega^2 + v_z^2}\;t}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the distance along the helix grows linearly in time.

Rates of the coordinates Law: power rule and chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.63; [Lecture 1, Slide 19]
$$\begin{aligned} \dot\theta &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\delta t^n\right) = n\delta t^{n-1} \quad &\text{(power rule)} \\ \ddot\theta &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(n\delta t^{n-1}\right) = n(n-1)\delta t^{n-2} \quad &\text{(power rule)} \\ \frac{dr}{d\theta} &= \frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\alpha e^{\beta\theta}\right) = \alpha\beta e^{\beta\theta} = \beta r \quad &\text{(derivative of the exponential, chain rule in $\beta\theta$)} \\ \dot r &= \frac{dr}{d\theta}\,\dot\theta = \beta r\dot\theta \quad &\text{(chain rule, $r$ depends on $t$ through $\theta$)} \\ \ddot r &= \beta\left(\dot r\,\dot\theta + r\,\ddot\theta\right) \quad &\text{(product rule on $\beta r\dot\theta$)} \\ &= \beta\left(\beta r\dot\theta\cdot\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta\right) = \beta^2 r\dot\theta^2 + \beta r\ddot\theta \quad &\text{($\dot r$ substituted)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot\theta = n\delta t^{n-1},\quad \ddot\theta = n(n-1)\delta t^{n-2},\quad \dot r = \beta r\dot\theta,\quad \ddot r = \beta^2 r\dot\theta^2 + \beta r\ddot\theta}$$

Meaning: on this spiral the radial rate is always the fraction $\beta$ of the transverse rate, whatever the time law.

Acceleration in the polar basis Law: definition of acceleration; product rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.64; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.30; [Lecture 1, Slide 19]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf a_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot r\,\mathbf e_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta\right) \quad &\text{(definition of acceleration)} \\ &= \ddot r\,\mathbf e_r + \dot r\,\dot{\mathbf e}_r + (\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta)\,\mathbf e_\theta + r\dot\theta\,\dot{\mathbf e}_\theta \quad &\text{(product rule on each term)} \\ &= \ddot r\,\mathbf e_r + \dot r\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta + (\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta)\,\mathbf e_\theta - r\dot\theta^2\,\mathbf e_r \quad &\text{(derivatives of the unit vectors)} \\ &= (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\,\mathbf e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\,\mathbf e_\theta \quad &\text{(grouping by unit vector)} \\ a_r &= \beta^2 r\dot\theta^2 + \beta r\ddot\theta - r\dot\theta^2 = (\beta^2 - 1)r\dot\theta^2 + \beta r\ddot\theta \quad &\text{($\ddot r$ of the rates box)} \\ a_\theta &= r\ddot\theta + 2\beta r\dot\theta\cdot\dot\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\beta r\dot\theta^2 \quad &\text{($\dot r$ of the rates box)} \\ a_r &= r\left[(\beta^2-1)n^2\delta^2t^{2n-2} + \beta n(n-1)\delta t^{n-2}\right] \quad &\text{($\dot\theta^2$ and $\ddot\theta$ substituted)} \\ &= n\delta t^{n-2}\,r\left[(\beta^2-1)n\delta t^n + \beta(n-1)\right] \quad &\text{(common factor $n\delta t^{n-2}$, since $t^{2n-2} = t^{n-2}t^n$)} \\ a_\theta &= n\delta t^{n-2}\,r\left[(n-1) + 2\beta n\delta t^n\right] \quad &\text{(same substitution and factor)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: for uniform rotation ($n$ equal to one) the bracket loses its constant term and the acceleration is set only by the square of the angular rate.

Meaning: the intrinsic basis is rigidly attached to the polar basis at the fixed angle $\psi$: a property of the logarithmic spiral alone.

(d) Tangential and normal components of the acceleration

Intrinsic components of the acceleration Law: tangential and normal acceleration Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.17; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.18; [Lecture 1, Slide 28]
$$\begin{aligned} a_t &= \ddot s = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,r\dot\theta\right) \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.18)} \\ &= \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\left(\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta\right) \quad &\text{(product rule)} \\ &= \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,r\left(\beta\dot\theta^2 + \ddot\theta\right) \quad &\text{($\dot r$ is $\beta r\dot\theta$)} \\ &= \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,n\delta t^{n-2}\,r\left[\beta n\delta t^n + (n-1)\right] \quad &\text{(rates substituted; factor $n\delta t^{n-2}$)} \\ a_n &= \frac{\dot s^2}{\rho} = \frac{r^2\dot\theta^2(1+\beta^2)}{r\sqrt{1+\beta^2}} \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.18 with $\rho$ of part (c))} \\ &= \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,r\dot\theta^2 = \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,n^2\delta^2t^{2n-2}\,r \quad &\text{(simplification)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{a_t = \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,n\delta t^{n-2}\,\alpha e^{\beta\delta t^n}\left[\beta n\delta t^n + n - 1\right], \qquad a_n = \sqrt{1+\beta^2}\,n^2\delta^2t^{2n-2}\,\alpha e^{\beta\delta t^n}}$$

Units: $[\delta^2t^{2n-2}] = [\mathrm{T^{-2}}]$, so both are $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the normal acceleration is always positive (the path always bends to the same side); the tangential one changes the speed both because the radius grows and because the angular rate changes.

Projection check of the intrinsic components Law: components of a vector by the dot product with the unit vectors Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.28; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.40

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: projecting the polar acceleration of part (b) gives the same intrinsic components as Eq. 2.17: the two routes agree.

Problem 03

Kinematics, Astroid

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 6
Intrinsic Kinematics

A point particle describes the astroid:

$$ x = a\cos^3\phi, \qquad y = a\sin^3\phi $$

with a motion whose tangential acceleration is constant, $k$. If the point is initially at rest at $\phi = 0$, compute the evolution of the position as a function of time while the particle is in one of the smooth segments of the astroid. (Hint: $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2}$).

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The particle $P$ moves on a given planar curve with a given tangential acceleration. The only unknown is the time law along the curve, so the problem is kinematic (no forces section): the position follows from integrating the motion along the path.

The fold-down of the arc element shows the right triangle of the hint: the legs are the changes of $x$ and $y$, the hypotenuse is the arc element.

Derivatives of the coordinates with respect to the parameter Law: chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.63; [Lecture 1, Slide 24]
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dx}{d\phi} &= 3a\cos^2\phi\,\frac{d(\cos\phi)}{d\phi} \quad &\text{(chain rule on the cube, $u$ is $\cos\phi$)} \\ &= -3a\cos^2\phi\sin\phi \quad &\text{(derivative of the cosine)} \\ \frac{dy}{d\phi} &= 3a\sin^2\phi\,\frac{d(\sin\phi)}{d\phi} \quad &\text{(chain rule on the cube, $u$ is $\sin\phi$)} \\ &= 3a\sin^2\phi\cos\phi \quad &\text{(derivative of the sine)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{dx = -3a\cos^2\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi, \qquad dy = 3a\sin^2\phi\cos\phi\,d\phi}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$ per radian

Meaning: on the arc, $x$ decreases and $y$ increases as $\phi$ grows: the particle moves up and to the left.

Arc element Law: arc length of a plane curve (hint of the statement: Pythagoras on the infinitesimal triangle) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.19; Problems.pdf, p. 6
$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 &= dx^2 + dy^2 \quad &\text{(hypotenuse $ds$, legs $dx$ and $dy$)} \\ &= 9a^2\cos^4\phi\sin^2\phi\,d\phi^2 + 9a^2\sin^4\phi\cos^2\phi\,d\phi^2 \quad &\text{(squares of the differentials)} \\ &= 9a^2\cos^2\phi\sin^2\phi\left(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi\right)d\phi^2 \quad &\text{(common factor $9a^2\cos^2\phi\sin^2\phi$)} \\ &= 9a^2\cos^2\phi\sin^2\phi\,d\phi^2 \quad &\text{(Pythagorean identity)} \\ ds &= 3a\sin\phi\cos\phi\,d\phi \quad &\text{(positive root: $\sin\phi$, $\cos\phi \ge 0$ on the arc and $s$ grows with $\phi$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{ds}{d\phi} = 3a\sin\phi\cos\phi \ge 0, \qquad 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{2}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$ per radian

Meaning: the rate of arc length per unit of parameter vanishes at both cusps, where the curve stops and turns back sharply.

Arc length as a function of the parameter Law: integration of the arc element with a substitution and Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 2.3; [Lecture 1, Slide 24]
$$\begin{aligned} s(\phi) &= \int_0^\phi 3a\sin u\cos u\,du \quad &\text{(origin of $s$ at the start cusp)} \\ w &= \sin u, \quad dw = \cos u\,du \quad &\text{(substitution and its differential)} \\ u = 0 \Rightarrow w = 0, \quad u &= \phi \Rightarrow w = \sin\phi \quad &\text{(new limits)} \\ s(\phi) &= 3a\int_0^{\sin\phi} w\,dw = 3a\left[\frac{w^2}{2}\right]_0^{\sin\phi} \quad &\text{(antiderivative of $w$)} \\ &= 3a\left(\frac{\sin^2\phi}{2} - 0\right) \quad &\text{(Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{s(\phi) = \frac{3a}{2}\sin^2\phi, \qquad s\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{3a}{2}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the smooth arc between two cusps has length one and a half times $a$; the whole astroid has four of them.

Time law and position as a function of time

Arc length as a function of time Law: the tangential acceleration is the second derivative of the arc length; two integrations with Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.18; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.20; [Lecture 1, Slide 28]
$$\begin{aligned} \ddot s &= a_t = k \quad &\text{(Eq. 2.18 and statement)} \\ \dot s(t) - \dot s(0) &= \int_0^t k\,dt' = \left[k t'\right]_0^t = kt - 0 \quad &\text{(antiderivative of a constant, Barrow's rule)} \\ \dot s(t) &= kt \quad &\text{(starts from rest)} \\ s(t) - s(0) &= \int_0^t k t'\,dt' = \left[\frac{k t'^2}{2}\right]_0^t = \frac{kt^2}{2} - 0 \quad &\text{(antiderivative of $kt'$, Barrow's rule)} \\ s(t) &= \frac{kt^2}{2} \quad &\text{(starts at the origin of $s$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot s = kt, \qquad s = \frac{kt^2}{2}}$$

Meaning: along the curve the motion is uniformly accelerated, exactly as on a straight line.

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 7
Coordinate Transformations

An aircraft moves at constant altitude $y_0$ with velocity $v_0$ in the $x$ direction. Polar unit vectors and the change of basis matrix Law: rotation of the basis about $\mathbf k_0$ by the angle $\theta$; definition of the change of basis matrix Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.24

Meaning: the polar unit vectors change only in direction, at the angular rate of the line of sight.

Problem 05

Plane and Glider

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 7
Aerospace Towed Flight

An airplane $A$ is flying horizontally with a constant speed $v_A$, and is towing a glider $B$. The glider is gaining altitude. The tow cable has a length $r$, and the angle $\theta$ formed between the tow cable and the horizontal is increasing at a constant rate of $\dot{\theta}$. We want to determine the magnitude of the velocity, $v_B^0$, and the acceleration, $a_B^0$, of the glider as a function of $\theta$ and its derivatives.

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The airplane $A$ and the glider $B$ are modelled as point particles that move in one vertical plane. The answer is asked in the fixed (ground) frame, so the velocity and acceleration of $B$ are the derivatives of its position vector in $S_0$. Only the tools of chapters 1 and 2 of Notes.pdf are used: the position of $B$ is written as the position of $A$ plus the vector from $A$ to $B$, and that sum is differentiated; no theorem of relative motion is needed.

Hypotheses and degrees of freedom:

  • The airplane flies in a straight horizontal line at constant speed, so its motion is prescribed.
  • The cable is taut and of fixed length, so $B$ stays on a circle of radius $r$ centred at $A$: a holonomic constraint that depends on time through the position of $A$. The glider keeps one degree of freedom, the angle $\theta$.

Polar angle of the cable from the angle with the horizontal Law: relation between an angle measured from $-\mathbf i_0$ and the standard polar angle measured from $\mathbf i_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.23; Problems.pdf, p. 7

The direction $-\mathbf i_0$ has polar angle $\pi$. The cable is reached from $-\mathbf i_0$ by turning $\theta$ clockwise (toward $+\mathbf j_0$ from the left side), that is, by turning $-\theta$ in the positive sense about $\mathbf k_0$ ⊙.

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi &= \pi - \theta \quad &\text{(polar angle of $-\mathbf i_0$ minus the clockwise turn $\theta$)} \\ \cos\varphi &= \cos\pi\cos\theta + \sin\pi\sin\theta = -\cos\theta \quad &\text{(cosine of a difference; $\cos\pi$ is $-1$, $\sin\pi$ is 0)} \\ \sin\varphi &= \sin\pi\cos\theta - \cos\pi\sin\theta = \sin\theta \quad &\text{(sine of a difference)} \\ \dot\varphi &= -\dot\theta \quad &\text{(chain rule; $\pi$ is constant)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: the speed is independent of the basis, as the orthogonality of the change of basis matrix requires.

Second computation of the acceleration, in the fixed basis Law: derivative of the Cartesian components with the product and chain rules Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left(v_A + r\dot\theta\sin\theta\right) &= r\ddot\theta\sin\theta + r\dot\theta^2\cos\theta \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule on $\sin\theta$)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(r\dot\theta\cos\theta\right) &= r\ddot\theta\cos\theta - r\dot\theta^2\sin\theta \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule on $\cos\theta$)} \\ -r\dot\theta^2\,\mathbf i_1 - r\ddot\theta\,\mathbf j_1 &= -r\dot\theta^2\left(-\cos\theta\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin\theta\,\mathbf j_0\right) - r\ddot\theta\left(-\sin\theta\,\mathbf i_0 - \cos\theta\,\mathbf j_0\right) \quad &\text{(cable basis written in the fixed basis, with $\mathbf i_1$ and $\mathbf j_1$ of the change of basis)} \\ &= \left(r\dot\theta^2\cos\theta + r\ddot\theta\sin\theta\right)\mathbf i_0 + \left(r\ddot\theta\cos\theta - r\dot\theta^2\sin\theta\right)\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(collect the components of $\mathbf i_0$ and $\mathbf j_0$; equal to the boxed result)} \\ \left(a_0^B\right)^2 &= r^2\ddot\theta^2(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) + r^2\dot\theta^4(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) \quad &\text{(sum of squares; the cross terms in $\sin\theta\cos\theta$ cancel)} \\ &= r^2\ddot\theta^2 + r^2\dot\theta^4 \quad &\text{(Pythagorean identity)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf a_0^B = \left(r\ddot\theta\sin\theta + r\dot\theta^2\cos\theta\right)\mathbf i_0 + \left(r\ddot\theta\cos\theta - r\dot\theta^2\sin\theta\right)\mathbf j_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the same magnitude as in the cable basis; the projection of the cable vectors onto the fixed basis, written in the row above, gives the same two components.

Limiting cases Law: evaluation of the results in special configurations Source: Problems.pdf, p. 7
$$\begin{aligned} \dot\theta \equiv 0 &\Rightarrow \mathbf v_0^B = v_A\,\mathbf i_0,\quad \mathbf a_0^B = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(the glider moves rigidly with the airplane)} \\ \theta = 0 &\Rightarrow v_0^B = \sqrt{v_A^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2} \quad &\text{(cable horizontal: relative velocity vertical, perpendicular to the flight)} \\ \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2} &\Rightarrow v_0^B = v_A + r\dot\theta \quad &\text{(cable vertical: relative velocity horizontal, forward)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{v_A \le v_0^B \le v_A + r\dot\theta\ \text{for}\ 0 \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\ \text{and positive rate}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the results reduce to the straight and the circular configurations when $\dot\theta$ or $\ddot\theta$ vanishes; the acceleration never depends on the flight speed because the airplane does not accelerate.

Problem 06

Particle on Sinusoidal Curve

Original statement · Problems.pdf, p. 7
Frenet-Serret Frame

A point particle $P$ travels along a curve given by:

$$ y = \sin x $$

with a velocity along $x$ given by $\dot{x} = 1$.

  • (a)
    Find the intrinsic, unitary vector basis associated to the curve.
  • (b)
    Express $x$ as a function of an arc length parameter, $s$. You can leave your solution as a function of an integral.
  • (c)
    Find the tangent and normal acceleration components of $P$ as a function of $x$.
  • (d)
    Write the radius of curvature of the trajectory as a function of $x$.
Worked Solution [ Click to Expand ]

Reference Frames, Bases and Coordinates

The particle $P$ moves on a fixed plane curve, the graph of the sine function, with a prescribed horizontal rate. The intrinsic (Frenet-Serret) basis of the trajectory is built from the definitions of Notes.pdf, Sec. 2.4, and the accelerations from the definitions of Sec. 2.5.

Hypotheses and degrees of freedom:

  • The data are read in SI units: the sine curve carries a length scale $\ell$ of 1 m (height and abscissa in metres, the argument of the sine in units of $\ell$) and the horizontal rate is 1 m/s; with that length scale every formula below keeps its dimensions.
  • The particle starts at the origin, so at the initial instant $x$ is zero and the arc length is measured from there.
  • The curve is a holonomic, time-independent constraint in the plane: one degree of freedom, the abscissa $x$, whose time law is given. There is no dynamics, so the forces section is omitted.
  • At the inflection points (zero $\sin x$) the curvature vanishes and the principal normal is not defined (Notes.pdf, footnote 12 of Sec. 2.4); the intrinsic basis is given for the other points.
Figure 1. Plane of the trajectory. The differential triangle has hypotenuse $ds = L\,dx$, adjacent leg $dx$ ($\cos\gamma$) and opposite leg $dy$ ($\sin\gamma$); the normal points to the concave side and flips between arches together with the binormal.

Change of Basis and Position Vector

Position vector and arc length element Law: definition of the position vector; length of the differential displacement along the curve Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.5; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.9; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.19

The differential displacement along the curve is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose horizontal leg is the increment of abscissa and whose vertical leg is the increment of height.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_0^P &= x\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin x\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(Cartesian coordinates of a point of the curve)} \\ \frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{dx} &= \mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(derivative with respect to $x$; $\tfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$)} \\ dy &= \cos x\,dx \quad &\text{(vertical leg of the differential triangle)} \\ ds^2 &= dx^2 + dy^2 = (1 + \cos^2 x)\,dx^2 \quad &\text{(Pythagoras in the differential triangle)} \\ ds &= \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx = L\,dx \quad &\text{(positive root: $s$ grows with $x$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf r_0^P = x\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin x\,\mathbf j_0,\qquad ds = L(x)\,dx,\quad L(x) = \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]$ for the position; $L$ is $[\mathrm{L}]/[\mathrm{L}] = [1]$

Meaning: the metric factor lies between 1 (at the crests, where the curve is horizontal) and the square root of 2 (at the inflection points, where the slope is 1 in magnitude).

Tangent unit vector and its angle Law: the tangent unit vector is the derivative of the position with respect to the arc length Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.9; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.19

In the differential triangle the hypotenuse is the arc element, the leg adjacent to the angle of the tangent is the horizontal increment and the opposite leg is the vertical increment.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_t &= \frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{ds} = \frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{ds} \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ &= \frac{\mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0}{L} \quad &\text{(derivative of the previous box; $dx/ds$ is $1/L$)} \\ \cos\gamma &= \frac{dx}{ds} = \frac{1}{L} \quad &\text{(adjacent leg over hypotenuse)} \\ \sin\gamma &= \frac{dy}{ds} = \frac{\cos x}{L} \quad &\text{(opposite leg over hypotenuse)} \\ \tan\gamma &= \cos x \quad &\text{(quotient: the slope of the curve)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf e_t = \frac{\mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}} = \cos\gamma\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin\gamma\,\mathbf j_0,\qquad \gamma = \arctan(\cos x)}$$

Units: $[1]$

Meaning: the tangent always points forward (positive horizontal component) and its angle swings between minus and plus 45 degrees, reached at the inflection points.

Figure 2. The tangent basis is the fixed basis turned by $\gamma$ about $\mathbf k_0$ (out of the paper); the component of $\mathbf e_\perp$ along $\mathbf i_0$ is negative because $\mathbf e_\perp$ leans toward $-\mathbf i_0$.
Auxiliary normal and the change of basis to the tangent basis Law: rotation of the basis by the angle of the tangent about $\mathbf k_0$; change of basis matrix Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15

The auxiliary normal is the tangent turned a right angle counterclockwise about $\mathbf k_0$ ⊙ (right-hand rule). In the view along $\mathbf k_0$ its leg adjacent to the angle of the tangent lies along $\mathbf j_0$ and its opposite leg points toward $-\mathbf i_0$, which explains the minus sign.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf e_\perp &= \mathbf k_0 \times \mathbf e_t = \cos\gamma\,(\mathbf k_0 \times \mathbf i_0) + \sin\gamma\,(\mathbf k_0 \times \mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(distributive property)} \\ &= \cos\gamma\,\mathbf j_0 - \sin\gamma\,\mathbf i_0 \quad &\text{(right-handed products: k0 cross i0 is j0, k0 cross j0 is minus i0)} \\ &= \frac{-\cos x\,\mathbf i_0 + \mathbf j_0}{L} \quad &\text{(sine and cosine of the angle of the tangent)} \\ \det[{}_0R_\gamma] &= 1\cdot(\cos^2\gamma + \sin^2\gamma) = 1 \quad &\text{(expansion along the third row)} \\ ([{}_0R_\gamma][{}_0R_\gamma]^T)_{12} &= \cos\gamma\sin\gamma - \sin\gamma\cos\gamma = 0 \quad &\text{(row 1 times row 2)} \\ ([{}_0R_\gamma][{}_0R_\gamma]^T)_{11} &= ([{}_0R_\gamma][{}_0R_\gamma]^T)_{22} = \cos^2\gamma + \sin^2\gamma = 1 \quad &\text{(rows of unit length)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_\gamma] = \frac{1}{L}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\cos x & 0 \\ \cos x & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & L \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det = 1,\qquad RR^T = I}$$

Units: $[1]$

Meaning: the columns are the tangent, the auxiliary normal and the out-of-plane vector written in the fixed basis; the pair turns with the slope of the curve.

Kinematics

Time law, velocity and speed Law: definition of velocity in $S_0$; chain rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16; Problems.pdf, p. 7
$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= x(0) + \int_0^t 1\,dt' = 0 + [t']_0^t = t \quad &\text{(prescribed rate; Barrow's rule)} \\ \mathbf v_0^P &= \dot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \frac{d}{dt}(\sin x)\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(derivative of the position; fixed basis)} \\ &= \dot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\dot x\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(chain rule)} \\ &= \mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0 = L\,\mathbf e_t \quad &\text{(unit horizontal rate; tangent of the previous section)} \\ \dot s &= \frac{ds}{dx}\,\dot x = L \quad &\text{(chain rule with the arc element)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = \mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0 = \dot s\,\mathbf e_t,\qquad \dot s = v = \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: the velocity is tangent to the curve, as Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.16 states; the speed is largest at the inflection points, where the curve is steepest.

Rotation rate of the tangent and derivatives of the unit vectors Law: derivative of a unit vector rotating with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$; vector products written out Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.62; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.56
$$\begin{aligned} \dot\gamma &= \frac{d}{dt}\arctan(\cos x) = \frac{1}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,\frac{d}{dt}\cos x \quad &\text{(chain rule; derivative of the arctangent)} \\ &= \frac{-\sin x\,\dot x}{1 + \cos^2 x} = -\frac{\sin x}{L^2} \quad &\text{(chain rule; unit horizontal rate)} \\ \boldsymbol\omega_{\gamma,0} &= \dot\gamma\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(rotation about $\mathbf k_0$ by $\gamma$)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_t &= \dot\gamma\,\mathbf k_0 \times (\cos\gamma\,\mathbf i_0 + \sin\gamma\,\mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 1.62)} \\ &= \dot\gamma\,(\cos\gamma\,\mathbf j_0 - \sin\gamma\,\mathbf i_0) = \dot\gamma\,\mathbf e_\perp \quad &\text{(right-handed products; definition of $\mathbf e_\perp$)} \\ \dot{\mathbf e}_\perp &= \dot\gamma\,\mathbf k_0 \times (-\sin\gamma\,\mathbf i_0 + \cos\gamma\,\mathbf j_0) \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 1.62)} \\ &= \dot\gamma\left(-\sin\gamma\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0) + \cos\gamma\,(\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0)\right) \quad &\text{(distribute $\mathbf k_0\times$ over the two terms)} \\ &= \dot\gamma\left(-\sin\gamma\,\mathbf j_0 - \cos\gamma\,\mathbf i_0\right) = -\dot\gamma\,\mathbf e_t \quad &\text{($\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf i_0 = \mathbf j_0$, $\mathbf k_0\times\mathbf j_0 = -\mathbf i_0$; definition of $\mathbf e_t$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot{\mathbf e}_t = -\frac{\sin x}{L^2}\,\mathbf e_\perp,\qquad \dot{\mathbf e}_\perp = \frac{\sin x}{L^2}\,\mathbf e_t}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: on the arches above the axis the tangent turns clockwise, on the arches below it turns counterclockwise; it stops turning at the inflection points.

(a) Intrinsic unit basis

Curvature and principal normal Law: derivative of the tangent with respect to the arc length; definition of the normal and of the curvature Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.11; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.12; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.15
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\mathbf e_t}{ds} &= \frac{\dot{\mathbf e}_t}{\dot s} \quad &\text{(chain rule: derivative in time over the rate of $s$)} \\ &= -\frac{\sin x}{L^3}\,\mathbf e_\perp \quad &\text{(previous two boxes)} \\ \left|\frac{d\mathbf e_t}{ds}\right| &= \frac{|\sin x|}{L^3} = \kappa = \frac{1}{\rho} \quad &\text{(norm; $\mathbf e_\perp$ is a unit vector)} \\ \mathbf e_n &= \frac{d\mathbf e_t/ds}{|d\mathbf e_t/ds|} = -\operatorname{sgn}(\sin x)\,\mathbf e_\perp = \sigma\,\mathbf e_\perp \quad &\text{(division by the norm, nonzero $\sin x$)} \\ &= \sigma\,\frac{-\cos x\,\mathbf i_0 + \mathbf j_0}{L} \quad &\text{(components of $\mathbf e_\perp$)} \\ \mathbf e_b &= \mathbf e_t \times \mathbf e_n = \sigma\,\mathbf e_t \times (\mathbf k_0 \times \mathbf e_t) \quad &\text{(definition of the binormal)} \\ &= \sigma\left((\mathbf e_t\cdot\mathbf e_t)\,\mathbf k_0 - (\mathbf e_t\cdot\mathbf k_0)\,\mathbf e_t\right) = \sigma\,\mathbf k_0 \quad &\text{(double vector product, Notes Eq. 1.37; $\mathbf e_t$ lies in the plane)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf e_t = \frac{\mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\mathbf j_0}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}},\quad \mathbf e_n = \operatorname{sgn}(\sin x)\,\frac{\cos x\,\mathbf i_0 - \mathbf j_0}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}},\quad \mathbf e_b = -\operatorname{sgn}(\sin x)\,\mathbf k_0}$$

Units: $[1]$ for the unit vectors; $\kappa$ is $[\mathrm{L^{-1}}]$

Meaning: on the arches above the axis the curve is concave down and the normal points down, toward the centre of curvature below; on the arches below the axis it points up. The binormal is constant on each arch and flips at each inflection point.

Change of basis matrix to the intrinsic basis Law: columns are the intrinsic unit vectors in the fixed basis; orthogonality and determinant checked Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.13; Notes.pdf, Eq. 1.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.13
$$\begin{aligned} \det[{}_0R_F] &= \sigma\cdot\left(\frac{1}{L}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{L} - \frac{-\sigma\cos x}{L}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{L}\right) \quad &\text{(expansion along the third row)} \\ &= \sigma^2\,\frac{1 + \cos^2 x}{L^2} = 1 \quad &\text{($\sigma^2$ is 1; definition of $L$)} \\ (RR^T)_{11} &= \frac{1 + \cos^2 x}{L^2} = 1,\quad (RR^T)_{22} = \frac{\cos^2 x + 1}{L^2} = 1 \quad &\text{(rows 1 and 2 of unit length)} \\ (RR^T)_{12} &= \frac{\cos x - \sigma^2\cos x}{L^2} = 0,\quad (RR^T)_{33} = \sigma^2 = 1 \quad &\text{(rows 1 and 2 orthogonal; row 3 unit)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{[{}_0R_F] = \frac{1}{L}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\sigma\cos x & 0 \\ \cos x & \sigma & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma L \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det[{}_0R_F] = 1,\qquad [{}_0R_F][{}_0R_F]^T = I}$$

Units: $[1]$

Meaning: the intrinsic basis is right-handed on every arch: when the normal flips, the binormal flips too, so the determinant stays 1.

(b) Abscissa as a function of the arc length

Arc length as an integral and its inversion Law: integration of the arc element with Barrow's rule; inverse of a strictly increasing function Source: Notes.pdf, Sec. 2.3; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.19
$$\begin{aligned} s(x) &= \int_0^x \frac{ds}{du}\,du = \int_0^x \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 u}\,du \quad &\text{(arc element, origin of $s$ at the origin $O$)} \\ \frac{ds}{dx} &= \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x} \ge 1 \gt 0 \quad &\text{(derivative of the integral with respect to its upper limit)} \\ x &\le s(x) \le \sqrt 2\,x \quad &\text{(bounds of the integrand, for positive $x$)} \\ s(x + \pi) &= s(x) + s(\pi) \quad &\text{(the integrand has period $\pi$)} \\ x &= s^{-1}(s) \quad &\text{(strictly increasing function: unique inverse)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{s(x) = \int_0^x \sqrt{1 + \cos^2 u}\,du,\qquad x = s^{-1}(s),\qquad s(\pi) \approx 3.8202\ \mathrm{m}}$$

Units: the integrand is $[1]$ and $du$ is $[\mathrm{L}]$, so $s$ is $[\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the integral has no elementary antiderivative (extension: it is an incomplete elliptic integral of the second kind), so the statement allows leaving it as an integral; along one arch the particle travels 3.82 m while advancing 3.14 m.

(c) Tangential and normal acceleration

Acceleration in the fixed basis Law: definition of acceleration; product and chain rules Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.7
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf a_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \cos x\,\dot x\,\mathbf j_0\right) \quad &\text{(definition of acceleration)} \\ &= \ddot x\,\mathbf i_0 + \left(-\sin x\,\dot x^2 + \cos x\,\ddot x\right)\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(product rule; chain rule on $\cos x$)} \\ &= -\sin x\,\mathbf j_0 \quad &\text{(unit rate, zero second derivative)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf a_0^P = -\sin x\,\mathbf j_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the horizontal motion is uniform, so the only acceleration is vertical, the second derivative of the height.

Intrinsic components of the acceleration Law: derivative of the velocity written in the intrinsic basis Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.17; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.18
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf a_0^P &= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot s\,\mathbf e_t\right) = \ddot s\,\mathbf e_t + \dot s\,\dot{\mathbf e}_t \quad &\text{(product rule)} \\ \ddot s &= \frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x} = \frac{2\cos x\,(-\sin x)\,\dot x}{2\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}} = -\frac{\sin x\cos x}{L} \quad &\text{(chain rule twice)} \\ \dot s\,\dot{\mathbf e}_t &= L\left(-\frac{\sin x}{L^2}\right)\mathbf e_\perp = -\frac{\sin x}{L}\,\sigma\,\mathbf e_n \quad &\text{(rotation of the tangent; $\mathbf e_\perp$ is $\sigma\,\mathbf e_n$)} \\ -\sigma\sin x &= \operatorname{sgn}(\sin x)\sin x = |\sin x| \quad &\text{(definition of $\sigma$)} \\ a_t &= -\frac{\sin x\cos x}{L},\quad a_n = \frac{|\sin x|}{L} \quad &\text{(components along $\mathbf e_t$ and $\mathbf e_n$)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{a_t = -\frac{\sin x\cos x}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}},\qquad a_n = \frac{|\sin x|}{\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]$

Meaning: the speed depends only on the slope, so it rises toward the inflection points and falls toward the crests and troughs, and the tangential acceleration is negative wherever $\sin x\cos x$ is positive; the normal acceleration is never negative, because the normal points to the centre of curvature.

(d) Radius of curvature

Radius of curvature Law: normal acceleration equals the square of the speed over the radius of curvature Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.18; Notes.pdf, Eq. 2.12
$$\begin{aligned} \rho &= \frac{\dot s^2}{a_n} \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 2.18 solved for $\rho$)} \\ &= \frac{1 + \cos^2 x}{|\sin x|/\sqrt{1 + \cos^2 x}} \quad &\text{(speed and normal acceleration)} \\ &= \frac{(1 + \cos^2 x)^{3/2}}{|\sin x|} \quad &\text{(product of the powers of $L$)} \\ \frac{1}{\kappa} &= \frac{L^3}{|\sin x|} \quad &\text{(curvature of part (a), same result)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\rho(x) = \frac{\left(1 + \cos^2 x\right)^{3/2}}{|\sin x|}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}]/[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{L}]$

Meaning: the radius is smallest at the crests and troughs (1 m) and grows without bound at the inflection points, where the curve is locally straight.

Figure 3. The radius of curvature is $1$ m at the crests and troughs, grows away from them ($2.598$ m at $x=\pi/4$) and tends to infinity at the inflection points.
