C01 — Intrinsic acceleration on a parabolic helix: constant angular rate is not constant speed

A point particle $P$ moves with respect to the inertial frame $S_0=\{O;\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\}$ along the curve given in cylindrical coordinates $(R,\varphi,z)$, with basis $\{\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\varphi,\mathbf e_z\}$, by

$$R=a,\qquad z=\frac{a}{2}\,\varphi^2,\qquad \varphi\ge0,$$

with $a>0$ a constant length: a helix wound on the cylinder $R=a$ whose pitch grows with $\varphi$. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Part (a) · 1 point

Constant angular rate

The particle moves with the time law $\varphi=\omega t$, $\omega>0$ constant. Statement: "Since $\dot\varphi$ is constant, the tangential acceleration of $P$ is zero, and at the point $\varphi=1$ the acceleration of $P$ is purely normal."

Step 1. Velocity in cylindrical coordinates

With $\mathbf r=R\,\mathbf e_R+z\,\mathbf e_z$, $\dot{\mathbf e}_R=\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi$ and $\dot{\mathbf e}_\varphi=-\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_R$ (Notes §2.6), the velocity is $\mathbf v=\dot R\,\mathbf e_R+R\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi+\dot z\,\mathbf e_z$. Here $\dot R=0$, $\dot\varphi=\omega$ and, by the chain rule, $\dot z=\frac{dz}{d\varphi}\dot\varphi=a\varphi\,\omega$:

$$\mathbf v=a\omega\,\mathbf e_\varphi+a\omega\varphi\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad |\mathbf v|=a\omega\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}$$

The speed is not constant, so the statement already fails: a constant angular rate does not mean a constant speed when the curve climbs faster and faster.

Step 2. Acceleration

$\mathbf a=(\ddot R-R\dot\varphi^2)\,\mathbf e_R+(R\ddot\varphi+2\dot R\dot\varphi)\,\mathbf e_\varphi+\ddot z\,\mathbf e_z$, with $\ddot\varphi=0$ and $\ddot z=\frac{d}{dt}(a\omega\varphi)=a\omega\dot\varphi=a\omega^2$:

$$\mathbf a=-a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_R+a\omega^2\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad |\mathbf a|^2=2a^2\omega^4$$

Step 3. Tangential and normal components

The tangential acceleration is $a_t=\frac{d|\mathbf v|}{dt}$ (Notes §2.5). With the chain rule $\frac{d}{dt}\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}=\frac{\varphi\,\dot\varphi}{\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}}$:

$$a_t=a\omega\,\frac{\varphi\,\omega}{\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}}=\frac{a\omega^2\varphi}{\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}}$$

The same result follows from $a_t=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf v/|\mathbf v|=a^2\omega^3\varphi/(a\omega\sqrt{1+\varphi^2})$. At $\varphi=1$:

$$a_t=\frac{a\omega^2}{\sqrt2}\neq0,\qquad a_n=\sqrt{|\mathbf a|^2-a_t^2}=a\omega^2\sqrt{2-\tfrac12}=\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,a\omega^2$$

The acceleration has a tangential part, so the statement is FALSE. For later use: at $\varphi=1$, $\mathbf e_t=(\mathbf e_\varphi+\mathbf e_z)/\sqrt2$, $a_t\mathbf e_t=\tfrac12a\omega^2(\mathbf e_\varphi+\mathbf e_z)$ and

$$\mathbf a_n=\mathbf a-a_t\mathbf e_t=a\omega^2\big(-\mathbf e_R-\tfrac12\mathbf e_\varphi+\tfrac12\mathbf e_z\big),\qquad \mathbf e_n=\frac{-2\,\mathbf e_R-\mathbf e_\varphi+\mathbf e_z}{\sqrt6}$$
Key points
  • FALSE.
  • $\mathbf v=a\omega\,\mathbf e_\varphi+a\omega\varphi\,\mathbf e_z$, $\ |\mathbf v|=a\omega\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}$ grows with time; $\ \mathbf a=a\omega^2(-\mathbf e_R+\mathbf e_z)$.
  • $a_t=a\omega^2\varphi/\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}$: at $\varphi=1$, $a_t=a\omega^2/\sqrt2\neq0$ and $a_n=\sqrt{3/2}\,a\omega^2$.

Part (b) · 1 point

Constant speed

The same curve is now traversed at a constant speed $v_0$. Statement: "At the point $\varphi=1$ the acceleration of $P$ is perpendicular to its velocity, has magnitude $\sqrt6\,v_0^2/(4a)$ and points along the same unit vector $\mathbf e_n$ as the normal acceleration of part (a)."

Step 1. Intrinsic acceleration

In the intrinsic basis (Notes §2.5), $\mathbf a=\ddot s\,\mathbf e_t+\dfrac{\dot s^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n$. With $\dot s=v_0$ constant, $\ddot s=0$, so $\mathbf a=\dfrac{v_0^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n$, which is perpendicular to $\mathbf v=v_0\,\mathbf e_t$. It is not zero: the trajectory is curved.

Step 2. Curvature at $\varphi=1$

The curvature depends only on the geometry. From part (a) at $\varphi=1$, $\kappa=\dfrac{a_n}{|\mathbf v|^2}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3/2}\,a\omega^2}{2a^2\omega^2}=\dfrac{\sqrt6}{4a}$. Independent check with the parameter $\varphi$: $\mathbf r'=a(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi,\varphi)$ and $\mathbf r''=a(-\cos\varphi,-\sin\varphi,1)$ in $\{\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\}$, so

$$\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''=a^2\big(\cos\varphi+\varphi\sin\varphi,\ \sin\varphi-\varphi\cos\varphi,\ 1\big),\qquad |\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''|=a^2\sqrt{2+\varphi^2},\qquad |\mathbf r'|=a\sqrt{1+\varphi^2}$$
$$\kappa=\frac{|\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''|}{|\mathbf r'|^3}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\varphi^2}}{a\,(1+\varphi^2)^{3/2}},\qquad \kappa(1)=\frac{\sqrt3}{a\,2^{3/2}}=\frac{\sqrt6}{4a}$$

Step 3. Magnitude and direction

$$|\mathbf a|=\kappa\,v_0^2=\frac{\sqrt6\,v_0^2}{4a}$$

The principal normal is $\mathbf e_n=\dfrac{d\mathbf e_t/ds}{|d\mathbf e_t/ds|}$, a function of the point of the curve only (Notes §2.4), so it is the same vector $(-2\,\mathbf e_R-\mathbf e_\varphi+\mathbf e_z)/\sqrt6$ found in (a). The three claims hold: TRUE.

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • $\mathbf a=\dot v\,\mathbf e_t+\dfrac{v^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf e_n$ with $\dot v=0$: $\mathbf a\perp\mathbf v$.
  • $\kappa(\varphi)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2+\varphi^2}}{a(1+\varphi^2)^{3/2}}$, $\ \kappa(1)=\dfrac{\sqrt6}{4a}$, $\ |\mathbf a|=\kappa v_0^2=\dfrac{\sqrt6\,v_0^2}{4a}$; $\mathbf e_n$ depends only on the curve.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $a\omega^2$ is m s$^{-2}$; $\kappa$ is m$^{-1}$ and $\kappa v_0^2$ is m s$^{-2}$.
  • Limits: at $\varphi=0$ the tangent is horizontal, but $\kappa(0)=\sqrt2/a$ is larger than the $1/a$ of the circle $R=a$ because the curve also bends upwards ($d^2z/d\varphi^2=a\neq0$); for $\varphi\to\infty$, $\kappa\approx1/(a\varphi^2)\to0$: the turns become almost vertical lines.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) confusing a constant angular rate $\dot\varphi$ with a constant speed; (2) saying "constant speed implies zero acceleration"; (3) believing that $\mathbf e_t$, $\mathbf e_n$ or $\rho$ depend on how fast the curve is traversed.

C02 — Motion in a cubic potential: a separatrix that is never crossed and an asymmetric well

A point particle $P$ of mass $m$ moves along the $x$ axis of an inertial frame under the single conservative force derived from the potential energy

$$V(x)=V_0\left[\Big(\frac{x}{b}\Big)^3-3\,\frac{x}{b}\right],$$

with $V_0>0$ (an energy) and $b>0$ (a length) constants. The figure shows $V(x)$ and two energy levels. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Figure 1. Potential $V(x)=V_0\big[(x/b)^3-3x/b\big]$ with the energy levels $E=2V_0$ (released at $x=2b$) and $E=0$ (released at $x=0$).

Part (a) · 1 point

Released at $x=2b$

Statement: "If $P$ is released from rest at $x=2b$, it moves to the left, reaches the top of the barrier at $x=-b$ after a finite time and then escapes towards $x\to-\infty$."

Step 1. Energy and direction of the motion

Write $u=x/b$, so $V=V_0(u^3-3u)$. By the chain rule $\frac{dV}{dx}=\frac{dV}{du}\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{3V_0}{b}(u^2-1)$: the equilibria are $x=\pm b$, with $V(-b)=2V_0$ (maximum, $V''<0$) and $V(b)=-2V_0$ (minimum). Released from rest at $x=2b$, the energy is $E=V(2b)=V_0(8-6)=2V_0$ and the initial force is $F=-\frac{dV}{dx}\big|_{2b}=-\frac{3V_0}{b}(4-1)=-\frac{9V_0}{b}<0$: $P$ moves to the left.

Step 2. The kinetic energy factorises

Energy conservation (Notes §4.5) gives $\tfrac12m\dot x^2=E-V$, and

$$E-V=V_0\big(2-u^3+3u\big)=V_0\,(u+1)^2\,(2-u)$$

(check: $(u+1)^2(2-u)=(u^2+2u+1)(2-u)=-u^3+3u+2$). For $-1<u<2$ it is positive, and it vanishes at $u=-1$ with a double root.

Step 3. Time to approach $x=-b$

On the way to the left $\dot x=-\sqrt{2V_0/m}\,(u+1)\sqrt{2-u}$, so $dt=\dfrac{-b\,du}{\sqrt{2V_0/m}\,(u+1)\sqrt{2-u}}$. Substitute $w=\sqrt{2-u}$: $u=2-w^2$, $du=-2w\,dw$, $u+1=3-w^2$, with $w=0$ at the start ($u=2$) and $w\to\sqrt3$ at the barrier ($u\to-1$):

$$t(w)=\frac{2b}{\sqrt{2V_0/m}}\int_0^{w}\frac{dw'}{3-w'^2}$$

With the antiderivative $\int\frac{dw}{3-w^2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}\ln\frac{\sqrt3+w}{\sqrt3-w}$ and Barrow's rule (the lower limit gives $\ln1=0$):

$$t(w)=b\sqrt{\frac{m}{6V_0}}\;\ln\frac{\sqrt3+w}{\sqrt3-w}\ \xrightarrow[\ w\to\sqrt3\ ]{}\ \infty$$

For instance, $P$ passes $x=0$ ($w=\sqrt2$) at $t=b\sqrt{m/(6V_0)}\,\ln\big(5+2\sqrt6\big)\approx0.936\,b\sqrt{m/V_0}$, but it needs an infinite time to reach $x=-b$. The particle tends to the unstable equilibrium and never escapes: the statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • $V(2b)=2V_0=V(-b)$: the energy equals the top of the barrier. $F(2b)=-9V_0/b<0$, so $P$ does start to the left.
  • $E-V=V_0(u+1)^2(2-u)$ with $u=x/b$: $|\dot x|\propto|x+b|$ near $x=-b$ and the time $t=b\sqrt{m/(6V_0)}\,\ln\dfrac{\sqrt3+w}{\sqrt3-w}$, $w=\sqrt{2-x/b}$, diverges as $x\to-b$: $P$ approaches the barrier top asymptotically and never crosses it.

Part (b) · 1 point

Released at $x=0$

Statement: "If $P$ is released from rest at $x=0$, it oscillates about the stable equilibrium $x=b$ between $x=0$ and $x=2b$, symmetrically about $x=b$."

Step 1. Energy and initial force

$E=V(0)=0$. The initial force is $F=-\frac{dV}{dx}\big|_0=-\frac{3V_0}{b}(0-1)=\frac{3V_0}{b}>0$: $P$ moves to the right, into the well of the minimum $x=b$.

Step 2. Turning points

They solve $V(x)=E=0$: $V_0(u^3-3u)=V_0\,u\,(u-\sqrt3)(u+\sqrt3)=0$, so $u\in\{-\sqrt3,0,\sqrt3\}$. Between $u=0$ and $u=\sqrt3$ we have $V<0=E$ and the kinetic energy is positive; the motion is confined to $0\le x\le\sqrt3\,b$. At both ends $dV/dx\neq0$ ($-3V_0/b$ at $x=0$, $+6V_0/b$ at $x=\sqrt3\,b$), so they are simple turning points reached in a finite time, and the motion is a periodic oscillation (Notes §4.5).

Step 3. Not symmetric, not up to $2b$

$x=2b$ is never reached, since $V(2b)=2V_0>E$. The interval $[0,\sqrt3\,b]$ extends $b$ to the left of the minimum and only $(\sqrt3-1)\,b\approx0.732\,b$ to its right: the cubic well is steeper on the right. Symmetry about the minimum holds only for a parabolic well, i.e. for small oscillations, whose frequency follows from $V''(b)=\frac{1}{b^2}\frac{d^2V}{du^2}\big|_{u=1}=\frac{6V_0}{b^2}$: $\omega^2=\dfrac{6V_0}{mb^2}$ (Notes §4.6). The statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • $E=V(0)=0$; $V=V_0u(u-\sqrt3)(u+\sqrt3)$: turning points $x=0$ and $x=\sqrt3\,b\approx1.732\,b$ (not $2b$, where $V=2V_0>E$).
  • $x=b$ is stable ($V''>0$), small-oscillation frequency $\omega^2=6V_0/(mb^2)$; the motion is periodic but the distances to the turning points are $b$ and $0.732\,b$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $V_0/b$ is a force (N); $b\sqrt{m/V_0}$ is $\text{m}\sqrt{\text{kg}/\text{J}}=\text{s}$; $\omega^2=6V_0/(mb^2)$ is s$^{-2}$.
  • Limits: with $E$ slightly below $2V_0$, $P$ would turn back just before $x=-b$ after a long but finite time; slightly above, it would cross the barrier and run to $x\to-\infty$ ($V\to-\infty$, unbounded speed). $E=2V_0$ is the separatrix between both behaviours.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) assuming that "enough energy to reach the top" means crossing it; (2) placing the turning points symmetrically about the minimum; (3) forgetting to check the sign of the initial force to know where the particle goes.

C03 — Energy of a pendulum hanging from a slider: one particle, the system, and a driven support

A slider $A$ of mass $M$ moves along a smooth, fixed horizontal rail (the $x$ axis direction of the inertial frame $S_0=\{O;\mathbf i,\mathbf j\}$, $y$ upward, gravity $-g\,\mathbf j$); its abscissa is $x_A$. A bob $P$ of mass $m$ hangs from $A$ on a massless, inextensible string of length $\ell$ that stays taut. The angle $\theta$ is measured from the downward vertical, positive towards $+x$, and $\mathbf e_r=\sin\theta\,\mathbf i-\cos\theta\,\mathbf j$ (from $A$ to $P$), $\mathbf e_\theta=\cos\theta\,\mathbf i+\sin\theta\,\mathbf j$. The motion is planar. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Figure 1. Slider $A$ (mass $M$) on a smooth horizontal rail, abscissa $x_A$; bob $P$ (mass $m$) on a string of length $\ell$, angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical, positive towards $+x$.

Part (a) · 1 point

Free slider

The slider moves freely on the rail. Statement: "The mechanical energy of the bob alone, $E_P=\tfrac12m|\mathbf v_P|^2+mg\,y_P$, is not conserved, but the mechanical energy of the system slider plus bob, $E=\tfrac12M\dot x_A^2+E_P$, is conserved."

Step 1. Power of the tension on the bob

The string pulls $P$ towards $A$: $\mathbf T_P=-T\,\mathbf e_r$, $T\ge0$. The absolute velocity of $P$ is $\mathbf v_P=\dot x_A\,\mathbf i+\ell\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$ (derivative of $\mathbf r_P=x_A\mathbf i+\ell\mathbf e_r$ with $\dot{\mathbf e}_r=\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$). With $\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf i=\sin\theta$ and $\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf e_\theta=0$:

$$\dot W_{T,P}=\mathbf T_P\cdot\mathbf v_P=-T\dot x_A\sin\theta$$

The tension is orthogonal to the velocity of $P$ relative to $A$, but not to its absolute velocity. By the work-energy theorem with the weight as the only conservative force on $P$ (Notes §4.5), $\dot E_P=-T\dot x_A\sin\theta$, which is not zero whenever the slider moves with the string inclined: $E_P$ is not conserved.

Step 2. The system

On $A$ act $M\mathbf g$ and the rail reaction (both vertical, power zero against $\dot x_A\mathbf i$: smooth rail) and the string force $\mathbf T_A=+T\,\mathbf e_r$, of power $T\dot x_A\sin\theta$. Hence $\frac{d}{dt}\big(\tfrac12M\dot x_A^2\big)=T\dot x_A\sin\theta$ and

$$\dot E=T\dot x_A\sin\theta-T\dot x_A\sin\theta=T\,\mathbf e_r\cdot(\mathbf v_A-\mathbf v_P)=-T\ell\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf e_\theta=0$$

The internal pair of an inextensible string does no net work (Notes §4.4), so $E$ is conserved. The statement is TRUE.

Step 3. How much energy moves from $P$ to $A$

The horizontal external force is zero, so $p_x=(M+m)\dot x_A+m\ell\dot\theta\cos\theta$ is conserved, equal to $0$ for a release from rest at $\theta_0$. At $\theta=0$: $\dot x_A=-\dfrac{m\ell\dot\theta}{M+m}$. At $\theta=0$, $\mathbf e_\theta=\mathbf i$, so $\mathbf v_P=(\dot x_A+\ell\dot\theta)\,\mathbf i$ with $\dot x_A+\ell\dot\theta=\dfrac{M\ell\dot\theta}{M+m}$, and $y_P=-\ell\cos\theta$. Energy conservation between the release ($\theta_0$, at rest) and $\theta=0$:

$$\frac12M\frac{m^2\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{(M+m)^2}+\frac12m\frac{M^2\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{(M+m)^2}-mg\ell=-mg\ell\cos\theta_0$$

The two kinetic terms add up to $\dfrac{Mm(m+M)\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{2(M+m)^2}=\dfrac{Mm\,\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{2(M+m)}$, so $\dfrac{Mm\,\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{2(M+m)}=mg\ell(1-\cos\theta_0)$, which gives $\ell\dot\theta^2=\dfrac{2g(1-\cos\theta_0)(M+m)}{M}$ and

$$\tfrac12M\dot x_A^2=\frac{M}{2}\,\frac{m^2\ell^2\dot\theta^2}{(M+m)^2}=\frac{m^2g\ell\,(1-\cos\theta_0)}{M+m}>0$$

which is exactly what $E_P$ has lost.

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • Tension on $P$: $-T\mathbf e_r$, power $-T\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf v_P=-T\dot x_A\sin\theta\neq0$ in general, so $\dot E_P=-T\dot x_A\sin\theta$.
  • Tension on $A$: $+T\mathbf e_r$, power $+T\dot x_A\sin\theta$; the pair has total power $T\mathbf e_r\cdot(\mathbf v_A-\mathbf v_P)=0$ (inextensible string); rail reaction and $M\mathbf g$ are vertical, orthogonal to $\dot x_A\mathbf i$: $\dot E=0$.
  • Example: released from rest at $\theta_0$, at $\theta=0$ the slider has $\tfrac12M\dot x_A^2=\dfrac{m^2g\ell(1-\cos\theta_0)}{M+m}$, energy that $P$ has lost.

Part (b) · 1 point

Driven slider

A motor now imposes $x_A(t)=b\sin(\Omega t)$. Let $S_1$ be the frame that translates with $A$. Statement: "In $S_1$ the tension does no work on $P$ and the weight is conservative; therefore $E_{rel}=\tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2-mg\ell\cos\theta$ is a first integral of the motion of the bob."

Step 1. Forces and work in $S_1$

$S_1$ translates with acceleration $\ddot x_A\mathbf i$, so (Notes §4.9) the forces on $P$ in $S_1$ are $m\mathbf g$, $-T\mathbf e_r$ and the inertia force $\mathbf F_{in}=-m\ddot x_A\,\mathbf i$ (no Coriolis or centrifugal terms: $\boldsymbol\omega_{10}=\mathbf 0$). With $\mathbf v_{rel}=\ell\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$ the tension does no work, and the weight has the potential $-mg\ell\cos\theta$. The power of the inertia force is

$$\mathbf F_{in}\cdot\mathbf v_{rel}=-m\ddot x_A\,\ell\dot\theta\,(\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf e_\theta)=-m\ddot x_A\,\ell\dot\theta\cos\theta$$

Step 2. Rate of change of $E_{rel}$

Projecting $m\mathbf a_{rel}=m\mathbf g-T\mathbf e_r+\mathbf F_{in}$ on $\mathbf e_\theta$: $m\ell\ddot\theta=-mg\sin\theta-m\ddot x_A\cos\theta$. Differentiating $E_{rel}$ with the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\theta=-\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$:

$$\dot E_{rel}=m\ell\dot\theta\,\big(\ell\ddot\theta+g\sin\theta\big)=-m\ddot x_A\,\ell\dot\theta\cos\theta$$

With $\ddot x_A=-b\Omega^2\sin(\Omega t)$: $\dot E_{rel}=mb\Omega^2\ell\,\sin(\Omega t)\,\dot\theta\cos\theta$. For example at $\Omega t=\pi/2$ with $\theta=0$ and $\dot\theta\neq0$ it equals $mb\Omega^2\ell\dot\theta\neq0$. $E_{rel}$ is not a first integral: the statement is FALSE.

Step 3. Why no other energy works either

For a constant $\ddot x_A=a_0$ (as in a uniformly accelerating support) the inertia force would be uniform and conservative, with potential $ma_0\ell\sin\theta$. Here, if we try $E^*=E_{rel}+m\ddot x_A(t)\,\ell\sin\theta$, the explicit time dependence leaves

$$\dot E^*=m\,\frac{d^3x_A}{dt^3}\,\ell\sin\theta=-mb\Omega^3\cos(\Omega t)\,\ell\sin\theta\neq0$$

The motor keeps pumping energy in and out of the bob.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • In $S_1$: $\mathbf v_{rel}=\ell\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$, $-T\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf v_{rel}=0$ (this part is true).
  • Equation: $\ell\ddot\theta=-g\sin\theta-\ddot x_A\cos\theta$; $\dot E_{rel}=-m\ddot x_A\,\ell\dot\theta\cos\theta=mb\Omega^2\ell\sin(\Omega t)\,\dot\theta\cos\theta\neq0$.
  • The inertia force depends explicitly on $t$: even $E_{rel}+m\ddot x_A\ell\sin\theta$ changes, at the rate $m\,\dfrac{d^3x_A}{dt^3}\,\ell\sin\theta$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $T\dot x_A$ is N m s$^{-1}$ = W; $mb\Omega^2\ell\dot\theta$ is kg m s$^{-2}$ m s$^{-1}$ = W; $m^2g\ell/(M+m)$ is J.
  • Limits: $M\to\infty$ makes the slider a fixed support ($\dot x_A\to0$): the energy transfer $m^2g\ell(1-\cos\theta_0)/(M+m)\to0$ and $E_P$ is conserved. $b\to0$ in (b) gives the ordinary pendulum and $\dot E_{rel}\to0$.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) "the tension is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work": true only for the velocity relative to the point the string is tied to; (2) forgetting the inertia force when writing the energy in a non-inertial frame; (3) treating a time-dependent inertia force as conservative.

C04 — Angular momentum under a non-central force and about a moving point

A point particle $P$ of mass $m$ moves with respect to the inertial frame $S_0=\{O;\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\}$, with no gravity. $r=|\mathbf r_P|$ is its distance to $O$, $\mathbf u_r=\mathbf r_P/r$, and $\{\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\varphi,\mathbf k\}$ is the cylindrical basis about the $z$ axis. The angular momentum about a point $A$ is $\mathbf H_A=\mathbf r_{AP}\times m\mathbf v_P$ (absolute velocity, Notes Eq. 4.48). Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Part (a) · 1 point

A force with an azimuthal part

$P$ moves under $\mathbf F=f(r)\,\mathbf u_r+q(r)\,\mathbf e_\varphi$, with $f$ and $q$ known functions and $q\neq0$. At $t=0$, $P$ is in the plane $z=0$ and its velocity is parallel to that plane. Statement: "Since $\mathbf F$ is not central, $\mathbf H_O$ is not conserved, and therefore the trajectory of $P$ cannot be planar."

Step 1. The plane $z=0$ is invariant

In Cartesian components $\mathbf u_r=(x,y,z)/r$ and $\mathbf e_\varphi=(-y,x,0)/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, so the $z$ component of Newton's law is

$$m\ddot z=f(r)\,\frac{z}{r}$$

With $z(0)=0$ and $\dot z(0)=0$, the function $z(t)\equiv0$ satisfies this equation and the initial conditions; by uniqueness it is the solution. $P$ never leaves the plane $z=0$: the trajectory is planar.

Step 2. Angular momentum is not conserved

On the plane, $\mathbf r_P=R\,\mathbf e_R$ (with $R=r$) and $\mathbf M_O=\mathbf r_P\times\mathbf F=R\,\mathbf e_R\times\big(f\,\mathbf e_R+q\,\mathbf e_\varphi\big)=R\,q(R)\,\mathbf k\neq\mathbf 0$. By Notes Eq. 4.53 ($O$ fixed), $\dot{\mathbf H}_O=\mathbf M_O$. With $\mathbf v_P=\dot R\,\mathbf e_R+R\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi$:

$$\mathbf H_O=R\,\mathbf e_R\times m\big(\dot R\,\mathbf e_R+R\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi\big)=mR^2\dot\varphi\,\mathbf k,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\big(mR^2\dot\varphi\big)=m\big(2R\dot R\dot\varphi+R^2\ddot\varphi\big)=mR\,a_\varphi=R\,q(R)$$

So $\mathbf H_O$ is not conserved (the areal velocity $\tfrac12R^2\dot\varphi$ is not constant either), but it always points along $\mathbf k$.

Step 3. Where the reasoning breaks

Conservation of $\mathbf H_O$ is sufficient for planar motion (Notes §4.8.1, property 2) but not necessary. Here $\mathbf M_O$ is parallel to $\mathbf H_O$: only the magnitude changes, and $\mathbf r_P\cdot\mathbf H_O=0$ keeps $P$ in the fixed plane normal to $\mathbf k$. The statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE (the first half is true, the conclusion is not).
  • $F_z=f(r)\,z/r$: zero on $z=0$, so with $z(0)=\dot z(0)=0$ the solution keeps $z\equiv0$: the motion is planar.
  • $\mathbf M_O=r\,q(r)\,\mathbf k\neq\mathbf 0$ on the plane: $\mathbf H_O=mR^2\dot\varphi\,\mathbf k$ changes magnitude, $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big(mR^2\dot\varphi\big)=R\,q(R)$, but keeps its direction $\mathbf k$.

Part (b) · 1 point

Angular momentum about a moving point

Now $P$ moves under a central force towards $O$ on a circle of radius $\rho$ with constant angular rate $n$: $\mathbf r_P=\rho(\cos nt\,\mathbf i+\sin nt\,\mathbf j)$. A point $A$ moves with constant velocity, $\mathbf r_A=ut\,\mathbf i$ ($u>0$). Statement: "A frame translating with $A$ at constant velocity is inertial, so the angular momentum of $P$ about $A$, $\mathbf H_A$, is conserved just like $\mathbf H_O$."

Step 1. The theorem about a moving point

Differentiating $\mathbf H_A=(\mathbf r_P-\mathbf r_A)\times m\mathbf v_P$ with the product rule:

$$\frac{d\mathbf H_A}{dt}=(\mathbf v_P-\mathbf v_A)\times m\mathbf v_P+(\mathbf r_P-\mathbf r_A)\times m\mathbf a_P=\mathbf M_A-\mathbf v_A\times m\mathbf v_P$$

because $\mathbf v_P\times\mathbf v_P=\mathbf 0$ and $m\mathbf a_P=\mathbf F$ (Notes Eq. 4.52). Both terms must vanish for conservation. The force passes through $O$, not through $A$: $\mathbf M_A=(\mathbf r_P-\mathbf r_A)\times\mathbf F=-\mathbf r_A\times\mathbf F$, since $\mathbf r_P\times\mathbf F=\mathbf 0$.

Step 2. Direct computation

$\mathbf v_P=n\rho(-\sin nt\,\mathbf i+\cos nt\,\mathbf j)$ and $\mathbf r_P-\mathbf r_A=(\rho\cos nt-ut)\,\mathbf i+\rho\sin nt\,\mathbf j$, so

$$H_{A,z}=m\big[(\rho\cos nt-ut)\,n\rho\cos nt+\rho\sin nt\;n\rho\sin nt\big]=m\big(n\rho^2-ut\,n\rho\cos nt\big)$$

Differentiating with the product rule, $\frac{d}{dt}(t\cos nt)=\cos nt-nt\sin nt$:

$$\dot H_{A,z}=-mun\rho\,\big(\cos nt-nt\sin nt\big),\qquad \dot H_{A,z}(0)=-mun\rho\neq0$$

At $t=0$, $A=O$ and $\mathbf M_A=\mathbf 0$, yet $\mathbf H_A$ changes because of $-\mathbf v_A\times m\mathbf v_P=-u\,\mathbf i\times mn\rho\,\mathbf j=-mun\rho\,\mathbf k$.

Step 3. The inertial frame of $A$ does not help

In the frame $S_A$ that translates with $A$ (inertial, since $\mathbf a_A=\mathbf 0$), Notes Eq. 4.55 gives $\dot{\mathbf H}_{A,A}=\mathbf M_A$ for $\mathbf H_{A,A}=\mathbf r_{AP}\times m(\mathbf v_P-\mathbf v_A)$. With $\mathbf F=-mn^2\mathbf r_P$, $\mathbf M_A=-ut\,\mathbf i\times(-mn^2\rho)(\cos nt\,\mathbf i+\sin nt\,\mathbf j)=mun^2\rho\,t\sin nt\,\mathbf k$, nonzero for instance at $nt=\pi/2$. A frame being inertial does not make the force central about its origin. The statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • $\mathbf M_A=\mathbf r_{AP}\times\mathbf F=-\mathbf r_A\times\mathbf F\neq\mathbf 0$ once $A\neq O$, and $\mathbf v_A\times m\mathbf v_P\neq\mathbf 0$.
  • $H_{A,z}=m\big(n\rho^2-ut\,n\rho\cos nt\big)$, $\ \dot H_{A,z}=-mun\rho\,(\cos nt-nt\sin nt)$, equal to $-mun\rho\neq0$ at $t=0$.
  • Even the relative angular momentum $\mathbf H_{A,A}$ of Notes Eq. 4.55 changes: $\dot H_{A,A,z}=M_{A,z}=mun^2\rho\,t\sin nt$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $mR^2\dot\varphi$ and $mn\rho^2$ are kg m$^2$ s$^{-1}$; $R\,q(R)$ and $mun\rho$ are N m = kg m$^2$ s$^{-2}$.
  • Limits: in (a), $q\to0$ gives a central force and $mR^2\dot\varphi$ constant; if the initial velocity had a $z$ component, $F_z=fz/r$ would not vanish and the motion would in general leave the plane. In (b), $u\to0$ ($A$ fixed at $O$) recovers $\dot{\mathbf H}_A=\mathbf 0$.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) treating "angular momentum conserved" as the only route to planar motion; (2) applying $\dot{\mathbf H}_A=\mathbf M_A$ about a moving point without the term $-\mathbf v_A\times m\mathbf v_P$; (3) thinking that a force is central about every point of an inertial frame.

C05 — Work of inertia forces: the Coriolis force on a rotating rod and a free particle in an accelerating frame

$S_0$ is an inertial frame. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Part (a) · 1 point

Bead on a rotating rod

A straight, smooth rod lies in a horizontal plane and rotates about a fixed vertical axis through its end $O$ with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\,\mathbf k$ ($\mathbf k$ upward). A bead of mass $m$ slides on the rod; $r$ is its distance to $O$, and $S_1=\{O;\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\varphi,\mathbf k\}$ is the frame of the rod ($\mathbf e_r$ along the rod). The bead is released at rest relative to the rod at $r=r_0$. Statement: "In $S_1$ the Coriolis force does work on the bead, and this work is what makes the relative kinetic energy of the bead grow as it slides outwards."

Step 1. Forces in $S_1$

$S_1$ rotates with constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$ about the fixed point $O$, so (Notes §4.9) $m\mathbf a_{rel}=\mathbf F+\mathbf F_{cf}+\mathbf F_{cor}$ with the centrifugal force $\mathbf F_{cf}=-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times r\,\mathbf e_r)=m\Omega^2r\,\mathbf e_r$ and the Coriolis force

$$\mathbf F_{cor}=-2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{rel}=-2m\,\Omega\,\mathbf k\times\dot r\,\mathbf e_r=-2m\Omega\dot r\,\mathbf e_\varphi$$

(no Euler term since $\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}=\mathbf 0$). The real forces are the weight and the rod reaction, both orthogonal to $\mathbf e_r$ on a smooth rod.

Step 2. Powers

With $\mathbf v_{rel}=\dot r\,\mathbf e_r$: $\ \mathbf F_{cor}\cdot\mathbf v_{rel}=-2m\Omega\dot r^2\,(\mathbf e_\varphi\cdot\mathbf e_r)=0$. This holds for any motion, because $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{rel}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf v_{rel}$: the Coriolis force never works in the moving frame. Only the centrifugal force works, with power $m\Omega^2r\dot r$.

Step 3. Relative kinetic energy

Along $\mathbf e_r$: $m\ddot r=m\Omega^2r$, whose solution with $r(0)=r_0$, $\dot r(0)=0$ is $r=r_0\cosh\Omega t$. Work-energy theorem in $S_1$, with the work of the centrifugal force from $r_0$ to $r$ given by Barrow's rule:

$$\tfrac12m\dot r^2-0=\int_{r_0}^{r}m\Omega^2s\,ds=\Big[\tfrac12m\Omega^2s^2\Big]_{r_0}^{r}=\tfrac12m\Omega^2\big(r^2-r_0^2\big)$$

The growth comes from the centrifugal force; the Coriolis force only presses the bead against the rod, where the reaction $N\mathbf e_\varphi+N_z\mathbf k$ balances it: along $\mathbf e_\varphi$, $N=2m\Omega\dot r$ against the Coriolis force, and along $\mathbf k$, $N_z=mg$ against the weight $-mg\,\mathbf k$ (both components are orthogonal to the smooth rod). The statement is FALSE.

Step 4. Seen from $S_0$

In $S_0$ there are no inertia forces; the energy is supplied by the rod reaction $N\mathbf e_\varphi+mg\,\mathbf k$, whose power with the absolute velocity $\mathbf v=\dot r\,\mathbf e_r+\Omega r\,\mathbf e_\varphi$ is $N\Omega r=2m\Omega^2r\dot r$ (the vertical part $mg\,\mathbf k$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf v$), exactly $\frac{d}{dt}\big[\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+\Omega^2r^2)\big]$ with $\ddot r=\Omega^2r$.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • Coriolis force $-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{rel}=-2m\Omega\dot r\,\mathbf e_\varphi\perp\mathbf v_{rel}$: zero power, always.
  • The work comes from the centrifugal force $m\Omega^2r\,\mathbf e_r$: $\tfrac12m\dot r^2=\tfrac12m\Omega^2(r^2-r_0^2)$, $r=r_0\cosh\Omega t$.
  • The rod reaction $N\mathbf e_\varphi+mg\,\mathbf k$, $N=2m\Omega\dot r$: its horizontal part balances the Coriolis force in $S_1$ and its vertical part the weight; in $S_0$ it does work at the rate $2m\Omega^2r\dot r$.

Part (b) · 1 point

Free particle in an accelerating frame

The frame $S_1$ translates without rotating, with constant acceleration $\mathbf a_{O_1}=a_0\,\mathbf e$ ($a_0>0$, $\mathbf e$ a fixed unit vector) with respect to $S_0$. A particle subject to no real force (no gravity) is observed from $S_1$. Statement: "The only inertia force in $S_1$ is $-m a_0\,\mathbf e$, and the relative trajectory of the particle is a straight line only when its initial relative velocity is zero or parallel to $\mathbf e$; otherwise it is a parabola."

Step 1. Inertia forces

For a frame in pure translation, $\mathbf a_{abs}=\mathbf a_{O_1}+\mathbf a_{rel}$ (Notes §3.2 with $\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf 0$), so $m\mathbf a_{rel}=\mathbf F-m\mathbf a_{O_1}$: the only inertia force is $-ma_0\,\mathbf e$, uniform and constant. With $\mathbf F=\mathbf 0$: $\mathbf a_{rel}=-a_0\,\mathbf e$.

Step 2. Relative motion

Integrating twice with Barrow's rule from $0$ to $t$: $\mathbf v_{rel}=\mathbf v_0-a_0t\,\mathbf e$ and $\mathbf r_{rel}=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0t-\tfrac12a_0t^2\,\mathbf e$. The path is straight exactly when $\mathbf v_{rel}$ keeps its direction, i.e. when $\mathbf v_{rel}\times\mathbf a_{rel}=\mathbf 0$ (zero curvature, Notes §2.5):

$$\mathbf v_{rel}\times\mathbf a_{rel}=(\mathbf v_0-a_0t\,\mathbf e)\times(-a_0\,\mathbf e)=-a_0\,\mathbf v_0\times\mathbf e$$

which vanishes if and only if $\mathbf v_0=\mathbf 0$ or $\mathbf v_0\parallel\mathbf e$.

Step 3. The parabola

With $\mathbf e=\mathbf i$ and $\mathbf v_0=U\mathbf j$: $x=x_0-\tfrac12a_0t^2$, $y=y_0+Ut$. Eliminating $t=(y-y_0)/U$ gives $x-x_0=-\dfrac{a_0}{2U^2}(y-y_0)^2$, a parabola. Both claims hold: TRUE. (In $S_0$ the same particle moves in a straight line at constant velocity.)

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • $\mathbf F_{in}=-m\,\mathbf a_{O_1}=-ma_0\,\mathbf e$; $\ \mathbf r_{rel}=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0t-\tfrac12a_0t^2\,\mathbf e$.
  • $\mathbf v_{rel}\times\mathbf a_{rel}=-a_0\,\mathbf v_0\times\mathbf e$ (constant): the path is straight if and only if $\mathbf v_0\times\mathbf e=\mathbf 0$.
  • Example $\mathbf v_0=U\mathbf j$, $\mathbf e=\mathbf i$: $x-x_0=-\dfrac{a_0}{2U^2}(y-y_0)^2$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $m\Omega\dot r$ and $ma_0$ are N; $m\Omega^2r^2$ is J; $a_0y^2/U^2$ is m.
  • Limits: $\Omega\to0$ in (a) gives $r\equiv r_0$ (no driving force). $a_0\to0$ in (b) makes $S_1$ inertial and every free trajectory straight.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) attributing work to the Coriolis force (it is always normal to $\mathbf v_{rel}$); (2) adding centrifugal or Coriolis forces in a frame that only translates; (3) "a free particle moves in a straight line" in a non-inertial frame.

C06 — Particle on a moving sphere: degrees of freedom, constraint type and the work of a smooth reaction

A rigid sphere of radius $R$ has its centre $C$ on the vertical axis $Oz$ of the inertial frame $S_0=\{O;\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\}$ ($\mathbf k$ upward, gravity $-g\,\mathbf k$). A motor moves the sphere without rotating it so that $\mathbf r_C=h(t)\,\mathbf k$ with the prescribed law $h(t)=H+b\sin\Omega t$ ($H$, $b$, $\Omega$ constants). A point particle $P$ of mass $m$ rests on the outer surface of the sphere, which is smooth, and can leave it. Use spherical coordinates about $C$: polar angle $\theta$ from the upward vertical and azimuth $\varphi$ about $Oz$, with the basis $\{\mathbf u_r,\mathbf e_\theta,\mathbf e_\varphi\}$, so that $\mathbf r_P=h\,\mathbf k+R\,\mathbf u_r$ while $P$ is in contact. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Figure 1. Sphere of radius $R$ with centre $C$ at the prescribed height $h(t)=H+b\sin\Omega t$ on the vertical axis; particle $P$ on its outer surface, polar angle $\theta$ from the upward vertical, azimuth $\varphi$ about the axis.

Part (a) · 1 point

Degrees of freedom

Statement: "While $P$ is in contact with the sphere it has a single degree of freedom, because two conditions are imposed on it: it must lie on the sphere, and the sphere moves with the law $h(t)$."

Step 1. Coordinates and constraint equations

A free particle has 3 degrees of freedom (Notes §2.7). Contact with the sphere imposes one scalar equation on the position of $P$:

$$f(\mathbf r,t)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+\big(z-h(t)\big)^2}-R=0$$

The motion of the sphere, $h(t)$, is a known function of time: it enters this equation but does not add another equation linking $x,y,z$. It removes no further degree of freedom.

Step 2. Count

$3-1=2$ degrees of freedom; $\theta$ and $\varphi$ fix the position at each instant through $\mathbf r_P=h(t)\,\mathbf k+R\,\mathbf u_r(\theta,\varphi)$. The Lagrangian in $(\theta,\varphi)$ gives two equations of motion, each with its own second derivative (their mass matrix has determinant $m^2R^4\sin^2\theta\neq0$ off the poles), which confirms two independent coordinates. The statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE.
  • One constraint on $P$: $f(\mathbf r,t)=|\mathbf r-h(t)\mathbf k|-R=0$. The law $h(t)$ is data, not a second constraint.
  • $3-1=2$ degrees of freedom while in contact, coordinates $(\theta,\varphi)$; 3 after $P$ leaves the sphere.

Part (b) · 1 point

Classification and work of the reaction

Statement: "The constraint is holonomic, rheonomic and unilateral, and although the sphere is smooth its normal reaction does work on $P$ in $S_0$."

Step 1. Classification (Notes §2.7)

The constraint $f(\mathbf r,t)=0$ involves positions and time but no velocities: holonomic. It depends explicitly on $t$ through $h(t)$, $\partial f/\partial t=-(z-h)\dot h/|\mathbf r-h\mathbf k|$, which is not identically zero (it vanishes only where $z=h$ or at the instants with $\dot h=0$): rheonomic. $P$ lies on the outside and can lose contact: the true condition is $f\ge0$ with $N\ge0$, an inequality: unilateral.

Step 2. Velocity of $P$

Differentiating $\mathbf r_P=h\,\mathbf k+R\,\mathbf u_r$ with $\dot{\mathbf u}_r=\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta+\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi$ (chain rule on $\mathbf u_r(\theta(t),\varphi(t))$):

$$\mathbf v_P=\dot h\,\mathbf k+R\big(\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta+\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi\big),\qquad \dot h=b\Omega\cos\Omega t$$

Step 3. Power of the reaction

Smooth means the reaction is normal to the surface: $\mathbf N=N\,\mathbf u_r$. With $\mathbf u_r\cdot\mathbf e_\theta=\mathbf u_r\cdot\mathbf e_\varphi=0$ and $\mathbf u_r\cdot\mathbf k=\cos\theta$:

$$\dot W_N=\mathbf N\cdot\mathbf v_P=N\dot h\cos\theta=Nb\Omega\cos(\Omega t)\cos\theta$$

At $t=0$ with $P$ at the top ($\theta=0$) it equals $Nb\Omega\neq0$. A smooth reaction does no work only against a fixed surface (Notes §6.4); here the surface pushes $P$ as it moves. In the frame translating with $C$ the velocity of $P$ is $\mathbf v_{rel}$, tangent to the sphere, and there $\mathbf N\cdot\mathbf v_{rel}=0$. All the claims hold: TRUE.

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • Holonomic: $f$ depends on position and time only; rheonomic: $\partial f/\partial t$ not identically zero; unilateral: $f\ge0$ (outer surface, $N\ge0$, $P$ may leave).
  • $\mathbf v_P=\dot h\,\mathbf k+R\big(\dot\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta+\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\mathbf e_\varphi\big)$, $\ \mathbf N\cdot\mathbf v_P=N\dot h\cos\theta=Nb\Omega\cos\Omega t\cos\theta\neq0$ in general.
  • In the frame translating with $C$ the reaction does no work ($\mathbf u_r\cdot\mathbf v_{rel}=0$).

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $N\dot h$ is N m s$^{-1}$ = W; $f$ is a length.
  • Limits: $b\to0$ (fixed sphere) gives a scleronomic constraint and $\dot W_N=0$: mechanical energy is then conserved. If $P$ were a bead threaded on a fixed meridian, a second constraint would leave 1 degree of freedom.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) counting a prescribed motion as an extra constraint; (2) "smooth implies no work" for a moving surface; (3) calling the constraint bilateral when the particle is only resting on the outside.

C07 — Damped and forced oscillator: energy decay per period and where the amplitude really peaks

A block of mass $m=2$ kg moves along the $x$ axis of an inertial frame, attached to a spring of stiffness $k=50$ N/m and to a viscous damper of constant $c=4$ N s/m. Its equation of motion is $m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=F(t)$, and (Notes Eq. 5.23) $\gamma=c/(2m)$, $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Part (a) · 1 point

Free motion

With $F(t)=0$. Statement: "The motion is underdamped, and over each period $T=2\pi/\omega$ of the damped oscillation the mechanical energy $E=\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12kx^2$ is multiplied by the factor $e^{-2\pi/\sqrt6}\approx0.0769$, the square of the factor by which the amplitude decays."

Step 1. Type of motion

$\gamma=\dfrac{c}{2m}=\dfrac{4}{4}=1$ s$^{-1}$ and $\omega_0=\sqrt{50/2}=5$ s$^{-1}$. The roots of $mp^2+cp+k=0$ are $p=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}=-1\pm2\sqrt6\,i$: complex, so the motion is underdamped (Notes §5.3.1), with $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}=\sqrt{24}=2\sqrt6$ s$^{-1}$ and $T=2\pi/\omega\approx1.283$ s.

Step 2. Exact decay over one period

The general solution is $x=e^{-\gamma t}\big(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\big)$. Since $\cos$ and $\sin$ have period $T=2\pi/\omega$, $x(t+T)=e^{-\gamma(t+T)}\big(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\big)=e^{-\gamma T}x(t)$, and differentiating this identity, $\dot x(t+T)=e^{-\gamma T}\dot x(t)$. Both terms of $E$ are quadratic:

$$E(t+T)=e^{-2\gamma T}E(t),\qquad \gamma T=\frac{2\pi}{2\sqrt6}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt6}$$

Step 3. Numbers

Amplitude factor $e^{-\pi/\sqrt6}\approx0.277$; energy factor $e^{-2\pi/\sqrt6}=\big(e^{-\pi/\sqrt6}\big)^2\approx0.0769$. The energy is dissipated by the damper: $\dot E=\dot x\,(m\ddot x+kx)=-c\dot x^2\le0$. The statement is TRUE.

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • $\gamma=1$ s$^{-1}$ $<\omega_0=5$ s$^{-1}$: underdamped; $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}=2\sqrt6\approx4.899$ s$^{-1}$, $T\approx1.283$ s.
  • $x(t+T)=e^{-\gamma T}x(t)$ and $\dot x(t+T)=e^{-\gamma T}\dot x(t)$ exactly, with $\gamma T=\pi/\sqrt6$: amplitude factor $e^{-\pi/\sqrt6}\approx0.277$, energy factor $e^{-2\pi/\sqrt6}\approx0.0769$.

Part (b) · 1 point

Forced motion

Now $F(t)=F_1\cos\omega_1t$ with $F_1=10$ N. Statement: "In the steady state the amplitude is largest when the forcing frequency equals $\omega_0=5$ s$^{-1}$; there the response lags the force by $90^\circ$ and its amplitude is $0.5$ m."

Step 1. Steady response at $\omega_1=\omega_0$

After the transient dies out (Notes §5.4), $x=a_1\cos(\omega_1t+\alpha_1)$ with $a_1$ above and $\tan\alpha_1=-\dfrac{2\gamma\omega_1}{\omega_0^2-\omega_1^2}$ (Notes Eq. 5.42). At $\omega_1=\omega_0$ the denominator of $\tan\alpha_1$ vanishes and $\alpha_1=-\pi/2$: a lag of $90^\circ$. The amplitude is

$$a_1(\omega_0)=\frac{F_1/m}{2\gamma\omega_0}=\frac{10/2}{2\cdot1\cdot5}=0.5\ \text{m}$$

Step 2. Where the maximum over $\omega_1$ is

Let $s=\omega_1^2$ and $D(s)=(\omega_0^2-s)^2+4\gamma^2s$. $a_1$ is largest where $D$ is smallest:

$$\frac{dD}{ds}=-2(\omega_0^2-s)+4\gamma^2=0\ \Longrightarrow\ s^*=\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2=25-2=23\ \text{s}^{-2}$$

($d^2D/ds^2=2>0$: a minimum). Substituting, $D(s^*)=4\gamma^4+4\gamma^2(\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2)=4\gamma^2(\omega_0^2-\gamma^2)$, so

$$a_{1,max}=\frac{F_1/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}}=\frac{5}{2\sqrt{24}}\approx0.510\ \text{m}>0.5\ \text{m}$$

at $\omega_1=\sqrt{23}\approx4.796$ s$^{-1}$, where $\tan\alpha_1=-\dfrac{2\sqrt{23}}{2}=-\sqrt{23}$, $\alpha_1\approx-78.2^\circ$.

Step 3. Verdict

The result "maximum at $\omega_0$" of Notes Eq. 5.45 holds when $\omega_1$ is fixed and $\omega_0$ is varied; when the forcing frequency is varied, the peak is shifted below $\omega_0$ by the damping. The statement is FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE (the phase and the value at $\omega_1=\omega_0$ are right; the location of the maximum is not).
  • At $\omega_1=\omega_0$: $a_1=\dfrac{F_1}{2m\gamma\omega_0}=0.5$ m, $\tan\alpha_1\to-\infty$, $\alpha_1=-90^\circ$.
  • Maximum over $\omega_1$ at $\omega_1^2=\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2=23$ s$^{-2}$ ($\omega_1\approx4.796$ s$^{-1}$): $a_{1,max}=\dfrac{F_1}{2m\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}}=\dfrac{5}{2\sqrt{24}}\approx0.510$ m $>0.5$ m, with $\alpha_1\approx-78.2^\circ$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $\gamma$, $\omega_0$, $\omega_1$ are s$^{-1}$; $F_1/m$ is m s$^{-2}$ and $(F_1/m)/(\gamma\omega_0)$ is m.
  • Limits: for $\gamma\to0$ the peak returns to $\omega_0$ and $a_{1,max}\to\infty$; for $\gamma\ge\omega_0/\sqrt2$ there is no peak at all ($s^*\le0$) and $a_1$ decreases from its static value $F_1/k=0.2$ m.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) using the same decay factor for the amplitude and for the energy; (2) placing the amplitude peak at $\omega_0$ for any damping; (3) confusing the $90^\circ$ phase lag (exactly at $\omega_0$) with the amplitude maximum.

C08 — Apsidal speeds and a perigee impulse: areal velocity, vis-viva and which apse moves

A satellite moves about the Earth (gravitational parameter $\mu$, the Earth's centre $F$ fixed at a focus) on a Kepler ellipse of eccentricity $e=1/3$ and perigee radius $r_p$. $\Pi$ is the perigee and $A$ the apogee; $a$ is the semi-major axis. Decide whether each statement is TRUE or FALSE and justify the answer. A verdict without a justification scores nothing.

Figure 1. Orbit of eccentricity $e=1/3$ about the focus $F$ (perigee $\Pi$ at $r_p$, apogee $A$ at $r_a=2r_p$) and, dashed, the orbit after a tangential impulse $\Delta v$ at $\Pi$ (apogee $A'$).

Part (a) · 1 point

Speeds and areal velocity

Statement: "The speed at perigee is twice the speed at apogee; consequently the radius vector $\mathbf r$ from $F$ to the satellite sweeps area twice as fast when the satellite passes the perigee as when it passes the apogee."

Step 1. Geometry of the ellipse

With $r_p=a(1-e)$ and $r_a=a(1+e)$: $a=\dfrac{r_p}{1-1/3}=\dfrac32r_p$ and $r_a=\dfrac32r_p\cdot\dfrac43=2r_p$.

Step 2. Speeds at the apsides

The force is central, so $\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$ is constant (Notes §4.8.1). At both apsides $\mathbf r\perp\mathbf v$, hence $h=r_pv_p=r_av_a$ and $\dfrac{v_p}{v_a}=\dfrac{r_a}{r_p}=2$. The vis-viva equation $v^2=\mu\big(\tfrac2r-\tfrac1a\big)$ (Notes §8.5) confirms it:

$$v_p^2=\mu\Big(\frac{2}{r_p}-\frac{2}{3r_p}\Big)=\frac{4\mu}{3r_p},\qquad v_a^2=\mu\Big(\frac{2}{2r_p}-\frac{2}{3r_p}\Big)=\frac{\mu}{3r_p},\qquad \frac{v_p^2}{v_a^2}=4$$

Step 3. Areal velocity

The area swept per unit time is $\dot A=\tfrac12|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|=\tfrac12h$, a constant: at perigee $\tfrac12r_pv_p$, at apogee $\tfrac12(2r_p)\,\tfrac{v_p}{2}=\tfrac12r_pv_p$, the same. The satellite is faster near perigee precisely because it is closer, so that $rv$ stays fixed. The conclusion is wrong: FALSE.

Key points
  • FALSE (the first half is true).
  • $r_a=r_p\dfrac{1+e}{1-e}=2r_p$, $a=\tfrac32r_p$; $h=r_pv_p=r_av_a\Rightarrow v_p/v_a=2$. Vis-viva: $v_p^2=\dfrac{4\mu}{3r_p}$, which equals $\dfrac{2\mu}{a}$ only because $a=\tfrac32r_p$ for this orbit; $v_a^2=\dfrac{\mu}{3r_p}$.
  • Areal velocity $\dot A=\tfrac12|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|=h/2$: the same at every point of the orbit (Kepler's second law).

A tangential impulse at perigee

At $\Pi$ a short engine burn along the velocity raises the speed from $v_p$ to $v_p'=\sqrt{9/8}\,v_p$ (an increase of about $6.07\,\%$) without changing the position. Statement: "The perigee radius is unchanged and the apogee radius grows from $2r_p$ to $3r_p$."

Step 1. The burn point remains an apse

The impulse changes $\mathbf v$ along its own direction, so right after it $\mathbf v'\perp\mathbf r$ at $r=r_p$: the radial velocity is zero and $\Pi$ is an apse of the new orbit. Since $v_p'^2=\dfrac{3\mu}{2r_p}$ exceeds the circular value $\mu/r_p$, the satellite is moving too fast to stay at $r_p$ and rises: $\Pi$ is the perigee of the new orbit.

Step 2. New energy and angular momentum

$$\varepsilon'=\frac{v_p'^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r_p}=\frac{3\mu}{4r_p}-\frac{\mu}{r_p}=-\frac{\mu}{4r_p}\ \Rightarrow\ a'=-\frac{\mu}{2\varepsilon'}=2r_p,\qquad h'^2=r_p^2v_p'^2=\tfrac32\mu r_p$$

Step 3. Apse radii

At an apse $v=h'/r$, so $\varepsilon'=\dfrac{h'^2}{2r^2}-\dfrac{\mu}{r}$. Multiplying by $r^2$: $-\dfrac{\mu}{4r_p}r^2+\mu r-\dfrac34\mu r_p=0$, i.e. $r^2-4r_pr+3r_p^2=(r-r_p)(r-3r_p)=0$. The perigee stays at $r_p$ and the new apogee $A'$ is at $3r_p$ (consistent with $r_p+r_a'=2a'=4r_p$), with $e'=\dfrac{3r_p-r_p}{3r_p+r_p}=\dfrac12$. The statement is TRUE.

Key points
  • TRUE.
  • $v_p'^2=\tfrac98\cdot\dfrac{4\mu}{3r_p}=\dfrac{3\mu}{2r_p}>\dfrac{\mu}{r_p}$ (above circular speed, so $\Pi$ stays the perigee).
  • $\varepsilon'=-\dfrac{\mu}{4r_p}$, $a'=2r_p$; $h'^2=\tfrac32\mu r_p$; the apse equation $\varepsilon'=\dfrac{h'^2}{2r^2}-\dfrac{\mu}{r}$ has roots $r=r_p$ and $r=3r_p$; $e'=1/2$.

Interpretation and checks

  • Dimensional check: $\mu$ is m$^3$ s$^{-2}$, so $\mu/r$ is m$^2$ s$^{-2}$ (a speed squared and a specific energy); $h^2/\mu$ is a length.
  • Limits: a burn that brings $v_p'^2$ to $2\mu/r_p$ gives $\varepsilon'=0$ and an escape parabola ($r_a'\to\infty$); a retrograde burn to $v_p'^2<\mu/r_p$ would turn $\Pi$ into the new apogee.
  • Common mistakes in the exam: (1) confusing a constant areal velocity with a constant speed (or the reverse); (2) thinking that a burn raises the point where it is applied, instead of the opposite apse; (3) forgetting that a tangential burn keeps the burn point an apse.
