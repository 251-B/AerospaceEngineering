1. Angular Momentum Vector and Torque of Forces

In linear dynamics, the fundamental dynamical vector of a point particle $P$ is its linear momentum $\mathbf{p}_0 = m^P \mathbf{v}_0^P$. In rotational mechanics about an arbitrary reference point $A$, the central physical quantity is the moment of linear momentum, termed the angular momentum vector (Slide 3 & Notes Eq. 4.48):

$$ \mathbf{H}_{A0}^P = \mathbf{AP} \times \mathbf{p}_0^P = \left(\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^A\right) \times m^P \mathbf{v}_0^P $$

Vector Properties & Units

  • Orthogonality: $\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P$ is an axial pseudo-vector perpendicular to both the displacement vector $\mathbf{AP}$ and the velocity $\mathbf{v}_0^P$ ($\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P \cdot \mathbf{AP} = 0, \mathbf{H}_{A0}^P \cdot \mathbf{v}_0^P = 0$).
  • Base Point Sensitivity: Translating the origin from $A$ to another point $B$ introduces a lever arm correction: $$ \mathbf{H}_{B0}^P = \mathbf{H}_{A0}^P + \mathbf{BA} \times m^P \mathbf{v}_0^P $$
  • SI Units: $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s} = \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$ (Dimensions: $[M][L]^2[T]^{-1}$).

Torque (Moment of Force)

The torque of a force $\mathbf{F}$ applied to particle $P$ about point $A$ is defined as:

$$ \mathbf{M}_A = \mathbf{AP} \times \mathbf{F} = \left(\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^A\right) \times \mathbf{F} \quad [\text{N}\cdot\text{m}] $$
Action-Reaction Symmetry of Internal Torques (Slide 3)

For any two particles $P$ and $Q$ interacting through collinear mutual forces $\mathbf{F}^P = -\mathbf{F}^Q \parallel \mathbf{PQ}$, the internal torques about any origin $A$ cancel identically:

$$ \mathbf{M}_A^P + \mathbf{M}_A^Q = \mathbf{AP} \times \mathbf{F}^P + \mathbf{AQ} \times (-\mathbf{F}^P) = (\mathbf{AP} - \mathbf{AQ}) \times \mathbf{F}^P = \mathbf{QP} \times \mathbf{F}^P \equiv \mathbf{0} $$

2. Differential Evolution Equation with Moving Reference Points

Differentiating the angular momentum vector $\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P$ with respect to time in the inertial frame $S_0$ (Slide 4 & Notes Eq. 4.52):

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P}{dt}\Bigg|_0 = \left(\frac{d\mathbf{r}_0^P}{dt}\Bigg|_0 - \frac{d\mathbf{r}_0^A}{dt}\Bigg|_0\right) \times m^P \mathbf{v}_0^P + (\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^A) \times m^P \frac{d\mathbf{v}_0^P}{dt}\Bigg|_0 $$

Substituting $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_0^P = \mathbf{v}_0^P$, $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_0^A = \mathbf{v}_0^A$, and Newton's Second Law $m^P \dot{\mathbf{v}}_0^P = \mathbf{F}$:

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P}{dt}\Bigg|_0 = \mathbf{M}_A - \mathbf{v}_0^A \times m^P \mathbf{v}_0^P $$

Key Simplifications:

  1. Fixed Base Point in $S_0$ ($\mathbf{v}_0^A = \mathbf{0}$): $$ \frac{d\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P}{dt}\Bigg|_0 = \mathbf{M}_A $$ This is the exact rotational counterpart to Newton's Second Law ($\dot{\mathbf{p}}_0 = \mathbf{F}$).
  2. Origin Moving Parallel to Particle ($\mathbf{v}_0^A \parallel \mathbf{v}_0^P$): The cross product vanishes identically: $\frac{d\mathbf{H}_{A0}^P}{dt}\big|_0 = \mathbf{M}_A$.
  3. Origin Translating at Constant Velocity ($\mathbf{a}_0^A = \mathbf{0}$): The frame $S_A$ is an inertial reference frame, so the relative angular momentum satisfies $\frac{d\mathbf{H}_{AA}}{dt}\big|_A = \mathbf{M}_A$.

3. Central Force Dynamics & Planar Trajectories

A force field is central with respect to a fixed origin $O$ if its line of action passes through $O$ at every instant (Slide 7):

$$ \mathbf{F} = F(r)\,\mathbf{e}_r = F(r)\,\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r} $$
The Five Fundamental Properties of Central Forces (Slide 7 & Notes 4.8.1)
  1. Invariant Angular Momentum: $\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \equiv \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{H}_{O0}^P = \text{constant vector}$ (3 scalar conservation laws).
  2. Strictly Planar Motion: Because $\mathbf{r}(t) \cdot \mathbf{H}_O = 0$ and $\mathbf{v}(t) \cdot \mathbf{H}_O = 0$, both position and velocity remain in the plane orthogonal to $\mathbf{H}_O$ passing through $O$.
  3. Natural Polar Coordinate Formulation: Motion is fully described by $(r, \theta)$ in the orbital plane ($z = 0$).
  4. Specific Angular Momentum Conservation: $h = r^2\dot{\theta} = \text{const} \implies \dot{\theta} = h/r^2$. Angular speed increases as radius decreases.
  5. Origin Inaccessibility: The centrifugal barrier $T_\theta = \frac{1}{2}m h^2 / r^2 \to +\infty$ prevents any particle with $h \ne 0$ from reaching the center $O$.

4. Kepler's Problem & Barycentric Two-Body Reduction

Consider two masses $m^P$ (satellite/planet) and $m^S$ (Earth/Sun) interacting via Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation (Slides 8–10):

$$ \mathbf{F}_g = -\frac{G m^P m^S}{\|\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^S\|^2} \frac{\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^S}{\|\mathbf{r}_0^P - \mathbf{r}_0^S\|} $$

Because the barycenter $G$ has zero acceleration ($(m^S + m^P)\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_0^G = \mathbf{0}$), a reference frame $S_G$ attached to $G$ is inertial. Subtracting the equations of motion yields the one-body relative Kepler equation:

$$ \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}}{dt^2} = -\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf{r} = -\frac{\mu}{r^2}\mathbf{e}_r, \qquad \mu \equiv G(m^S + m^P) \approx G m^S $$

Kepler's Second Law (Areal Velocity)

Projecting into polar coordinates, the transverse equation yields $r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} = 0 \implies h = r^2\dot{\theta} = \text{const}$. The area swept per unit time is constant (Slide 12):

$$ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\theta} = \frac{h}{2} = \text{constant} $$

5. The Binet Equation & Conic Orbit Trajectories

Eliminating time $t$ in favor of polar angle $\theta$ via the reciprocal substitution $u(\theta) \equiv 1/r(\theta)$ and $\dot{\theta} = h u^2$ (Slide 13 & Notes 8.4):

$$ \dot{r} = -h\frac{du}{d\theta}, \qquad \ddot{r} = -h^2 u^2 \frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} $$

Substituting into the radial equation of motion $\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = -\mu/r^2$ yields the Binet Equation:

$$ \frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{\mu}{h^2} $$

The general solution describes a conic section with the gravitational focus at the origin (Kepler's First Law):

$$ r(\theta) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\theta} = \frac{h^2/\mu}{1 + e\cos\theta} $$

where $p = h^2/\mu$ is the semi-latus rectum and $e \ge 0$ is the eccentricity.

Eccentricity Semi-Major Axis $a$ Geometric Conic Specific Energy $\xi$ Orbital Regime
$e = 0$ $a = p > 0$ Circle $\xi < 0$ Bound
$0 < e < 1$ $a > 0$ Ellipse $\xi < 0$ Bound (Planetary / Satellite)
$e = 1$ $a \to \infty$ Parabola $\xi = 0$ Critical Escape Threshold
$e > 1$ $a < 0$ Hyperbola $\xi > 0$ Unbound (Interplanetary Flyby)

6. Kepler's 3rd Law & The Vis-Viva Equation

Kepler's Third Law (Harmonic Law)

Integrating areal velocity over one complete orbital period $\tau$ (Slide 15):

$$ \frac{h}{2}\tau = \pi a b = \pi a^2 \sqrt{1 - e^2} \implies \tau = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} \implies \tau^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{\mu} a^3 $$

The orbital period depends strictly on the semi-major axis $a$ and gravitational parameter $\mu$, completely independent of eccentricity $e$!

The Vis-Viva Energy Equation (Slide 16)

The mass-specific mechanical energy $\xi \equiv \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{\mu}{r}$ is constant. Evaluating at periapsis yields $\xi = -\frac{\mu}{2a}$, leading to the famous Vis-Viva equation:

$$ v^2 = \mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) $$

Fundamental Orbital Velocities

  • Circular Velocity ($r = a$): $v_c = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}$
  • Escape Velocity ($a \to \infty$): $v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\,v_c$
  • Periapsis Speed ($r_p = a(1-e)$): $v_p = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a}\frac{1+e}{1-e}}$
  • Apoapsis Speed ($r_a = a(1+e)$): $v_a = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a}\frac{1-e}{1+e}}$

7. Solved Benchmark Example: Linear Central Force under Gravity

Example: Problem 17 (Slide 6 Benchmark)

Decoupled Cartesian harmonic equations & 3D elliptical trajectory
Slide 6

Problem Statement: A force attracts a particle $M$ of mass $m$ to origin $O$ with $\mathbf{F} = -m k^2 \mathbf{r}$ under vertical gravity $\mathbf{g} = -g\mathbf{k}$. Initial conditions: $\mathbf{r}(0) = \frac{\sqrt{3}g}{k^2}\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{v}(0) = \frac{2g}{k}\mathbf{j}$. Determine the motion, path shape, and velocity $\mathbf{v}(t)$.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Equations of motion: $m\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -m k^2\mathbf{r} - mg\mathbf{k} \implies \ddot{x} + k^2 x = 0, \ddot{y} + k^2 y = 0, \ddot{z} + k^2 z = -g$.
  2. Integration: $$ x(t) = \frac{\sqrt{3}g}{k^2}\cos(kt), \qquad y(t) = \frac{2g}{k^2}\sin(kt), \qquad z(t) = \frac{g}{k^2}(\cos(kt) - 1) $$
  3. Planar confinement: $z(t) = \frac{x(t)}{\sqrt{3}} - \frac{g}{k^2} \implies x - \sqrt{3}z - \frac{\sqrt{3}g}{k^2} = 0$. The path lies in a plane inclined at $30^\circ$.
  4. Elliptic path: $\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{3}g/k^2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{2g/k^2}\right)^2 = 1$. The 3D trajectory is an ellipse centered at $(0, 0, -g/k^2)$.
  5. Constant speed: $\mathbf{v}(t) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}g}{k}\sin(kt)\mathbf{i} + \frac{2g}{k}\cos(kt)\mathbf{j} - \frac{g}{k}\sin(kt)\mathbf{k} \implies \|\mathbf{v}(t)\| = \frac{2g}{k} = \text{constant}$.
