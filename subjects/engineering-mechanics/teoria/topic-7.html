Central idea of the topic

The linear momentum, the angular momentum and the kinetic energy of a point particle are extended to a rigid body by integrating over its mass distribution, and the velocity field of the body turns those integrals into compact expressions built from the mass $m$, the centre of mass $G$, the angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega_{B0}$ and the tensor of inertia. Newton's second law, written for every particle of the body and summed, then gives the two vector equations that govern a free rigid body: the motion of $G$ under the resultant force $\mathbf F$, and the evolution of the angular momentum under the resultant moment $\mathbf M_A$. Written in a body-fixed principal basis, the second one becomes Euler's equations. The work and energy theorem closes the topic. The chapter follows chapters 11 and 12 of the course notes and Lectures 9 and 10.

The linear momentum of an element of mass $dm$ is the momentum of a point particle (Notes Eq. 4.1) with that mass; the momentum of the body is the integral over $\mathcal D$.

Linear momentum of a material system Law: momentum of each material element (Eq. 4.1) summed over the mass distribution Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.1; [Lecture 9, Slide 3]
$$\begin{aligned} d\mathbf p_0 &= \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(momentum of an element of mass dm, Notes Eq. 4.1)} \\ \mathbf p_0 &= \int_{\mathcal D} d\mathbf p_0 \quad &\text{(sum of the momenta of all the elements)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(substitution of the element momentum)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf p_0 = \int_{\mathcal D} d\mathbf p_0 = \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf v_0^P\,dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]\,[\mathrm{M}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-1}}]$, kg m/s

Meaning: the momentum of the body is the vector sum of the momenta of all its material elements.

The definition of the centre of mass (Notes Eq. 10.8) is differentiated in time. The mass of each material element does not change while the body moves, so the time derivative can be taken inside the integral over the material elements.

Momentum equals mass times the velocity of the centre of mass Law: definition of the centre of mass, differentiated in $S_0$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.2; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.3; [Lecture 9, Slide 3]
$$\begin{aligned} m\,\mathbf r_0^G &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 10.8 multiplied by m: Eq. 11.2)} \\ \left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(m\,\mathbf r_0^G\right)\right|_0 &= m\left.\frac{d\mathbf r_0^G}{dt}\right|_0 = m\,\mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(m is constant; definition of velocity)} \\ \left.\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_0^P\,dm\right|_0 &= \int_{\mathcal D} \left.\frac{d\mathbf r_0^P}{dt}\right|_0 dm \quad &\text{(the mass dm of each element is constant in time)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(definition of velocity of each element)} \\ &= \mathbf p_0 \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.1)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{m\,\mathbf v_0^G = \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf v_0^P\,dm = \mathbf p_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}]\,[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-1}}]$ on both sides

Meaning: the momentum of the body equals that of a point particle with the total mass $m$ located at $G$ and moving like $G$ (Notes Eq. 11.3).

2. Angular momentum of a rigid body

2.1 Definition and expansion with the velocity field

Angular momentum about a point A Law: angular momentum of each material element (Eq. 4.48) summed over the body Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.4; [Lecture 9, Slide 4]
$$\begin{aligned} d\mathbf H_{A,0} &= \mathbf r_A^P \times \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(angular momentum of the element about A, Notes Eq. 4.48)} \\ \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \int_{\mathcal D} d\mathbf H_{A,0} \quad &\text{(sum over all the elements)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(substitution)} \\ &\equiv \int_{\mathcal D} \overrightarrow{AP} \times \mathbf v_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(notation of Lecture 9, Slide 4)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{A,0} = \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \mathbf v_0^P\,dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]\,[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]\,[\mathrm{M}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$, kg m$^2$/s

Meaning: the arm is measured from $A$, but the velocity is the absolute velocity in $S_0$, not the velocity relative to $A$.

The point $A$ may or may not belong to the body. The notes write $A(B)$ for the point of the body that occupies the position of $A$ at the instant considered (Notes Sec. 9.1.4): both have the same position, but in general not the same velocity.

Velocity field written from the body point A(B) Law: velocity field of the rigid body between two body points Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.5; Notes.pdf, Eq. 9.3; [Lecture 9, Slide 4]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_0^{Q_2} &= \mathbf v_0^{Q_1} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \overrightarrow{Q_1 Q_2} \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 9.3 for two body points Q1, Q2)} \\ \mathbf v_0^{P} &= \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \overrightarrow{A(B)P} \quad &\text{(choice Q1 = A(B), Q2 = P)} \\ \overrightarrow{A(B)P} &= \mathbf r_0^P - \mathbf r_0^{A(B)} = \mathbf r_0^P - \mathbf r_0^{A} = \mathbf r_A^P \quad &\text{(A and A(B) have the same position, Notes Eq. 9.14)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf v_0^P = \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\,[\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]$, every term a velocity

Meaning: the velocity of any element is a translation with $A(B)$ plus a rotation about it.

First expansion of the angular momentum Law: velocity field substituted in the definition; centre of mass Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.6; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.7; [Lecture 9, Slide 4]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.5 in Eq. 11.4)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)}\,dm + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(distributive property of the cross product)} \\ &= \left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P\,dm\right) \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(the velocity of A(B) is the same for every element: Eq. 11.6)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P\,dm &= \int_{\mathcal D} \left(\mathbf r_0^P - \mathbf r_0^A\right) dm \quad &\text{(position of P relative to A)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_0^P\,dm - \mathbf r_0^A \int_{\mathcal D} dm \quad &\text{(the position of A does not depend on P)} \\ &= m\,\mathbf r_0^G - m\,\mathbf r_0^A \quad &\text{(Notes Eqs. 10.8 and 10.6)} \\ &= m\,\mathbf r_A^G \quad &\text{(position of G relative to A)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P\,dm\right) \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} = m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}]\,[\mathrm{L}]\,[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$, an angular momentum

Meaning: the translational part of the velocity field contributes like a point mass $m$ placed at $G$ moving with $\mathbf v_0^{A(B)}$.

Double vector product of the rotational term Law: expansion of the double vector product, Notes Eq. 1.37 Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.8; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.9; [Lecture 9, Slide 4]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf A \times (\mathbf B \times \mathbf C) &= (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf C)\,\mathbf B - (\mathbf A \cdot \mathbf B)\,\mathbf C \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 1.37, written as Eq. 11.8)} \\ \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) &= \left(\mathbf r_A^P \cdot \mathbf r_A^P\right)\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} - \left(\mathbf r_A^P \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\mathbf r_A^P \quad &\text{(A = r, B = omega, C = r)} \\ &= (r_A^P)^2\,\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} - \left(\mathbf r_A^P \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\mathbf r_A^P \quad &\text{(the dot product of a vector with itself is its squared modulus)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm = \int_{\mathcal D} \left[(r_A^P)^2\,\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} - \left(\mathbf r_A^P \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\mathbf r_A^P\right] dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L^2}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\,[\mathrm{M}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ in both terms

Meaning: the rotational part of the angular momentum has a component along $\boldsymbol\omega_{B0}$ and a component along each arm $\mathbf r_A^P$.

Rotational term written with the tensor of inertia Law: unit tensor, dyadic product (Eq. 1.36) and tensor of inertia (Eq. 10.35) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.10; Notes.pdf, Eq. 10.35; [Lecture 9, Slide 4]
$$\begin{aligned} (r_A^P)^2\,\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} &= (r_A^P)^2\,\overline{\overline{\mathcal U}} \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(the unit tensor maps every vector onto itself)} \\ \mathbf r_A^P\left(\mathbf r_A^P \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) &= \left(\mathbf r_A^P \otimes \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 1.36 with A = B = r, C = omega)} \\ \left[\left(\mathbf r_A^P \otimes \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right]_i &= \sum_{j} r_i\,r_j\,\omega_j = r_i \left(\sum_j r_j\,\omega_j\right) \quad &\text{(component check of footnote 18, ri taken out of the sum)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm &= \int_{\mathcal D} \left[(r_A^P)^2\,\overline{\overline{\mathcal U}} \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} - \left(\mathbf r_A^P \otimes \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right] dm \quad &\text{(both rows in Eq. 11.9)} \\ &= \left\{\int_{\mathcal D} \left[(r_A^P)^2\,\overline{\overline{\mathcal U}} - \mathbf r_A^P \otimes \mathbf r_A^P\right] dm\right\} \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(the angular velocity is the same for every element)} \\ &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 10.35 written about the point A)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: all the information about the mass distribution needed by the rotational part sits in the six independent components of the tensor of inertia about $A$.

Angular momentum about A, first form Law: sum of the translational and the rotational terms Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.11; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.12; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.13; [Lecture 9, Slide 5]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{A,0} &= m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Eqs. 11.6, 11.7 and 11.10)} \\ \mathbf H_{G,0} &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + m\,\mathbf r_G^G \times \mathbf v_0^{G} = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(case A = G: the arm from G to G is zero, Eq. 11.12)} \\ \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf 0 = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(case A fixed body point: its velocity is zero, Eq. 11.13)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{A,0} = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^{A(B)}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ in both terms

Meaning: the expression is short when $A$ is $G$ or a fixed point of the body; for any other point the extra term must be kept.

2.2 Second expansion about the centre of mass: change of pole

The frame $S_G\{G; B_0\}$ has its origin at $G$ and axes parallel to those of $S_0$: it translates with $G$ and does not rotate, so it is not body-fixed in general [Lecture 9, Slide 4]. The velocity of an element seen from $S_G$ is written $\mathbf v_G^P$.

Four integrals of the second expansion Law: position split through G, velocity field from G, first moment about G (Eq. 10.44) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.14; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.15; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.16; [Lecture 9, Slide 6]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf r_A^P &= \mathbf r_A^G + \mathbf r_G^P \quad &\text{(vector sum of the arms A to G and G to P)} \\ \mathbf v_0^P &= \mathbf v_0^G + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 9.3 from G, which is fixed in the body)} \\ \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \int_{\mathcal D} \left(\mathbf r_A^G + \mathbf r_G^P\right) \times \left(\mathbf v_0^G + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(both rows in Eq. 11.4)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G\,dm + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \mathbf v_0^G\,dm + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^G \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(distributive property, four terms)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G\,dm &= m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(both vectors constant over the body, Eq. 11.14)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \mathbf v_0^G\,dm &= \left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P\,dm\right) \times \mathbf v_0^G = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(first moment about G is zero, Notes Eq. 10.44: Eq. 11.15)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^G \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm &= \mathbf r_A^G \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P\,dm\right) = \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(same reason, Eq. 11.16)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{A,0} = m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G + \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ in both terms

Meaning: the cross terms vanish because the mass is balanced about $G$; only a translational and a purely rotational term survive.

The remaining integral is the angular momentum seen from S_G Law: composition of velocities between $S_0$ and the translating frame $S_G$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.17; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.18; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.19; [Lecture 9, Slide 7]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_G^P &= \mathbf v_0^P - \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(SG translates with G and does not rotate)} \\ &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P \quad &\text{(velocity field from G)} \\ \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_G^P\right) dm &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \mathbf v_G^P\,dm \equiv \mathbf H_{G,G} \quad &\text{(definition Eq. 11.4 in the frame SG: Eq. 11.17)} \\ \mathbf H_{G,G} &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \left(\mathbf v_0^P - \mathbf v_0^G\right) dm \quad &\text{(first row, Eq. 11.18)} \\ &= \int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P \times \mathbf v_0^P\,dm - \left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_G^P\,dm\right) \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(distributive property; G velocity out of the integral)} \\ &= \mathbf H_{G,0} + \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.4 with A = G, and Notes Eq. 10.44: Eq. 11.19)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{G,G} \equiv \mathbf H_{G,0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ on both sides

Meaning: about the centre of mass, the angular momentum is the same whether the velocities are measured in $S_0$ or in the translating frame $S_G$.

Change of pole from G to any point A Law: second expansion combined with the result about G Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.21; [Lecture 9, Slide 7]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{A,0} &= m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G + \mathbf H_{G,G} \quad &\text{(previous two boxes)} \\ &= \mathbf H_{G,0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(H about G is the same in SG and S0: Eq. 11.20)} \\ &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.12: Eq. 11.21)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{A,0} = \mathbf H_{G,0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$ in every term

Meaning: the angular momentum about $A$ is the angular momentum of the rotation about $G$ plus that of a point mass $m$ at $G$ moving with $\mathbf v_0^G$, seen from $A$.

Extension: change of pole between two arbitrary points

The notes give the change of pole from $G$ to $A$ only. Applying Eq. 11.20 twice gives the rule between any two points $A$ and $C$; it is the same rule as the change of reduction point of a system of forces (Notes Eq. 12.15), with the momentum $\mathbf p_0$ in the place of the force. This box is an extension.

Change of pole between A and C (extension) Law: Eq. 11.20 written about two points and subtracted Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.3
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{C,0} - \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \left(\mathbf H_{G,0} + m\,\mathbf r_C^G \times \mathbf v_0^G\right) - \left(\mathbf H_{G,0} + m\,\mathbf r_A^G \times \mathbf v_0^G\right) \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.20 about C and about A)} \\ &= m\left(\mathbf r_C^G - \mathbf r_A^G\right) \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(the rotational term cancels)} \\ &= \mathbf r_C^A \times m\,\mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(r from C to G minus r from A to G is r from C to A)} \\ &= \mathbf r_C^A \times \mathbf p_0 \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.3)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf H_{C,0} = \mathbf H_{A,0} + \overrightarrow{CA} \times \mathbf p_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L}]\,[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]$

Meaning: moving the pole only adds the moment of the momentum, placed at the old pole; if the body has zero momentum, the angular momentum is the same about every point.

3. Kinetic energy of a rigid body

Kinetic energy of a material system Law: kinetic energy of each element (Eq. 4.2) summed over the mass distribution Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.22; [Lecture 9, Slide 8]
$$\begin{aligned} dT_0 &= \tfrac{1}{2}\left(v_0^P\right)^2 dm \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 4.2 for an element of mass dm)} \\ T_0 &= \int_{\mathcal D} dT_0 \quad &\text{(sum over the elements)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left(v_0^P\right)^2 dm \quad &\text{(constant factor out of the integral)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{T_0 = \int_{\mathcal D} dT_0 = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left(v_0^P\right)^2 dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}]\,[\mathrm{M}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, J

Meaning: a positive scalar, zero only when every element is at rest in $S_0$.

3.1 Expansion with the velocity field from A(B)

Kinetic energy expanded from the body point A(B) Law: velocity field Eq. 11.5 and the square of a sum Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.23; Notes.pdf, Eq. 11.24; [Lecture 9, Slide 8]
$$\begin{aligned} T_0 &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left(\mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \left(\mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(Eq. 11.5 in Eq. 11.22)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left[\left(v_0^{A(B)}\right)^2 + 2\,\mathbf v_0^{A(B)} \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) + \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right)\right] dm \quad &\text{(square of a sum of two vectors)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^{A(B)}\right)^2 + \left[\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P\,dm\right)\right] \cdot \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm \quad &\text{(constant vectors out of the integrals: Eq. 11.23)} \\ \left[\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\int_{\mathcal D} \mathbf r_A^P\,dm\right)\right] \cdot \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} &= m\left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^G\right) \cdot \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} \quad &\text{(first moment about A, Eq. 11.7: Eq. 11.24)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{T_0 = \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^{A(B)}\right)^2 + m\left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^G\right) \cdot \mathbf v_0^{A(B)} + \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal D} \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^P\right) dm}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}]\,[\mathrm{L^2\,T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ in each of the three terms

Meaning: a translational term, a coupling term and a rotational term; the last one is reduced next.

4. Equations of motion of a material system

A material system is regarded as a collection of point particles $P_i$, $i$ from 1 to $N$ (for a continuous body the sums become integrals without changing the results). Two kinds of forces act on it (Notes Fig. 12.1):

$\mathbf f_{P_j}^{P_i}$internal force on $P_i$ caused by the particle $P_j$ of the same system

A system of $N$ free particles has $3N$ degrees of freedom and needs the $3N$ scalar equations of Newton's second law. A rigid body is an infinite collection of particles, but the rigidity constraint leaves only 6 degrees of freedom, so 6 independent scalar equations are enough [Lecture 10, Slide 3]. These are the two vector equations derived in this section.

5. General equations for a rigid body

5.1 Choice of the point A

The two convenient choices of the point of moments Law: Eq. 12.13 with the extra term made zero; Eqs. 11.12 and 11.13 Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.13; [Lecture 10, Slide 8]
$$\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 &= \mathbf M_A - m\,\mathbf v_0^A \times \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.13)} \\ m\,\mathbf v_0^G \times \mathbf v_0^G &= \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(choice 1: A = G, a vector crossed with itself)} \\ m\,\mathbf 0 \times \mathbf v_0^G &= \mathbf 0 \quad &\text{(choice 2: A a fixed point of the body)} \\ \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Eqs. 11.12 and 11.13 for both choices)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{m\,\mathbf a_0^G = \mathbf F, \qquad \left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\right|_0 = \mathbf M_A \quad (A \equiv G \text{ or } A \text{ fixed body point})}$$

Units: N on the left equation, N m on the right equation

Meaning: the translation depends only on the resultant force and the mass; the rotation only on the resultant moment and the tensor of inertia.

The centre of mass is the point used for a general motion. When the body has a fixed point, that point is preferable: the reaction that keeps it fixed is unknown, but it has no moment about the pivot, so it does not appear in the rotational equations (Notes Sec. 12.2). The translational and rotational equations are coupled in general; the six scalar equations are of second order, so 12 initial conditions are needed, for example the coordinates of $G$, three Euler angles and their first derivatives at the initial instant.

5.2 External torques

When two external forces are equal and opposite but have different lines of action, their resultant is zero and they produce a torque $\mathbf Q_k$, the same about every point. It is convenient to replace such pairs by their torques.

Resultant moment with external torques Law: moment of the external forces plus the free vectors of the torques Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.14; [Lecture 10, Slide 12]
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf M_A &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf r_A^{P_i} \times \mathbf F^{P_i} + \sum_{k=1}^{M} \mathbf Q_k \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.5 redefined with M external torques)} \\ \mathbf r_A^{P} \times \mathbf F^{P} + \mathbf r_A^{P'} \times \left(-\mathbf F^{P}\right) &= \left(\mathbf r_A^{P} - \mathbf r_A^{P'}\right) \times \mathbf F^{P} = \overrightarrow{P'P} \times \mathbf F^{P} \quad &\text{(a couple: the arm from A cancels, so it is a free vector)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf M_A = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf r_A^{P_i} \times \mathbf F^{P_i} + \sum_{k=1}^{M} \mathbf Q_k}$$

Units: N m in every term

Meaning: with this definition Eq. 12.13 stays unchanged; internal torques, if any, cancel in pairs and do not affect a rigid body.

5.3 Equivalence and reduction of systems of forces and moments

The external forces on a rigid body are sliding vectors and the torques are free vectors, so the dynamics depend only on the pair $\{\mathbf F; \mathbf M_A\}$, the reduction of the system at $A$. Two systems are equivalent when they have the same resultant force and the same resultant moment about one point [Lecture 10, Slide 9]. These ideas apply to rigid bodies only.

Meaning: on the central axis the moment is minimum and parallel to the force (analogous to the instantaneous axis of rotation and slip); the position of the foot is an extension of the notes, which state only that the line exists.

Meaning: the moment about $G$ may be computed at any convenient point $C$ and moved to $G$.

6. Rate of change of angular momentum and Euler's equations

6.1 Derivative of the angular momentum in the body frame

The tensor of inertia is not constant in S_0 Law: product rule for a tensor times a vector, Eqs. 11.12 and 11.13 Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.17; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.18; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.19
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf H_{A,0} &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(A is G or a fixed body point: Eq. 12.17)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 &= \left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\right|_0 \quad &\text{(left-hand side of Eq. 12.13: Eq. 12.18)} \\ &= \left.\frac{d\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \left.\frac{d\boldsymbol\omega_{B0}}{dt}\right|_0 \quad &\text{(product rule)} \\ &= \left.\frac{d\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} \quad &\text{(definition of angular acceleration, Notes Eq. 3.9)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)\right|_0 = \left.\frac{d\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} + \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, N m

Meaning: when the body rotates, its mass distribution seen from $S_0$ changes, so the components of the tensor in $B_0$ change in time.

Derivative computed in the body-fixed frame Law: Coriolis theorem (Eq. 3.8) between $S_0$ and the body frame $S_B\{A; B_B\}$ Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.20; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.21; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.22; [Lecture 10, Slide 13]
$$\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 &= \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_B + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf H_{A,0} \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 3.8 with the body frame: Eq. 12.20)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_B &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \left.\frac{d\boldsymbol\omega_{B0}}{dt}\right|_B \quad &\text{(the mass distribution is fixed in SB: constant tensor)} \\ &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} \quad &\text{(the derivative of omega is the same in SB and S0, Notes Eq. 3.9)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 &= \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.21)} \\ \left.\frac{d\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.19 compared with Eq. 12.21: Eq. 12.22)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 = \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right)}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ in both terms

Meaning: a body-fixed basis is the natural one: the tensor has constant components there, and the change of direction of the angular momentum appears as the gyroscopic term.

6.2 Euler's equations in principal axes

The basis $B_B$, with unit vectors $\mathbf e_1$, $\mathbf e_2$, $\mathbf e_3$, is now chosen along the principal axes of inertia at $A$ (Notes Sec. 10.7.1), with principal moments $I_1$, $I_2$, $I_3$. The components of the angular velocity and of the angular acceleration in this basis are written $\omega_i$ and $\alpha_i$.

Figure 5. In a clean rotation about a principal axis the angular momentum about $G$ is parallel to the angular velocity; off the principal axes it is not, and the Euler equations couple..
Euler's equations Law: Eq. 12.21 written in components in the principal basis (Eqs. 10.42 and 10.43) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.23; Notes.pdf, Eq. 10.42; [Lecture 10, Slide 14]
$$\begin{aligned} \left[\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A\right]_{BB} &= \operatorname{diag}\left(I_1, I_2, I_3\right) \quad &\text{(principal basis: diagonal matrix, Notes Eq. 10.42)} \\ \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} &= I_1\alpha_1\,\mathbf e_1 + I_2\alpha_2\,\mathbf e_2 + I_3\alpha_3\,\mathbf e_3 \quad &\text{(product of the diagonal matrix by the column of alpha)} \\ \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} &= I_1\omega_1\,\mathbf e_1 + I_2\omega_2\,\mathbf e_2 + I_3\omega_3\,\mathbf e_3 \quad &\text{(same product with omega)} \\ \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) &= \left(\omega_2 I_3\omega_3 - \omega_3 I_2\omega_2\right)\mathbf e_1 + \left(\omega_3 I_1\omega_1 - \omega_1 I_3\omega_3\right)\mathbf e_2 + \left(\omega_1 I_2\omega_2 - \omega_2 I_1\omega_1\right)\mathbf e_3 \quad &\text{(vector product in the right-handed basis BB, cofactors of the rows e, omega, I omega)} \\ &= \left(I_3 - I_2\right)\omega_2\omega_3\,\mathbf e_1 + \left(I_1 - I_3\right)\omega_3\omega_1\,\mathbf e_2 + \left(I_2 - I_1\right)\omega_1\omega_2\,\mathbf e_3 \quad &\text{(expansion along the first row)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \mathbf e_1 &= I_1\alpha_1 + \left(I_3 - I_2\right)\omega_2\omega_3 = I_1\alpha_1 - \left(I_2 - I_3\right)\omega_2\omega_3 \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.21 projected on e1)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \mathbf e_2 &= I_2\alpha_2 - \left(I_3 - I_1\right)\omega_3\omega_1 \quad &\text{(projected on e2)} \\ \left.\frac{d\mathbf H_{A,0}}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \mathbf e_3 &= I_3\alpha_3 - \left(I_1 - I_2\right)\omega_1\omega_2 \quad &\text{(projected on e3)} \end{aligned}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-2}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$ in every term

Meaning: these are Euler's equations in the principal axes of inertia; the cyclic order 1, 2, 3 of the indices reflects the right-handed basis.

Euler's equations with the applied moment Law: rotational equation (A is G or a fixed body point) projected on the principal basis Source: [Lecture 10, Slide 13]; [Lecture 10, Slide 14]; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.23
$$\begin{aligned} \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) &= \mathbf M_A \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.21 equal to the moment, Section 5.1)} \\ \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\alpha_{B0} &= \mathbf M_A - \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \left(\overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right) \quad &\text{(Euler's equation in vector form, Lecture 10 Slide 13)} \\ I_1\alpha_1 &= M_1 - \left(I_3 - I_2\right)\omega_2\omega_3 = M_1 + \left(I_2 - I_3\right)\omega_2\omega_3 \quad &\text{(component e1 of the previous box)} \end{aligned}$$

Meaning: $M_i$ and $\omega_i$ are components in the body-fixed principal basis; the products of angular velocities couple the three rotations.

The body-fixed basis is therefore chosen for overall convenience, and Eqs. 12.23 are used only when it is principal.

8. Work, power and conservation of mechanical energy

8.1 Power of a system of forces and torques

Power of the external forces and torques Law: power of each force at its point of application (Eq. 4.10) plus the power of each torque Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.26; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.27; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.28; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.29; [Lecture 10, Slide 11]; [Lecture 10, Slide 12]
$$\begin{aligned} \dot W_0 &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \mathbf v_0^{P_i} + \sum_{k=1}^{M} \mathbf Q_k \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(internal forces do no work in a rigid body: Eq. 12.26)} \\ \mathbf v_0^{P_i} &= \mathbf v_0^A + \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^{P_i} \quad &\text{(velocity field from the body point A, Eq. 12.27)} \\ \sum_{i} \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \mathbf v_0^{P_i} &= \sum_{i} \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \mathbf v_0^A + \sum_{i} \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^{P_i}\right) \quad &\text{(distributive property)} \\ \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \left(\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \times \mathbf r_A^{P_i}\right) &= \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \left(\mathbf r_A^{P_i} \times \mathbf F^{P_i}\right) \quad &\text{(cyclic property of the scalar triple product)} \\ \sum_{i} \mathbf F^{P_i} \cdot \mathbf v_0^{P_i} &= \left(\sum_i \mathbf F^{P_i}\right) \cdot \mathbf v_0^A + \left(\sum_i \mathbf r_A^{P_i} \times \mathbf F^{P_i}\right) \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(common factors out of the sums: Eq. 12.28)} \\ \dot W_0 &= \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^A + \left(\sum_i \mathbf r_A^{P_i} \times \mathbf F^{P_i} + \sum_k \mathbf Q_k\right) \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(torque terms added)} \\ &= \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^A + \mathbf M_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.14: Eq. 12.29)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\dot W_0 = \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^A + \mathbf M_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\,[\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}}]$ and $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}}]$, W

Meaning: the power of a load system equals the power of its reduction at any body point $A$, so two equivalent systems do the same work [Lecture 10, Slide 11].

Two remarks of [Lecture 10, Slide 17]: an external force perpendicular to the velocity of its point of application (of any point on its line of action) does no work, and an external torque perpendicular to $\boldsymbol\omega_{B0}$ does no work.

8.2 Work and energy theorem

Work and energy theorem for a rigid body Law: power as the rate of the kinetic energy (chain rule) integrated with Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.30; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.31; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.32; [Lecture 10, Slide 17]; [Lecture 10, Slide 18]
$$\begin{aligned} \dot W_0 &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} m^{P_i}\left.\frac{d\mathbf v_0^{P_i}}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \mathbf v_0^{P_i} \quad &\text{(second law for each particle, internal power zero)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2}m^{P_i}\,\frac{d\left(\mathbf v_0^{P_i} \cdot \mathbf v_0^{P_i}\right)}{dt} \quad &\text{(product rule: the derivative of v dot v is twice v dot its derivative)} \\ &= \frac{d}{dt}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2}m^{P_i}\left(v_0^{P_i}\right)^2\right] = \frac{dT_0}{dt} \quad &\text{(constant masses; definition of T)} \\ W_0(t_1, t_2) &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \dot W_0\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \frac{dT_0}{dt}\,dt \quad &\text{(work as time integral of the power)} \\ &= \Big[T_0\Big]_{t_1}^{t_2} = T_{0,2} - T_{0,1} \quad &\text{(antiderivative T, Barrow's rule: Eq. 12.30)} \\ T_0 &= \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_A \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(A fixed body point, Eq. 11.32: Eq. 12.31)} \\ T_0 &= \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(always, Eq. 11.31: Eq. 12.32)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{W_0(t_1, t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \dot W_0\,dt = T_{0,2} - T_{0,1}}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}}]\,[\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, J

Meaning: the work of all external forces and torques between two instants equals the change of the kinetic energy of the body.

Conservation of mechanical energy Law: work of conservative loads as minus the change of a potential (Eq. 4.14) Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.33; [Lecture 10, Slide 18]
$$\begin{aligned} W_0(t_1, t_2) &= V_{0,1} - V_{0,2} \quad &\text{(all loads that do work derive from a potential)} \\ T_{0,2} - T_{0,1} &= V_{0,1} - V_{0,2} \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.30)} \\ T_{0,2} + V_{0,2} &= T_{0,1} + V_{0,1} \quad &\text{(terms of each instant on one side)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{T_0 + V_0 = T_{0,2} + V_{0,2} = T_{0,1} + V_{0,1} = E_0}$$

Units: J in every term

Meaning: the mechanical energy is conserved when every force or torque that does work is conservative; non-conservative forces may act if they are always perpendicular to the velocity of their point, and non-conservative torques if they are always perpendicular to the angular velocity.

Potential energy of the weight of a rigid body Law: potential of the weight of each element (Eq. 4.14) summed over the body; definition of the centre of mass Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.34; Notes.pdf, Eq. 4.14; [Lecture 10, Slide 18]
$$\begin{aligned} d\mathbf F_g &= -g\,\mathbf k_0\,dm \quad &\text{(weight of an element, k0 vertical upwards)} \\ dV_0 &= -\int \left(-g\,\mathbf k_0\,dm\right) \cdot d\mathbf r_0^P = g\,dm \int dz_0^P = g\,z_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(Notes Eq. 4.14; reference at zero height)} \\ V_0 &= \int_{\mathcal D} g\,z_0^P\,dm = g\int_{\mathcal D} z_0^P\,dm \quad &\text{(sum over the elements, g uniform)} \\ &= g\,m\,z_0^G \quad &\text{(vertical component of Notes Eq. 10.8)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{V_0 = m\,g\,z_0^G}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M}]\,[\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]\,[\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, J

Meaning: the weight is conservative and its potential is that of the whole mass at the height of $G$; as the slides warn, such potentials are integrals over the body.

8.3 Translational and rotational work

Work of the resultant force and translational energy Law: Eq. 12.8 dotted with the velocity of G; chain rule; Barrow's rule Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.35; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.8
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^G &= m\,\mathbf a_0^G \cdot \mathbf v_0^G = m\left.\frac{d\mathbf v_0^G}{dt}\right|_0 \cdot \mathbf v_0^G \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.8)} \\ &= \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2\right] \quad &\text{(product rule on v dot v)} \\ \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^G\,dt &= \Big[\tfrac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2\Big]_{t_1}^{t_2} \quad &\text{(antiderivative, Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^G\,dt = \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2\Big|_2 - \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2\Big|_1}$$

Units: J on both sides

Meaning: the resultant force, applied at $G$, changes only the translational part of the kinetic energy (first term of Eq. 12.32).

Work of the moment about G and rotational energy Law: power reduced at G (Eq. 12.29), energy theorem (Eq. 12.30) and the previous box Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.36; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.29; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.32
$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dT_0}{dt} &= \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^G + \mathbf M_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.29 with A = G, and dT/dt equal to the power)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}m\left(v_0^G\right)^2\right] + \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right] &= \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf v_0^G + \mathbf M_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.32 differentiated)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\right] &= \mathbf M_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \quad &\text{(the translational terms are equal, previous box)} \\ \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf M_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\,dt &= \Big[\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\Big]_{t_1}^{t_2} \quad &\text{(antiderivative, Barrow's rule)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf M_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\,dt = \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\Big|_2 - \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_G \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0}\Big|_1}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]\,[\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\,[\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, J

Meaning: the moment about $G$ changes only the rotational kinetic energy (second term of Eq. 12.32): translation and rotation can be treated separately.

Reaction at the pin Law: Eq. 12.8 projected on the body basis B1 Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.8
$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf R + m g\,\mathbf i_0 &= m\,\mathbf a_0^G \quad &\text{(resultant force: pin reaction and weight)} \\ R_1 + m g\cos\theta &= -m\,\frac{L}{2}\,\dot\theta^2 \quad &\text{(component i1)} \\ R_2 - m g\sin\theta &= m\,\frac{L}{2}\,\ddot\theta = m\,\frac{L}{2}\left(-\frac{3g}{2L}\sin\theta\right) \quad &\text{(component j1, equation of motion substituted)} \\ R_2 &= m g\sin\theta - \frac{3}{4}m g\sin\theta = \frac{1}{4}m g\sin\theta \quad &\text{(solved for R2)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\mathbf R = -m\left(g\cos\theta + \frac{L}{2}\,\dot\theta^2\right)\mathbf i_1 + \frac{1}{4}m g\sin\theta\,\mathbf j_1}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]$ in each term, N

Meaning: at the lowest point the pin pulls up with the weight plus the centripetal force; the reaction never enters the moment equation about $O$.

Energy check of the compound pendulum Law: Eqs. 12.31, 12.34 and 12.33 Source: Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.31; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.34; Notes.pdf, Eq. 12.33
$$\begin{aligned} T_0 &= \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol\omega_{B0} \cdot \overline{\overline{\mathcal I}}_O \cdot \boldsymbol\omega_{B0} = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{3}m L^2\,\dot\theta^2 \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.31 about the fixed point O)} \\ V_0 &= m g\left(-\frac{L}{2}\cos\theta\right) \quad &\text{(Eq. 12.34: G is L/2 cos theta below O)} \\ \frac{d}{dt}\left(T_0 + V_0\right) &= \frac{1}{3}m L^2\,\dot\theta\,\ddot\theta + m g\,\frac{L}{2}\sin\theta\,\dot\theta \quad &\text{(chain rule on theta dot squared and on cos theta)} \\ &= \frac{1}{3}m L^2\,\dot\theta\left(\ddot\theta + \frac{3g}{2L}\sin\theta\right) = 0 \quad &\text{(equation of motion)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{1}{6}m L^2\,\dot\theta^2 - \frac{1}{2}m g L\cos\theta = E_0}$$

Units: $[\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]$, J, in both terms

Meaning: the pin reaction does no work (its point does not move) and the weight is conservative, so the energy integral is consistent with the equation of motion.
