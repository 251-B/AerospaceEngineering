# 📖 Concept: Intermolecular Forces and Secondary Bonds

> **Key idea in one sentence:** secondary (van der Waals) bonds are weak attractions between electric dipoles, which appear when the electron charge of an atom or molecule is distributed asymmetrically [Slide 30].

Full explanation, in slide order: [[Topic 1 - Bonding in Solids and Material Properties]].

## 1. Primary vs secondary [Slides 29-30]
- Primary bonds are strong: ionic 150-370, covalent 125-300, metallic 25-200 kcal/mol [Slide 29].
- Secondary bonds are weak and come from dipoles: **permanent** (do not change with time) or **fluctuating** (the electron cloud changes with time) [Slide 30].

## 2. Permanent dipole, 1-10 kcal/mol [Slides 31-33]
- Molecules with a constant dipole moment, made of atoms of different electronegativity joined by covalent bonds [Slide 31].
- CH₄: symmetrical → no dipole. CH₃Cl: asymmetrical → dipole [Slide 31].
- PVC: Cl (negative) and H (positive) on the chains give van der Waals bonds between chains, which make PVC stiffer; under a force they break and the chains slide [Slide 32].
- **Hydrogen bond** (7 kcal/mol in H₂O): dipole-dipole interaction between polar bonds containing H (O–H, N–H, F–H) [Slide 33].

> [!warning] Erratum in Slide 33
> The slide writes "positive oxygen pole and negative hydrogen pole". Oxygen, the more electronegative atom, is the negative pole and hydrogen the positive pole (Slides 4 and 20).

## 3. Fluctuating dipole, < 2 kcal/mol [Slide 34]
Forms in non-polar molecules: instantaneous fluctuations of the electron charge create fluctuating dipoles.

## 4. Example [Slide 35]
Boiling points rise with the period, but strong hydrogen bonding makes those of H₂O, HF and NH₃ much higher (the graph's axis is the boiling point, although its caption says melting points).
