# ✏️ Problem: T1 Question 1, Metallic vs Covalent Bonding

## 📄 Statement [Slide 39]
> Compare and contrast metallic and covalent primary bonds in terms of (a) the nature of the bond, (b) the valence of the atoms involved, and (c) the ductility of the materials bonded in these ways.

Theory: [[Topic 1 - Bonding in Solids and Material Properties]].

---

## 1. Setup
- Both are **primary (strong) bonds**: covalent 125-300 kcal/mol, metallic 25-200 kcal/mol.
- The question asks for three comparisons: the nature of the bond, the role of valence, and ductility.

## 2. Key ideas
| | Covalent | Metallic |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| How electrons are used | **Shared** between two atoms | Given up to an **electron sea** shared by all the atoms |
| Direction | **Highly directional**, no free electrons | **Non-directional** |
| Elements | Nonmetals, small electronegativity difference | Metals: cation cores + delocalized electrons |
| Valence | Outer s and p electrons shared to reach a noble-gas configuration; silicon (valence 4) must form 4 bonds | The valence electrons join the sea; more valence electrons → higher bonding energy (K +1: 89.6 kJ/mol; Ca +2: 177 kJ/mol) |
| Mechanical behaviour | Covalent solids are hard and incompressible; covalent lattices are very hard and brittle | Pure metals are much more malleable; metallic substances are ductile and workable |

## 3. Answer
**(a) Nature of the bond.** A covalent bond is a pair of electrons **shared between two specific atoms**, so it points in fixed directions (e.g. 109.5° in silicon). A metallic bond is the attraction between positive cores and a **sea of delocalized electrons** shared by all the atoms, so it has no direction.

**(b) Valence.** In covalent bonding the valence fixes **how many bonds** an atom must form: silicon, with valence 4, forms exactly four. In metallic bonding the valence fixes **how many electrons go to the sea**: the more valence electrons, the higher the bonding energy and the melting point (K → Ca).

**(c) Ductility.** Covalent materials are hard and brittle: their bonds have fixed directions. Metals are ductile, malleable and workable: their bonds are non-directional because the electron sea is shared by all the atoms.

## 4. Interpretation
The two differences, **directional vs non-directional** and **localized vs delocalized** electrons, explain the rest: the same pair of ideas gives conduction in metals (the electron sea moves under a voltage) and its absence in covalent solids (no free electrons, non-conductive lattices).
