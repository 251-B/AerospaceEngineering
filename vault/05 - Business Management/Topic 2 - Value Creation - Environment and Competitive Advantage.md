# 🎯 Topic 2: Value Creation — Environment and Competitive Advantage

> **Topic Objective:** Examine how modern enterprises define their objectives beyond simplistic profit maximization, integrating stakeholder dynamics and organizational survival into a unified framework of **Value Creation**. Understand strategic decision-making levels (corporate vs business strategy), mechanisms of corporate diversification and vertical integration, drivers of competitive advantage (Cost Leadership vs Differentiation), macro-environmental scanning through PESTEL analysis, industry structural analysis through Michael Porter's Five Competitive Forces, and internal process decomposition via Porter's Value Chain.

**Slide-deck outline [Slide 2]:** (1) Business objectives: (a) traditional concept, benefit maximization; (b) emergence of new objectives, the role of stakeholders; (2) Business strategies and types; (3) Generic and specific environment. The deck then continues with the strategic management process, competitive advantage, diversification and Porter's value chain.

### 1. Business Objectives and Value Creation

#### The Traditional Concept: Profit Maximization [Slide 3]
* As per the Classical Theory the business objective is **profit maximization**, and business objective = entrepreneur's objective.
* **Limitations listed on the slide:**
  1. The term profit is difficult to define and quantify.
  2. It does not take into account the risk associated with achieving profits.
  3. The concept of maximization.
  4. Excessive leading role of the entrepreneur: separation of ownership and control.

#### Management and Objectives [Slide 4]
* To avoid the conflict of interests the aim is to **align management and shareholder objectives**. The slide links this to incentive compensation and to three disciplining markets: the market of enterprises, the labour market and the markets of the factors of production.
* Management will try to maximize its own interests, always subject to achieving a **minimum level of profit** that satisfies shareholders.

#### The Role of Stakeholders [Slide 5, Slide 6]
* **Stakeholders on the slide [Slide 5]:** management, shareholders, society (CSR), banks and other financial agents, customers, suppliers, the State, employees / labour unions.
* **Organizational theory [Slide 6]:** the business objective is the outcome of a **negotiation process** between the different stakeholders, limited by the **survival goal**. Not all groups have the same decision-making power; the business objective is the one that satisfies the group with higher bargaining power, taking into account the objectives of the other groups and the survival of the enterprise. The unifying statement on the slide: *maximize the value of the enterprise: create value*.
  * *(standard reference, not on the slides)* The behavioural theory of the firm is usually attributed to Cyert and March (1963).

#### A Unified Vision: Survival, Profit and Growth [Slide 7]
The slide links products/markets (current and new) to sales and profits; profits are split into **dividends** and **retained profits** (for future investments); equity attributable to investors and total equity feed the **capital-market valuation** of the company.
$$\text{Products/Markets} \longrightarrow \text{Sales} \longrightarrow \text{Profits} \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{Dividends} \\ \text{Retained profits} \longrightarrow \text{Investments} \end{cases} \longrightarrow \text{Capital-market valuation}$$

### 2. Business Strategies and Types

#### Strategic Management Process [Slide 16]
$$\text{Mission Statement} \longrightarrow \text{Performance Objectives} \longrightarrow \text{Develop Strategies} \longrightarrow \text{Strategy Implementation} \longrightarrow \text{Evaluation of Performance}$$

#### Decision Hierarchy [Slide 8, Slide 9]
To achieve the business objective the firm defines a **strategy**, which entails **strategic decisions**, **tactic decisions** and **operative decisions** [Slide 8]. The deck quotes the Wordsmyth Dictionary: strategy is "a plan, method or series of actions designed to achieve a specific goal or effect". The slides give no further definition of the three decision levels.
* *(standard reference)* Strategic decisions are long-term and commit substantial resources; tactical decisions are medium-term implementations; operative decisions are short-term, day-to-day.

#### Types of Strategies [Slide 9, Slide 17, Slide 18]
* **Corporate strategy** (which businesses should we be in? industry attractiveness) is concerned with *where* a firm competes, i.e. the **scope** of its activities: product scope (diversification), geographical scope (internationalization), vertical scope (vertical integration) [Slide 9, Slide 18].
* **Competitive or business strategy** (how should we compete? competitive advantage) is concerned with *how* a firm competes within a particular market: **cost leadership** or **differentiation** [Slide 9, Slide 17, Slide 18].
* Together they answer *how do we make money?*, i.e. a rate of profit above the competitive level [Slide 17].
* **Scope examples [Slide 19]** (after Grant, 2002): *vertical* (steel, steel sheet and steel cans by three companies or one); *geographical* (can makers in the US, UK and Italy as three companies or one multinational); *product* (metal cans, plastic packaging and domestic appliances separate or merged).

#### What makes a successful strategy [Slide 10]
Long-term, simple and agreed objectives; deep understanding of the competitive environment; objective appraisal of resources; all supported by **effective implementation**.

### 3. Generic and Specific Environment

#### Generic environment [Slide 11]
| Factor | Items on the slide |
| :--- | :--- |
| Economic | macroeconomic variables; business-cycle period; type of economic policy; country resources |
| Political-legal | monetary and fiscal policies; labour-market regulation; industry regulation |
| Socio-cultural | value system; level of education; class structure; income distribution; population distribution; etc. |
| Technological | basic technologies; key technologies; incipient technologies |

* *(standard reference, not on the slides)* The PESTEL framework (Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological, Environmental, Legal) is a common textbook expansion of these four factors and is used in the practical session; the slides name only the four factors above, and a wider view of the environment (natural environment, demographic structure, social structure, government and politics) appears on [Slide 13].

#### Specific environment [Slide 12]
Relationship with suppliers; relationship with customers; substitutes; rivalry within the industry (concentration, industry rate of growth, product differentiation); barriers to entry; barriers to exit.

#### From environmental analysis to industry analysis [Slide 13]
The **industry environment** (suppliers, competitors, customers) sits inside the national/international economy, technology, government and politics, the natural environment, demographic structure and social structure.

#### Porter's Five Competitive Forces (Porter, 1982, as cited on the slide) [Slide 14, Slide 15]
The five forces are: rivalry among existing firms (industry competitors), bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, threat of new entrants [Slide 14]. The determinants listed on [Slide 15]:

| Force | Determinants on the slide |
| :--- | :--- |
| Industry rivalry | concentration; diversity of competitors; product differentiation; excess capacity and exit barriers; cost conditions |
| Supplier power | suppliers' price sensitivity; relative bargaining power |
| Buyer power | buyers' price sensitivity; relative bargaining power |
| Threat of entry | capital requirements; economies of scale; absolute cost advantage; product differentiation; access to distribution channels; legal/regulatory barriers |
| Substitute competition | buyers' propensity to substitute; relative prices and performance of substitutes |

* *(standard reference)* Extended conditions of strong supplier/buyer power (concentration, switching costs, threat of integration) are textbook elaborations of "relative bargaining power" and are covered in [[05 - Business Management/Concept - External Environment Analysis (PESTEL and Porters 5 Forces)|the concept note]].

### 4. Competitive Strategy: Sources of Competitive Advantage [Slide 20]
Competitive advantage has two sources: **cost advantage** (similar product at lower cost) and **differentiation advantage** (price premium from a unique product).

#### Drivers of cost advantage [Slide 21, Slide 22]
| Driver | Slide wording |
| :--- | :--- |
| Economies of learning | improved organizational routines |
| Economies of scale | specialization and division of labour |
| Production techniques | process innovation; reengineering business processes |
| Product design | standardizing designs and components; design for manufacture |
| Input costs | location advantages; ownership of low-cost inputs; non-union labour; bargaining power |
| Capacity utilization | ratio of fixed-to-variable costs; speed of capacity adjustment |
| Residual efficiency | organizational slack; motivation and culture; managerial efficiency |

#### Differentiation [Slide 23, Slide 24]
* **Definition [Slide 23]:** how a firm distinguishes its offerings from those of competitors; "providing something unique that is valuable to the buyer beyond simply offering a low price" (M. Porter). *The key is to create value for the customer.*
* **Tangible differentiation [Slide 24]:** observable product characteristics (size, colour, materials, etc.), performance, packaging, complementary services.
* **Intangible differentiation [Slide 24]:** unobservable and subjective characteristics that appeal to the customer's image, status, identity and desire for exclusivity.
* **Total customer responsiveness [Slide 24]:** differentiation is not just about the product; it embraces the whole relationship between supplier and customer.

### 5. Corporate Strategy: Diversification and Vertical Integration

#### Diversification [Slide 25, Slide 26]
* Diversification decisions involve two issues: how attractive is the sector to be entered, and can the firm achieve a competitive advantage there [Slide 25].
* **Reasons [Slide 25]:** *growth* (escape from declining industries, e.g. tobacco, oil, newspapers); *risk* (diversification reduces earnings volatility); *profit* (bringing together different businesses may increase profitability).
* **Types [Slide 26]:** *related* diversification, when a business expands its existing product lines or markets; *unrelated* diversification, when it adds new or unrelated product lines or markets.
* **Forms [Slide 26]:** vertical integration (along your value chain); horizontal diversification (moving into a new industry); geographical diversification (opening new markets).

#### Vertical integration [Slide 27]
Forms: **forward** and **backward** vertical integration.
* *Advantages:* inputs/outputs control; reduction of transaction costs; independency; better competitive position; reduced information asymmetries.
* *Disadvantages:* increased risk (outsourcing); decreased flexibility; loss of specialization advantages; increased administrative costs.
* *(standard reference)* Backward = upstream (suppliers), forward = downstream (distribution/customers).

### 6. Internal Process Analysis: Porter's Value Chain [Slide 28-31]
* **Idea [Slide 28]:** the term value chain was used by Michael Porter (1985). Organizations are more than a random compilation of resources: they arrange resources into systems and activities that yield products customers are willing to pay for; managing these activities and their linkages gives competitive advantage.
* **Structure [Slide 29]:** support activities (Firm Infrastructure, Human Resources, Technology Development, Procurement) over primary activities (Inbound Logistics, Operations, Outbound Logistics, Marketing and Sales, Service), leading to the **Margin**.
* **Primary activities [Slide 30]:**
  1. *Inbound logistics:* relationships with suppliers; all activities to receive, store and disseminate inputs.
  2. *Operations:* all activities to transform inputs into outputs.
  3. *Outbound logistics:* all activities to collect, store and distribute the output.
  4. *Marketing and sales:* inform buyers about products and services, induce them to purchase and facilitate the purchase.
  5. *Service:* keep the product or service working effectively for the buyer after it is sold and delivered.
* **Secondary activities [Slide 31]:** *procurement* (acquisition of inputs or resources); *human resource management* (recruiting, hiring, training, developing, compensating and, if necessary, dismissing or laying off personnel); *technological development* (equipment, hardware, software, procedures and technical knowledge used to transform inputs into outputs); *infrastructure* (ties the parts together: accounting, legal, finance, planning, public affairs, government relations, quality assurance and general management).
* Reference on the slide: Porter, M. E., *Competitive Advantage* (1985), Ch. 1, pp. 11-15.
* *(standard reference)* Margin is the difference between the value customers pay and the total cost of performing the activities; the slide shows the margin only graphically.
