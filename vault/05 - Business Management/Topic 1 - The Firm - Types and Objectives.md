# 🏢 Topic 1: The Firm — Types and Objectives

> **Topic Objective:** Understand the economic nature of business enterprise as a coordinating mechanism that converts scarce factors of production (inputs) into goods and services (outputs) to satisfy human needs. Explore business functional areas, Henri Fayol's administrative management process, the evolutionary economic role of the entrepreneur, legal forms of ownership (sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, cooperative), and the separation of ownership and control giving rise to the Agency Problem and corporate governance mechanisms.

## 📑 Core Theoretical Modules

> [!info] Citation convention
> **[Slide N]** refers to the official deck *Topic 1_The Firm_Types Objectives.pdf* (29 slides). Items tagged *(standard reference)* are textbook material that is **not** on the slides; they are kept only as clearly marked context.

**Slide-deck outline [Slide 2]:** (1) Concept and nature of business, (2) Business functional areas, (3) Management process, (4) Role of engineers in business management, (5) The enterprise and the entrepreneur, (6) Types of companies and legal forms.

### 1. Concept and Nature of Business
* **Definition [Slide 3]:** Business is an economic activity that transforms a set of inputs (factors of production) into outputs (goods and services aimed to satisfy customers' needs and wants). Worked illustration in the deck: the production process of bread [Slide 4].
* **Inputs [Slide 3]:** Land (natural resources), Labour, Capital, Entrepreneurship. **Outputs:** Goods and Services.
* **Circular flow of income [Slide 5]:** a simple economic model describing the reciprocal circulation of income between producers and consumers. The interaction of the goods-and-services market and the factor market determines a double flow (monetary and real) through which the economy decides what to produce, how and for whom.
  * **Two economic agents [Slide 6]:** Households and Firms.
  * **Households [Slide 7]:** (i) owners of the production factors (land, labour, capital, technology and know-how); (ii) fundamental units of consumption.
  * **Firms [Slide 8, Slide 10]:** use the factors supplied by households to produce the goods and services households demand; they collect the money spent by households in the market of goods and services.
  * **Factor market [Slide 9]:** households deliver inputs and receive incomes (wages, rents, dividends); exchange takes place in the labour market and the stock-exchange market.
  * **Real flows vs financial flows [Slide 11]:** real flows are goods, services and factors; financial flows are their monetary compensation.
* **Nature of business [Slide 12]:** Economics is the study of how people and a society choose to use scarce resources (factors of production) to satisfy people's unlimited wants. An enterprise arises because there is a dissatisfied need; specialization (division of labour) requires **coordination**, which can be provided by the **market** or by the **enterprise**.
  * *(standard reference, not on the slides)* The choice between market and enterprise is usually explained by transaction costs (Coase, 1937): a firm emerges when internal coordination is cheaper than contracting on the market.

### 2. Business Functional Areas [Slide 13]
The deck splits business functions into three groups and draws the cycle *Inputs - Real activity - Outputs* with the opposite financial activity (payment of inputs' acquisition, fundraising, revenues from sales, return of capital and interests).
1. **Real functions:** supply (acquisition of inputs); production; marketing; other support functions (Human Resources, Information Systems, ...).
2. **Financial functions:** financing; investing.
3. **Management functions:** planning; control; organization.

*(standard reference)* Investing is efficient when the expected return exceeds the cost of capital; this criterion is not stated on the Topic 1 slides.

---

### 3. The Management Process [Slide 14]
Management process: **planning, organizing, leading and controlling** (Henri Fayol, 1841-1925, as cited on the slide).
$$\text{Planning} \longrightarrow \text{Organizing} \longrightarrow \text{Leading} \longrightarrow \text{Controlling}$$

*(descriptions paraphrase the four-stage cycle diagram on [Slide 14])*
1. **Planning:** defining goals and strategies and developing plans to coordinate activities.
2. **Organizing:** deciding which tasks are done, by whom, how they are grouped and who reports to whom.
3. **Leading:** motivating, communicating and resolving conflicts.
4. **Controlling:** comparing actual performance with plans and correcting deviations.

> [!note] Slide inconsistency
> [Slide 13] lists the management functions as planning, control and organization, whereas [Slide 14] gives the four-stage Fayol cycle (planning, organizing, leading, controlling). Both are reproduced as stated.

### 5. The Enterprise and the Entrepreneur
* **Definition [Slide 16]:** an entrepreneur is a risk taker who starts and operates a business in the hope of making a profit. Entrepreneurship is the process of bringing land, labour and capital together and taking the risk involved in producing a good or service in the hope of making a profit. The slide stresses that there is no conclusive agreement about who the entrepreneur is, especially in big companies where owners, management directors and moneylenders share the characteristics attributed to a single person.
* **Four views of the entrepreneur [Slide 17]:**
  * "Coordinator" (no author given on the slide);
  * "Risk Taker" (Knight, 1921);
  * "Innovator" (Schumpeter, 1950);
  * "On alert" (Kirzner, 1973).
* **Entrepreneur's functions [Slide 17]:** coordinates and controls the productive process; ensures the payment of the factors of production; takes risky decisions; innovates; detects new business opportunities.
* **Internal organization = teamwork [Slide 18]:** team production requires a **controller**; the slide asks what the incentive for performing this task is, and answers that the entrepreneur holds four rights: (1) to receive the residual income or surplus; (2) to control and reassign the other factors of production; (3) to be the central party in all the contracts signed with the inputs (raw materials, capital, human resources); (4) to sell the mentioned rights.
  * *(standard reference)* This four-right bundle is the Alchian-Demsetz (1972) team-production argument; the slide does not name the authors.

### 6. Types of Enterprises and Legal Forms

#### Classification criteria [Slide 19]
* **Economic criteria:** scope of the business (one market or multi-market; national or multinational); sector (primary, secondary, tertiary); size (big, medium and small).
* **Legal criteria:** ownership of the factors of production (private companies; state-owned companies).
* **Main forms of business ownership [Slide 19, Slide 20]:** sole proprietorship, partnership (general partnership; limited liability partnership, LLP), corporation, and others (cooperative associations, joint ventures, mutual companies, etc.).

#### Sole Proprietorship [Slide 21]
A business owned by one person, who assumes all the risk, takes decisions and looks for business opportunities; he or she owns all the rights.
* *Advantages:* simple to start; the proprietor owns all profits; personal satisfaction (being your own boss); sole decision maker; no tax on the business as distinct from the owner; easy to dissolve.
* *Disadvantages:* unlimited financial liability; hard to raise funds for expansion; often no one to share management responsibilities (one person's talents and skills); impermanence.

#### Partnership [Slide 22, Slide 23]
An association of two or more persons carrying on as co-owners of a business for profit, formed by a **partnership agreement**: a written contract identifying the business and stating the partners' rights and duties (name, location, type of business, sharing of profits, dissolving the partnership, etc.) [Slide 22].
* *Advantages [Slide 23]:* few restrictions on starting; pooling of funds, talents and borrowing power; more chance to specialize than the sole proprietorship; no tax on the business as distinct from the owners.
* *Disadvantages [Slide 23]:* unlimited and joint financial liability; potential for personal disagreements; relative impermanence; frozen investment.

#### Corporation [Slide 24]
A legally chartered organization that is a separate legal entity apart from its owners and has most of the rights of a person.
* *Advantages:* separate legal entity (shareholders are the owners); limited financial liability of owners; permanence (it can exist forever); easy transfer of ownership (shares); greater financial capability.
* *Disadvantages:* special and double taxation; complex and costly to form and dissolve; government regulation and public disclosure requirements.

#### Cooperative [Slide 27, Slide 28]
An incorporated organization whose user-members (owners) get back any revenue left after expenses are paid [Slide 27].
* *Principles [Slide 27]:* (1) membership is open; (2) owned and democratically controlled by its members; (3) economic benefits distributed in proportion to each member's level of participation; (4) based on the values of self-help, self-responsibility, democracy and equality; (5) members believe in the ethical values of honesty, openness, social responsibility and caring for others.
* *Examples [Slide 28]:* employee credit unions (savings deposits and loans between members); agricultural co-ops (help member farmers market their products); buying co-ops (lower purchase prices); consumer co-ops (customer-owned retail facilities, buying pool for quantity discounts).

#### Other forms [Slide 29]
* **Limited partnership:** a business in which one or more, but not all, of the partners are liable for the firm's debts only to the extent of their financial investment.
* **Joint venture:** a special type of partnership set up by individuals or firms to accomplish a specific task or project; very important and usual in international business.

### 7. Separation of Ownership and Control and the Agency Problem [Slide 25, Slide 26]
* **Problem description [Slide 25]:** companies are managed by managers, who are employees (professionals) that take decisions and assume risks. Managers may decide in their own interest, disregarding the effect on shareholders; shareholders and managers have different access to information and different objectives; shareholders lack the information to assess managers' performance and the incentives to do so or to improve management. The result is a conflict of interest between ownership and control: the **agency problem**. In large corporations the **Board of Directors** guides the business.
* **Solution [Slide 26]:** shareholders must be able to control managers so that they do not pursue their own benefit. *Mechanisms of control:* selecting the Board of Directors; contracts signed with managers (goal-linked); requesting audits; additional mechanisms: indicators. The slide adds that shareholders owning many shares are increasingly the ones taking decisions.
* *(standard reference, not on the slides)* The principal-agent vocabulary (shareholders as principals, managers as agents, information asymmetry) and the market for corporate control (hostile takeover threat) are textbook extensions of this slide.
* **CNMV recommendations** (practical session 1.2, not the Topic 1 deck): see [[05 - Business Management/Practica 1.2 - Corporate Governance and CNMV Good Governance Code|Practical Session 1.2]]; Recommendation 13 sets the board size between five and fifteen members.
