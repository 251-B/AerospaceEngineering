## B05.1 The most important source of innovation

- id: B05-Q1
- concept_id: u7-sources-linkages
- format: identify
- asks: definition

According to the course, which is the most important source of innovation:

a) The lone individual inventor working on a problem of his or her own
b) The linkages between individuals, firms, universities, government-funded research and private non-profit organizations
c) The central corporate research laboratories of large firms
d) Government laboratories, science parks and incubators

### Answer

b

### Explanation

Topic 7, Slide 4: the most important source of innovation is the linkages between the players; networks of innovators that leverage knowledge and other resources from multiple sources are one of the most powerful agents of technological advance.

- a) The individual inventor is one of the sources listed on Slide 3, but the course does not rank it first; it singles out the linkages between all the sources.
- c) Central corporate research ranks first among firms' sources of research work on Slide 6, which is a ranking inside firms, not the most important source of innovation overall.
- d) Government laboratories, science parks and incubators are one source among several (Slide 8), not the linkages that the course names as the most important source.

## B05.3 Knowledge that crosses boundaries

- id: B05-Q3
- concept_id: u7-technological-spillovers
- format: complete-sentence
- asks: definition

A positive externality of R&D that results from the spread of knowledge across organizational or regional boundaries is called ...

a) a technological spillover
b) a technology cluster
c) absorptive capacity
d) a network externality

### Answer

a

### Explanation

Topic 7, Slide 9 defines technological spillovers as a positive externality of R&D resulting from the spread of knowledge across organizational or regional boundaries.

- b) A technology cluster is a regional group of firms connected to a common technology, a set of firms, not the externality that flows between them.
- c) Absorptive capacity is the ability of an organization to recognize, assimilate and use new knowledge (Slide 18), a capability of one firm, not a spread of knowledge.
- d) A network externality is the rise in the value of a good to a user as the number of its users grows (Slide 19); it is not caused by spread R&D knowledge.

## B05.4 Robotic welding on the seat line

- id: B05-Q4
- concept_id: u7-product-vs-process
- format: identify
- asks: application

An aircraft-seat manufacturer installs robotic welding on its assembly line. The defect rate of the seat frames halves and the number of seats produced per shift rises by 20%, while the seats delivered to airlines are identical to the previous ones. Which type of innovation is this:

a) A product innovation, because the seats are the firm's output
b) An architectural innovation, because the assembly line is a system
c) A process innovation
d) A dominant design of the seat industry

### Answer

c

### Explanation

Process innovations change the way an organization conducts its business, such as the techniques of producing goods, and are often aimed at improving the effectiveness or efficiency of production by reducing defect rates or increasing the quantity produced in a given time, exactly what the robotic welding does (Slide 11).

- a) A product innovation is embodied in the outputs; here the seats delivered are unchanged, so the novelty is not in the output.
- b) Architectural innovation refers to a change in the overall design of a system and in how its components interact; nothing in the case changes the design of the seat or its configuration.
- d) A dominant design is a product or process architecture that holds usually 50% or more of a market, not a single firm's improvement of its own line.

## B05.5 Competence-enhancing or destroying

- id: B05-Q5
- concept_id: u7-competence-perspective
- format: false-statement
- asks: comparison

Which one of the following statements about competence-enhancing and competence-destroying innovations is FALSE:

a) Whether an innovation is competence-enhancing or competence-destroying depends on whose perspective is taken
b) A radical innovation is always competence-destroying for every firm of the industry
c) An innovation is competence-enhancing for a firm if it builds on that firm's existing knowledge base
d) An innovation is competence-destroying for a firm if it renders that firm's competencies obsolete

### Answer

b

### Explanation

The statement is false because the course classifies competence effects firm by firm: the same innovation can enhance the competences of one firm and destroy those of another (Slide 12). Being radical (very new and different from prior solutions) is a different pair of the classification and does not fix the effect on every firm.

- a) True: Slide 12 states that the classification depends on whose perspective is being taken.
- c) True: this is the course's definition of competence-enhancing from the perspective of a particular firm.
- d) True: this is the course's definition of competence-destroying, which also covers an innovation that does not build on the firm's competences.

## B05.6 From one rotor to four

- id: B05-Q6
- concept_id: u7-architectural-vs-component
- format: identify
- asks: classification

A drone maker moves from a single-rotor helicopter layout to a quadcopter. It keeps the same type of electric motors, batteries and cameras, but rearranges them and changes how they work together: the drone is now steered by varying the speed of four rotors instead of tilting the blades of one rotor. On the architectural vs component pair, this is ...

a) an architectural innovation, because it changes the overall design of the system and the way its components interact
b) a component innovation, because it changes one or more components without affecting the overall configuration
c) a component innovation, because the motors, batteries and cameras are of the same type as before
d) neither, because no new component has been invented

### Answer

a

### Explanation

Slide 13: an architectural innovation changes the overall design of a system or the way its components interact with each other. The layout and the control of the drone change, even though the components are of the same type.

- b) The definition is the right one for a component innovation, but the overall configuration of the drone does change, so it does not apply here.
- c) Reusing the same type of components is compatible with an architectural innovation; what matters is the change in configuration and interaction.
- d) An architectural innovation needs no new component: rearranging existing components into a new design is precisely what it covers.

## B05.7 Doubling effort on a mature technology

- id: B05-Q7
- concept_id: u7-s-curve-limit
- format: what-happens
- asks: effect

The technology of a firm is already in the flat upper part of its S-curve of performance. If the firm doubles the effort it invests in that technology, what happens to performance:

a) It improves faster than ever, because the steepest part of the curve lies at the top
b) It doubles, because performance is proportional to effort
c) It falls, because the curve turns down after its maximum
d) It improves only a little, because the technology is approaching its limit

### Answer

d

### Explanation

On the S-curve of technology performance (Slide 14), performance improves slowly, then quickly, and then flattens as it approaches the limit of the technology; near that limit additional effort brings small gains.

- a) The steepest part of the S-curve is in the middle, not at the top, where the curve is flat.
- b) Performance is not proportional to effort: the curve is S-shaped, not a straight line through the origin.
- c) The S-curve does not turn down: it levels off at the limit of the technology drawn as a horizontal dotted line.

## B05.8 Arrival of a discontinuous technology

- id: B05-Q8
- concept_id: u7-discontinuous-technology
- format: true-statement
- asks: comparison

Which one of the following statements about the introduction of a discontinuous technology is TRUE:

a) It always starts with a higher performance than the technology it replaces
b) It improves the components of the existing technology on the same knowledge base
c) It may start below the existing technology on its S-curve and later overtake it
d) It is the moment at which the diffusion of the existing technology reaches the laggards

### Answer

c

### Explanation

A discontinuous technology fulfils a similar market need by building on an entirely new knowledge base; the technology S-curves of Slide 15 show that the second technology may start below the first one and later cross it.

- a) Slide 15 (b) draws the second technology below the first at the beginning, so "always higher" contradicts the course figure.
- b) Building on the same knowledge base describes an improvement of the existing technology, the opposite of an entirely new knowledge base.
- d) The laggards are the last adopter category of the diffusion curve (Slide 16), a pattern of adoption over time, not the definition of a discontinuous technology.

## B05.10 Why a design wins

- id: B05-Q10
- concept_id: u7-dominant-design-selection
- format: cause-effect
- asks: cause

Which pair of forces does the course give as the reasons why an industry selects a dominant design:

a) Official enforcement by a standards body and patent protection
b) Learning effects and network externalities
c) Technological spillovers and technology clusters
d) Product innovations and process innovations

### Answer

b

### Explanation

Slides 18-19: learning effects (the more a technology is used, the more it is developed and the more efficient it becomes) and network externalities (the value to a user grows with the number of users, through the installed base and complementary goods) push the market towards a single design, which can produce winner-take-all markets.

- a) A dominant design is a de facto standard: it need not be officially enforced (Slide 17), and patents are not among the reasons the course gives.
- c) Spillovers and clusters describe how innovation arises in collaborative networks (Slide 9), not why one design comes to dominate a market.
- d) Product and process are a pair of types of innovation (Slide 11); they classify innovations and do not explain the selection of a design.
