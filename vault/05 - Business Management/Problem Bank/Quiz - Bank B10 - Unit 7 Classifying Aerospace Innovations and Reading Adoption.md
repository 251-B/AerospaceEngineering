## B10.1 A turbofan that burns less fuel

- id: B10-Q1
- concept_id: u7-product-vs-process
- format: identify
- asks: classification

An engine maker launches a new turbofan for regional jets that burns 15% less fuel than its previous model and sells it to airlines. On the product vs process pair, this is ...

a) a process innovation, because it lowers the operating costs of the airlines that use it
b) a product innovation, because the novelty is embodied in the output the firm sells
c) a process innovation, because any gain in efficiency is a process innovation
d) neither, because a 15% gain is too small to count as an innovation

### Answer

b

### Explanation

Product innovations are embodied in the outputs of an organization, its goods or services, like the hybrid car of Slide 11; the new turbofan is the engine maker's output.

- a) The airlines' lower costs are a benefit of using the new product; a process innovation changes the way the innovating firm itself produces or markets, which is not the case here.
- c) Process innovations often aim at efficiency of production, but a more efficient product sold to customers is still a product innovation; the pair depends on where the novelty lies.
- d) The size of the change decides between radical and incremental, a different pair; it does not stop the turbofan from being an innovation.

## B10.2 Better solar cells on the same bus

- id: B10-Q2
- concept_id: u7-architectural-vs-component
- format: identify
- asks: application

A satellite manufacturer replaces the solar cells of its standard platform with cells of higher efficiency. The panels keep their size, mounting and electrical interface, and no other subsystem of the satellite changes. How does the course classify this change on the architectural vs component pair:

a) An architectural innovation, because a satellite is a system of subsystems
b) An architectural innovation, because more power changes the satellite's performance
c) Neither, because solar cells are bought from a supplier
d) A component innovation, because one component changes without significantly affecting the overall configuration

### Answer

d

### Explanation

Slide 13: a component (modular) innovation changes one or more components without significantly affecting the overall configuration of the system, which is what happens when only the cells change and every interface stays.

- a) Every satellite is a system; an architectural innovation needs a change in the overall design or in the way the components interact, and none happens here.
- b) Better performance does not make an innovation architectural; the classification depends on the configuration of the system, not on how much it improves.
- c) The classification is about what changes in the system, not about who makes the component; a supplied component can be the object of a component innovation.

## B10.3 Electric motors for light aircraft

- id: B10-Q3
- concept_id: u7-competence-perspective
- format: identify
- asks: application

Electric propulsion for light training aircraft spreads. Firm E has built electric motors and battery packs for drones for twenty years; firm P has only ever built piston engines. On the competence pair, how should this innovation be classified:

a) Competence-enhancing for E and competence-destroying for P
b) Competence-destroying for both, because it is new to the light-aircraft market
c) Competence-enhancing for both, because both firms already build aircraft engines
d) It cannot be classified until the industry selects a dominant design

### Answer

a

### Explanation

Slide 12: the classification depends on whose perspective is taken. The innovation builds on E's existing knowledge base (competence-enhancing for E) and does not build on P's competencies, which it may render obsolete (competence-destroying for P).

- b) Novelty in a market is not the criterion; for E the innovation builds on existing knowledge, so it is not competence-destroying for every firm.
- c) P's knowledge is about piston engines, which electric propulsion does not build on; making propulsion units is not the same knowledge base.
- d) The competence pair is judged from each firm's knowledge base; it does not depend on the later selection of a dominant design (Slide 17).

## B10.4 Small gains from the first efforts

- id: B10-Q4
- concept_id: u7-s-curve-shape
- format: what-happens
- asks: effect

A start-up has just begun developing a new battery chemistry for electric aircraft, and its first efforts bring only small gains in energy density. If it keeps investing effort, what does the course's S-curve of technology performance lead us to expect:

a) The gains will stay small for ever, because a slow start shows that the technology has no future
b) The gains will be the same for every additional unit of effort
c) The gains will speed up for a while and then slow down as the technology approaches its limit
d) The gains will be largest at the end, when the technology reaches its limit

### Answer

c

### Explanation

Plotted against effort, performance improves slowly at first, then quickly, and finally flattens as it approaches the limit of the technology (Slide 14); the start-up is in the first, flat part of the curve.

- a) A slow start is the normal first part of the S-curve, not a sign that the curve will never rise.
- b) Equal gains for every unit of effort would be a straight line, not the S-shape of the course.
- d) Near the limit the curve is flat, so the gains there are the smallest, not the largest.

## B10.5 Which one is discontinuous

- id: B10-Q5
- concept_id: u7-discontinuous-technology
- format: identify
- asks: application

Which of the following is a discontinuous technology in the sense of the course:

a) A chemical thruster with a 5% higher specific impulse, developed from the firm's previous model
b) Electric (plasma) thrusters that keep a satellite in its orbit, a need until now met by chemical thrusters, built on an entirely different body of knowledge
c) A new satellite service for a market need that no earlier technology served
d) A chemical thruster that a regulator imposes as the industry standard

### Answer

b

### Explanation

A discontinuous technology fulfils a similar market need by building on an entirely new knowledge base (Slide 15); station-keeping is the same need, and plasma propulsion rests on knowledge different from combustion.

- a) An improvement developed from the previous model builds on the same knowledge base; it moves along the existing S-curve instead of starting a new one.
- c) The definition requires a similar market need to an existing technology; a need that nothing served before does not fit it.
- d) Imposing a design as a standard says nothing about its knowledge base; besides, a dominant design is a de facto standard that need not be officially enforced (Slide 17).

## B10.6 Cumulative adopters after the early majority

- id: B10-Q6
- concept_id: u7-adopter-cumulative-shares
- format: numeric
- asks: calculation
- calc: 2.5 + 13.5 + 34

On the course's diffusion curve, what cumulative share of the population has adopted a technology once the whole early majority has adopted it:

a) 50%
b) 34%
c) 84%
d) 16%

### Answer

a

### Explanation

The cumulative share adds the innovators (2.5%), the early adopters (13.5%) and the early majority (34%): $2.5 + 13.5 + 34 = 50$, so 50% (Slide 16, panel (a)).

- b) 34% is the share of the early majority alone, read from the bell curve, not the cumulative share.
- c) 84% is the cumulative share once the late majority has also adopted; it adds another 34%.
- d) 16% is the cumulative share at the end of the early adopters (2.5% + 13.5%), before any of the early majority.

## B10.7 Early adopters among flight schools

- id: B10-Q7
- concept_id: u7-adopter-categories
- format: numeric
- asks: calculation
- calc: 0.135 * 600

A new electric trainer aircraft is expected to be adopted, in time, by all 600 flight schools of a region, following the adopter categories of the course. How many of these flight schools are early adopters:

a) 96 flight schools
b) 15 flight schools
c) 81 flight schools
d) 204 flight schools

### Answer

c

### Explanation

Early adopters are 13.5% of the adopters (Slide 16): $0.135 \times 600 = 81$ flight schools.

- a) 96 is 16% of 600, the cumulative share of innovators and early adopters together (or the share of the laggards), not the early adopters alone.
- b) 15 is 2.5% of 600, the innovators, who adopt before the early adopters.
- d) 204 is 34% of 600, the size of the early majority or of the late majority.

## B10.8 Which change is radical

- id: B10-Q8
- concept_id: u7-radical-vs-incremental
- format: identify
- asks: application

An airliner manufacturer considers four projects. Which one is a radical innovation in the sense of the course:

a) Retrofitting winglets to the wings of its current airliner
b) A software update that raises the take-off thrust of its current engines by 3%
c) A cabin with slightly wider seats in the same fuselage
d) A blended-wing-body airliner that merges wing and fuselage into a single lifting body, unlike any earlier tube-and-wing design

### Answer

d

### Explanation

A radical innovation is very new and different from prior solutions (Slide 12); a blended wing body departs from the tube-and-wing solution of every earlier airliner.

- a) Winglets are a relatively minor change to the existing wing, an incremental innovation.
- b) A 3% thrust gain from a software update is a minor adjustment to existing practice, so it is incremental.
- c) Slightly wider seats in the same fuselage are a small adjustment to the existing cabin, an incremental change.

## B10.9 More users, more apps

- id: B10-Q9
- concept_id: u7-network-externalities
- format: complete-sentence
- asks: classification

A flight-planning platform becomes more valuable to each airline as more airlines use it, because route data and procedures are shared among its users. In the course's terms, the number of airlines using the platform is its ...

a) installed base
b) complementary goods
c) absorptive capacity
d) technological spillover

### Answer

a

### Explanation

The installed base is the number of users of a particular good (Slide 19); through network externalities, the value of the platform to each user grows with that number.

- b) Complementary goods are additional goods and services that enhance the value of a good, such as applications for the platform, not the count of its users.
- c) Absorptive capacity is an organization's ability to recognize, assimilate and use new knowledge (Slide 18), a capability of one firm.
- d) A technological spillover is the spread of R&D knowledge across organizational or regional boundaries (Slide 9), not a number of users.

## B10.10 After the design is chosen

- id: B10-Q10
- concept_id: u7-dominant-design-consequence
- format: what-happens
- asks: effect

Once an industry has selected a dominant design, what does the course say producers and customers do:

a) They keep developing and comparing several alternative designs with the same effort as before
b) They wait until a regulator officially enforces the design before investing in it
c) They focus on improving their efficiency in manufacturing, delivering, marketing or deploying that design
d) They abandon the design as soon as it reaches 50% of the market

### Answer

c

### Explanation

Slide 17: once the dominant design is selected, producers and customers focus their efforts on improving their efficiency in manufacturing, delivering, marketing or deploying it, rather than continuing to develop and consider alternative designs.

- a) This is the opposite of what the course says: after the selection the effort shifts away from alternative designs.
- b) A dominant design is a de facto standard: it may not be officially enforced or acknowledged, so nobody waits for a regulator.
- d) Holding usually 50% or more of the market is what makes a design dominant, not a reason to abandon it.
