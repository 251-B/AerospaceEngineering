# Bank M13 — Orbis Aerostructures launches a quality and training programme

> [!info] Practice item, not an official exercise
> Case written for the bank in the style of Exercise 3 of the examples PDF, centred on the four functions, the types and levels of decisions, human resources management and controlling. The firm is fictitious. Content: Topic 6, Slides 3-5, 7-8, 10-11, 14-16, 18 and 22-25. KPIs are not defined in the Topic 6 deck: they are taken from Examples PDF, Exercise 3, and treated as an **extension**. The trap of part (e): unlike a flexible working method, a programme of written, standard procedures is easiest in the machine bureaucracy.

## Statement

Orbis Aerostructures is a plant of 2,400 employees that machines and assembles fuselage panels and wing ribs for two aircraft manufacturers. Its operators do short, repetitive tasks on each workstation, every task follows a written work instruction, decisions are taken at the top and pass down through seven hierarchical levels, and a methods office designs the instructions. Last year one panel in every twenty-five was rejected at the customer's incoming inspection, mostly for drilling and sealing defects, and one manufacturer has warned that it will move part of its orders if the figure does not halve within a year.

The board of directors has approved a **quality-control and training programme** for the whole plant. Other plants of the same group solved a similar problem with programmes of this kind, but Orbis must adapt them to its own parts and machines. The programme has these actions:

1. Set the target "rejections below one panel in fifty within twelve months" and the calendar of the programme.
2. Create a quality cell in each production line, with a quality inspector who reports to the head of quality.
3. Hire twelve new quality inspectors, since nobody in the plant holds the inspection certificate.
4. Train the 900 operators of drilling and sealing in the new inspection checklists.
5. Explain to the operators in meetings on every shift why the programme matters for the plant's orders.
6. Pay a quarterly bonus to the lines that meet the target, and assess each operator's work every six months.
7. Measure every week the rejections at the customer and the defects found inside the plant, and correct the lines that fall behind.
8. Buy two new automatic drilling machines.

In each shift, the shift supervisor decides every morning which operators attend that day's training session, following the rota set by the training office.

Tasks:

a) Classify the actions 1 to 8 into the four managerial functions, and say which action is not a managerial function. (0.5 points)

b) Classify by type of decision and by management level: the board's decision to launch the programme, and the supervisor's daily choice of who attends the session. Say which managerial skill weighs most in each case. (0.5 points)

c) Using the activities of human resources management, explain how Orbis should recruit, select, train, appraise and compensate its people in the programme, and say which actions belong to directing and which to staffing. (0.5 points)

d) Define three KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) to control the programme and classify the control of action 7 by when it takes place and by its frequency. (0.5 points)

e) State in which basic organizational configuration this programme is easiest to introduce and in which it is hardest, justifying each answer with the features of the configuration. (0.5 points)

## (a) The actions and the four managerial functions [0.5 pts]

### Hint

The four functions are planning, organizing, leading and controlling (Slides 3-4). In the course, leading is human resources management: directing and staffing (Slide 23). Ask of each action whether it sets goals, arranges people, manages people or compares results with the plan.

### Checkpoint

- Planning: action 1.
- Organizing: action 2.
- Leading (human resources management): actions 3, 4, 5 and 6.
- Controlling: action 7.
- Action 8 (buying machines) is an investment and an operation of the production function, not a managerial function.

### Solution

#### Planning: action 1

Planning is setting goals and deciding on the strategy to achieve them, and a goal is a specific achievement to be attained at a future date (Topic 6, Slide 10). Action 1 sets exactly such a goal, "below one panel in fifty within twelve months", and its calendar.

#### Organizing: action 2

Organizing arranges the human resources so that their activities contribute to the firm's goals, by dividing and coordinating the work (Topic 6, Slide 11). Action 2 creates a new unit, the quality cell, and fixes who reports to whom: it changes the organization chart.

#### Leading: actions 3, 4, 5 and 6

Leading is human resources management (Topic 6, Slides 2 and 23). Hiring inspectors (3), training operators (4), explaining the programme on every shift (5), and the bonus and the six-monthly assessment (6) all manage people; part (c) sorts them into directing and staffing.

#### Controlling: action 7

Controlling ensures that activities provide the planned results: standards, measurement of actual performance, deviations and corrective action (Topic 6, Slide 24). Action 7 measures rejections and defects every week against the target of action 1 and corrects the lines that fall behind: planning and controlling are inseparable (Topic 6, Slide 24).

#### What does not belong: action 8

Buying two drilling machines is an investment decision and part of the production process, not one of the four managerial functions. Like "communication is not a product attribute" in Exercise 2 of the examples PDF, it is the item that does not belong to the list of the course.

## (b) Type of decision, management level and skill [0.5 pts]

### Hint

Slide 5 places a decision between routine and innovative by its problem and its alternatives; Slide 7 gives the levels (top: strategic, general; lower: tactical, functional); Slide 8 the skill that weighs most at each level.

### Checkpoint

- Board decision: adaptable decision (known problem of rejections, alternatives known from the group's other plants but to be adapted); top management, strategic and general; conceptual skill.
- Supervisor's choice: routine (planned) decision (known, well-defined problem, known alternatives, a rota); low management (first-line supervisor), operational; technical skill.
- Interpersonal skill weighs at every level.

### Solution

#### The board's decision

The problem, rejections of drilled and sealed panels, is known and measured, and "other plants of the same group solved a similar problem", so the alternatives are partly known; but Orbis "must adapt them to its own parts and machines". It is neither a routine decision (known problem, known alternatives) nor an innovative one (unusual problem, ambiguous and not planned alternatives): it is an **adaptable decision**, between the two (Topic 6, Slide 5). It affects the whole plant and its relation with the customers, so it is a **strategic, general** decision of **top management** (Topic 6, Slide 7), where the skill that weighs most is the **conceptual skill**, seeing the firm as a whole (Topic 6, Slide 8).

#### The supervisor's daily choice

Choosing which operators attend today's session is a problem that is known and well defined, with known alternatives fixed by the rota of the training office: a **routine (planned) decision** (Topic 6, Slide 5). It is taken by a first-line supervisor, **low management**, and concerns one shift on one day, an operational decision of the lower levels of the pyramid, which the course calls tactical and functional by scope (Topic 6, Slide 7). The skill that weighs most there is the **technical skill**, knowing how the workstations operate and who can leave them (Topic 6, Slide 8). The interpersonal skill weighs at every level, for the board and for the supervisor alike.

## (c) Human resources management in the programme [0.5 pts]

### Hint

Slide 23: human resources management is the process of recruiting, selecting, training, appraising and compensating employees; it involves directing (motivation, leadership and communication) and staffing (recruiting, selecting and training; performance appraisal, compensation and incentive programmes). Recruitment and selection are two different activities.

### Checkpoint

- Recruiting: attract candidates holding the inspection certificate (action 3).
- Selecting: choose the twelve inspectors among them.
- Training: action 4, the 900 operators.
- Appraising: the six-monthly assessment (action 6).
- Compensating: the quarterly bonus as an incentive programme (action 6).
- Directing: action 5 (communication, motivation); staffing: actions 3, 4 and 6.

### Solution

#### The five activities

Human resources management is the process of recruiting, selecting, training, appraising and compensating employees, and its problem is to align individual interests with the objectives of the company (Topic 6, Slide 23). In the programme:

- **Recruiting:** attract candidates for the twelve inspector posts, since "nobody in the plant holds the inspection certificate"; for example, a job offer to people who hold it.
- **Selecting:** choose the twelve best among the candidates attracted, for example with a test of measurement and inspection of a panel. Recruitment builds the pool, selection chooses from it; confusing them is the usual error.
- **Training:** action 4, the 900 operators of drilling and sealing learn the new checklists.
- **Appraising:** the six-monthly assessment of each operator's work (action 6), a performance appraisal system.
- **Compensating:** the quarterly bonus to the lines that meet the target (action 6), an incentive programme that ties the operators' interest to the objective of action 1.

#### Directing and staffing

**Directing** is motivation, leadership and communication: action 5, the shift meetings that explain why the programme matters. **Staffing** is recruiting, selecting and training, plus appraisal, compensation and incentives: actions 3, 4 and 6 (Topic 6, Slide 23).

## (d) KPIs and the type of control [0.5 pts]

### Hint

A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a measurable indicator of whether an objective is being achieved (Examples PDF, Exercise 3; **extension**). Slide 25 classifies control by who, by what area, by when (reactive, proactive, interactive) and by frequency (constant, periodic, occasional).

### Checkpoint

- Three measurable KPIs linked to the target: rejection rate at the customer, internal defect rate per line, share of operators trained (or inspections passed first time).
- Action 7 is periodic (weekly) control.
- By when: it reacts to defects already produced (reactive), with the in-plant measurement allowing correction before the panels ship (interactive part); reasoned answers accepted.

### Solution

#### Three KPIs

A KPI is a quantified measure of performance against an objective (Examples PDF, Exercise 3; **extension**). It is the measure of actual performance that controlling compares with the standard (Topic 6, Slide 24).

1. **Customer rejection rate:** panels rejected at the customer's incoming inspection divided by panels delivered, per month; the standard is the target of action 1, below one in fifty (2%), from one in twenty-five (4%) today.
2. **Internal defect rate per line:** drilling and sealing defects found by the quality cell per hundred panels, per week and per line, which shows the lines that fall behind.
3. **Training coverage:** operators of drilling and sealing who have completed the checklist training divided by the 900 planned, per month.

Other valid KPIs: share of panels passing inspection first time, hours of rework per panel, number of lines receiving the bonus.

#### Type of control of action 7

By **frequency**, action 7 is **periodic** control: it takes place every week, not constantly or only occasionally (Topic 6, Slide 25). By **when**, measuring rejections at the customer is **reactive**: it acts after the defect has happened. The weekly in-plant count of defects lets the quality cells correct a line before more panels are shipped, which brings it close to **interactive** control. **Proactive** control would act before the defects appear, as the training of action 4 does. By area, it controls each production line, a unit of the plant (Topic 6, Slide 25).

## (e) Easiest and hardest configuration [0.5 pts]

### Hint

This programme is not a flexible way of working: it is a set of written, standard inspection procedures designed by analysts and imposed on the whole plant. Ask which configuration coordinates by standardization and has a technostructure, and which one has neither.

### Checkpoint

- Easiest: machine bureaucracy (standardization and very formal procedures in the operating core, routine specialized tasks, centralized decisions, elaborate administrative structure); Orbis already is one, and its methods office is the technostructure that writes the checklists.
- Hardest: adhocracy (low standardization and formalization, technostructure disappears, decentralization); also accepted: professional bureaucracy, where qualified professionals standardize their skills and decide on their own work, or the simple structure, which has no technostructure.
- The trap: the opposite of the answer for an agile or flexible methodology (Examples PDF, Exercise 3).

### Solution

#### Easiest: machine bureaucracy

The **machine bureaucracy** has many rules and very formal procedures in the operating core, highly specialized routine tasks, functional departmentalization, centralized decision-making and an elaborate administrative structure with many layers (Topic 6, Slide 16). Orbis already shows these features: "short, repetitive tasks", "a written work instruction" for every task, decisions "taken at the top" through "seven hierarchical levels", and a methods office that "designs the instructions", which is its **technostructure**, the part that designs systems and processes (Topic 6, Slide 14). A programme of new written checklists, decided by the board and applied to every line, uses the configuration's own way of coordinating: standardization. It is therefore easiest there.

#### Hardest: adhocracy

The **adhocracy** has low standardization and formalization, decentralization as the key to flexibility, and in it the technostructure disappears (Topic 6, Slide 22). Nobody would write the checklists and nobody would impose them across teams that organize their own work, so a plant-wide programme of standard procedures is hardest there.

#### Alternative answers

The **professional bureaucracy** is also a reasoned answer for "hardest": highly qualified professionals with standardization of their skills and knowledge, and decentralized decisions (Topic 6, Slide 18), resist a checklist written by analysts for their work. The **simple structure** has only a strategic apex and an operating core, so it has no technostructure to design the programme (Topic 6, Slide 15). The trap is to answer as in Exercise 3 of the examples PDF, where an agile methodology was hardest in the machine bureaucracy and easiest in the adhocracy: what is easy or hard depends on what is introduced, flexibility or standardization.

## Interpretation

The case covers the four functions as one cycle (Slides 3-4): the target of planning (action 1) is the standard of controlling (action 7), and organizing and human resources management (Slides 11 and 23) carry it out. The two decisions of part (b) show that the type of decision and the level of the manager change together (Slides 5, 7 and 8). The configuration of part (e) is the opposite of the one that suits a flexible methodology, because a quality programme of written procedures relies on the standardization that defines the machine bureaucracy (Slide 16).
