# Bank M05 — Celeris reusable launcher against an expendable incumbent

> [!info] Practice item, not an official exercise
> Case written for the bank in the style of Exercises 2 and 3 of the examples PDF, which has no Topic 7 exercise. Celeris Launch, Vektor Space and Carbotec are fictitious companies. Concepts and definitions: Topic 7, Slides 3-19.

## Statement

Celeris Launch is a Spanish start-up founded in 2021 by four engineers who had worked for six years on the guidance of vertically landing vehicles in a university research group. The university's technology transfer office helped them license the guidance software and set up the company, and a private non-profit foundation, which does no research of its own, funded the last two years of the group's landing experiments. Celeris has its plant in an aerospace park where some twenty firms (structures, avionics, test services) share suppliers and customers; several of its engineers came from those neighbouring firms, bringing with them what they had learnt there. The national space agency granted Celeris access to the engine test stand of a government laboratory.

Celeris's small launcher has a reusable first stage: after separation it restarts its engines, deploys grid fins and landing legs, and lands vertically on a barge, so the stage, its engines and its guidance work together in a way no expendable launcher needs. The firm also 3D-prints the injector of its engine as a single piece instead of machining and brazing forty parts; the injector keeps the same geometry and performance, its production time falls from six weeks to five days, and the printers were already used in the plant for brackets. Finally, Carbotec, a composites supplier of the park, co-developed with Celeris a carbon-fibre interstage that replaces the aluminium one with the same shape and the same interfaces, and is 30% lighter.

The incumbent, Vektor Space, has built expendable launchers in series for thirty years; its strength is producing single-use stages cheaply, and it has no experience in landing guidance or in refurbishing a stage between flights. Its last three upgrades each cost more than the previous one and raised the payload delivered to orbit per million euros by less than 2%. Celeris's first flights delivered less payload per million euros than Vektor's launcher, but the figure has improved quickly with each landing and reuse. Celeris's chief executive says that "our design will become the standard of the industry as soon as the space agency declares it the official one".

## (a) Classify the three innovations of Celeris (reusable first stage, printed injector, composite interstage) along the four pairs of innovation types, with the reason for each [1.0 pts]

### Hint

Take one innovation at a time and ask four questions: is it in the output or in the way of producing? How far is it from prior solutions? Whose knowledge does it use or make obsolete (name the firm)? Does it change the configuration of the system or only one part?

### Checkpoint

- Reusable first stage: product, radical, architectural; competence-destroying for Vektor Space, competence-enhancing for Celeris.
- Printed injector: process, incremental, component; competence-enhancing for Celeris (printers already in use).
- Composite interstage: product, incremental, component; competence-enhancing for Carbotec.
- The competence pair is always stated from the perspective of a named firm.

### Solution

#### Reusable first stage

- **Product innovation:** it is embodied in Celeris's output, the launcher and the launch service it sells ("Celeris's small launcher has a reusable first stage") [Topic 7, Slide 11].
- **Radical:** a stage that "restarts its engines, deploys grid fins and landing legs, and lands vertically on a barge" is very new and different from prior solutions, the expendable stages Vektor has built for thirty years [Topic 7, Slide 12].
- **Architectural:** the case says that "the stage, its engines and its guidance work together in a way no expendable launcher needs": the overall design of the vehicle and the way its components interact change [Topic 7, Slide 13].
- **Competence-destroying for Vektor Space,** whose strength is "producing single-use stages cheaply" and which has "no experience in landing guidance or in refurbishing a stage"; **competence-enhancing for Celeris,** whose founders "had worked for six years on the guidance of vertically landing vehicles". The same innovation enhances one firm's competences and destroys another's, as the course stresses [Topic 7, Slide 12].

#### Printed injector

- **Process innovation:** "the injector keeps the same geometry and performance"; what changes is the technique of producing it, with a gain in efficiency ("production time falls from six weeks to five days") [Topic 7, Slide 11].
- **Incremental:** the engine and the injector are unchanged and the printers "were already used in the plant for brackets", so it is a relatively minor change to the firm's existing practices [Topic 7, Slide 12].
- **Component:** only one component is affected and the configuration of the engine and of the launcher does not change [Topic 7, Slide 13].
- **Competence-enhancing for Celeris,** because it builds on the printing knowledge it already had. (It would be competence-destroying for a workshop whose skill is machining and brazing the forty parts; the case does not name one, so this remains a remark.)

#### Composite interstage

- **Product innovation:** the interstage is part of the launcher Celeris sells [Topic 7, Slide 11].
- **Incremental:** it replaces the aluminium interstage "with the same shape and the same interfaces" and makes it lighter, a relatively minor change to an existing solution [Topic 7, Slide 12].
- **Component:** one component changes without significantly affecting the overall configuration of the system, since the shape and interfaces are kept [Topic 7, Slide 13].
- **Competence-enhancing for Carbotec,** a composites supplier whose existing knowledge base it builds on [Topic 7, Slide 12].

## (b) Which sources of innovation and which features of collaborative networks appear in the case? [0.5 pts]

### Hint

Go through the players of Slides 3 and 8 (individuals, firms, universities, government-funded research, private non-profits) and then through the two features of networks of Slide 9.

### Checkpoint

- University research and its technology transfer office.
- Government-funded research: the agency and the test stand of a government laboratory.
- A private non-profit that funds the R&D of others without doing its own.
- Firms: Celeris's own R&D and suppliers' technology (Carbotec), a source of development work.
- A technology cluster (the aerospace park) and technological spillovers (engineers moving between firms).
- The linkages among all of them, the most important source of innovation.

### Solution

#### Sources of innovation

- **Universities:** the founders come from "a university research group", and "the university's technology transfer office helped them license the guidance software", the role the course gives to these offices: to increase the degree to which university research leads to commercial innovation [Topic 7, Slide 8].
- **Private non-profit organizations:** the foundation "which does no research of its own, funded the last two years of the group's landing experiments", one of the nonprofits that fund the R&D of other organizations without performing it [Topic 7, Slide 8].
- **Government-funded research:** the national space agency gives access to "the engine test stand of a government laboratory" [Topic 7, Slide 8].
- **Firms:** Celeris's own development work, and "Carbotec, a composites supplier ... co-developed" the interstage: suppliers' technology is the third source of development work in the ranking of Slide 6 [Topic 7, Slides 3 and 6].

#### Collaborative networks

- **Technology cluster:** the aerospace park where "some twenty firms ... share suppliers and customers" is a regional cluster of firms connected to a common technology, with buyer, supplier and complementary relationships, helped by geographical proximity [Topic 7, Slide 9].
- **Technological spillovers:** engineers who "came from those neighbouring firms, bringing with them what they had learnt there" spread knowledge across organizational boundaries, a positive externality of the R&D of those firms [Topic 7, Slide 9].
- Together, university, nonprofit, agency, laboratory and supplier form the network of linkages that the course calls the most important source of innovation [Topic 7, Slide 4].

## (c) Where does Vektor's expendable technology sit on its S-curve, and what does Celeris's reusable technology mean for Vektor as a discontinuous technology? [0.5 pts]

### Hint

Read the effect of Vektor's last three upgrades on the S-curve of Slide 14, then compare Celeris's first flights and later flights with the two curves of Slide 15.

### Checkpoint

- Vektor: flat upper part of its S-curve, near the limit of the technology (rising cost of each upgrade, gains below 2%).
- Celeris: a discontinuous technology (same market need, entirely new knowledge base) on a second S-curve that starts below and improves quickly.
- Risk for Vektor: the second curve can cross the first; its competences do not carry over.

### Solution

#### Vektor's position on its S-curve

Each of Vektor's last three upgrades "cost more than the previous one and raised the payload delivered to orbit per million euros by less than 2%". More effort brings smaller and smaller gains in performance: the expendable technology is in the flat upper part of its S-curve, approaching the limit of the technology [Topic 7, Slide 14].

#### Celeris's technology as a discontinuous technology

Reusable launch fulfils the same market need (putting payloads in orbit) by building on an entirely new knowledge base (landing guidance, engine restart, refurbishment between flights), which makes it a discontinuous technology [Topic 7, Slide 15]. Its curve behaves like the second technology of Slide 15 (b): "Celeris's first flights delivered less payload per million euros than Vektor's launcher", so it starts below, "but the figure has improved quickly with each landing and reuse", so it is in the steep part of its own S-curve.

#### What it means for the incumbent

If the trend continues, the second curve crosses Vektor's curve, and further effort on the expendable technology yields little because it is near its limit. Since the new technology is competence-destroying for Vektor (part a), its thirty years of skill in series production of single-use stages do not carry over: to compete it must acquire a new knowledge base rather than keep investing in the old curve.

## (d) Which factors of the course could make a reusable design dominant, and what is wrong with the chief executive's statement? [0.5 pts]

### Hint

Slides 17-19: what a dominant design is (share, kind of standard) and the two forces that select it.

### Checkpoint

- Dominant design: a single architecture with usually 50% or more of the market, a de facto standard.
- Learning effects: each landing and reuse improves the design; revenue reinvested; absorptive capacity.
- Network externalities: installed base and complementary goods (extension of the console example to launchers, stated as such).
- Winner-take-all tendency, with alternatives surviving in niches.
- The chief executive confuses a de facto standard with an officially declared one.

### Solution

#### Learning effects

The figure of Celeris "has improved quickly with each landing and reuse": the more the technology is used, the more it is developed and the more efficient it becomes; the sales of each flight generate revenue to reinvest in refining it, and the firm's experience lets it use the technology more productively. Its absorptive capacity, the ability to recognize, assimilate and use new knowledge (here the university guidance research and the knowledge of engineers from the park), speeds this up [Topic 7, Slide 18].

#### Network externalities

The course defines network externalities as an increase in the value of a good to a user with the number of users, through the installed base and complementary goods, with the example of a video game console [Topic 7, Slide 19]. Applied to launchers (an application of the case, **extension** of the course example): the installed base would be the number of customers and satellites already flying on the reusable design, and the complementary goods the payload adapters, integration services and ground support built for it; the more of them there are, the more valuable the design is to the next customer.

#### Dominant design and the chief executive's statement

If these forces work, the reusable architecture could reach usually 50% or more of the market and become the dominant design, pushing the market towards winner-take-all, with alternative designs (expendable launchers) surviving in niches [Topic 7, Slides 17 and 19]. The chief executive's claim does not belong to the course's explanation: a dominant design is a **de facto standard**, a standard of the industry even if it is not officially enforced or acknowledged; it is selected by the market through learning effects and network externalities, not by an official declaration of the space agency [Topic 7, Slide 17].

## Interpretation

The case shows that the pairs of innovation types are not exclusive alternatives for one innovation but four independent questions, and that the competence pair has no answer until the firm is named. The reusable stage is at once product, radical, architectural and competence-destroying for the incumbent, which is why it threatens Vektor far more than the lighter interstage or the printed injector, both incremental component changes.
