# Bank C12 — Island regional airlines under Porter's five forces

> [!info] Practice item
> Written for the problem bank. It is not an official exercise and has no official key.

## Statement

Three airlines fly between the islands of an archipelago with similar turboprops and similar fares; none is clearly larger. Two manufacturers in the world build turboprops of that size, and a single company supplies the jet fuel at the airports. Most passengers are price-sensitive residents who compare fares online, plus the regional government, which buys tickets for public employees. A fast-ferry company has just started services between the two main islands. To start a new airline one needs aircraft, an operating certificate and slots at airports that are almost full.

## (a) Analyse the five competitive forces of this industry, rating each one as high, medium or low with a reason [1.5 pts]

### Hint

Rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of entry, threat of substitutes, each with its determinants [Slides 14-15].

### Checkpoint

- Rivalry: high (three similar firms, little differentiation, no leader).
- Suppliers: high (two aircraft makers, one fuel supplier).
- Buyers: high (price-sensitive residents; the government as a large buyer).
- New entrants: low-medium (capital, certificate, full slots: legal and access barriers).
- Substitutes: medium-high (fast ferry on the main route).

### Solution

| Force [Slides 14-15] | Rating | Determinants in the case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rivalry among existing firms** | High | Three firms of similar size (low concentration, no leader), similar product and fares (little differentiation) |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | High | Only two turboprop manufacturers and a single fuel supplier: high relative bargaining power |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | High | Residents are price-sensitive and compare online; the government buys in volume |
| **Threat of new entrants** | Low to medium | Capital requirements (aircraft), legal and regulatory barriers (operating certificate), access to "distribution" (airport slots almost full) |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Medium to high | The fast ferry: buyers' propensity to substitute depends on its relative price and travel time |

## (b) Conclude on the attractiveness of the industry and suggest one strategic response for an airline [0.5 pts]

### Hint

The stronger the forces, the lower the profitability the industry allows. Connect your answer with the sources of competitive advantage [Slide 20].

### Checkpoint

- Low attractiveness: four of five forces are strong.
- A response: cost advantage (fleet standardization, high capacity utilization) or differentiation (schedule, connections, service) that weakens buyer power; possibly cooperation or consolidation to reduce rivalry.

### Solution

With strong rivalry, strong suppliers, strong buyers and a growing substitute, the industry is **unattractive**: the profit available is squeezed from every side, and only the entry barriers protect it. An airline can respond by building a **cost advantage** (a single aircraft type [Slide 21], high capacity utilization [Slide 22]) to survive the price war, or a **differentiation advantage** (frequent schedules, connections with long-haul flights, loyalty schemes for residents) that reduces buyers' price sensitivity [Slides 23-24].
