# 🏢 Bank C13 — Can a car-seat maker enter the aircraft-seat market? Barriers to entry

> [!info] Practice item
> Written for the problem bank. It is not an official exercise and has no official key.

## Statement

ComfortAuto S.A., a large maker of car seats, wants to enter the market of passenger seats for commercial aircraft. Three established firms supply almost all airlines. Each new seat model must pass crash and flammability certification that takes two years. Airlines buy seats through long contracts linked to aircraft orders, and the established firms have decades of reputation for reliability. They produce in large volumes in plants built for this purpose, and they own patents on lightweight seat frames. ComfortAuto, however, has strong engineering, low-cost purchasing of foam and fabrics, and spare plant capacity.

## (a) Identify the barriers to entry ComfortAuto faces, using the determinants of the threat of entry [1.25 pts]

### Hint

Capital requirements, economies of scale, absolute cost advantage, product differentiation, access to distribution channels, legal and regulatory barriers [Slide 15].

### Checkpoint

- Legal and regulatory: two-year certification; patents.
- Product differentiation: reputation for reliability.
- Access to distribution channels: long contracts linked to aircraft orders.
- Economies of scale: incumbents' volumes in dedicated plants.
- Capital requirements and absolute cost advantages (patents, experience).

### Solution

| Determinant [Slide 15] | In the case |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Legal and regulatory barriers** | Two years of crash and flammability certification per model; patents on lightweight frames |
| **Product differentiation** | The incumbents' decades of reputation for reliability make airlines reluctant to switch |
| **Access to distribution channels** | Seats are sold through long contracts tied to aircraft orders, already held by the incumbents |
| **Economies of scale** | Incumbents produce large volumes in dedicated plants, so their unit cost is lower |
| **Absolute cost advantage** | Patents and accumulated experience (economies of learning) |
| **Capital requirements** | Certification programmes and adapting a plant require large investment before any sale |

These barriers make the threat of entry low and protect the profitability of the three incumbents.

## (b) Assess whether the diversification makes sense for ComfortAuto [0.75 pts]

### Hint

A diversification decision asks two questions: how attractive is the sector, and can the firm achieve a competitive advantage in it [Slide 25]?

### Checkpoint

- Attractive sector: high barriers keep profitability high for those inside.
- Competitive advantage: ComfortAuto has transferable strengths (engineering, cheap inputs, spare capacity) but lacks certification, reputation and contracts.
- Related diversification: a gradual entry (a niche, a partnership or acquiring a small certified firm) is more realistic.

### Solution

The diversification answers the two questions of [Slide 25] differently. **Attractiveness:** the same barriers that block ComfortAuto keep the industry profitable for those inside, so the sector is attractive. **Competitive advantage:** ComfortAuto brings real strengths (seat engineering, low-cost inputs, spare capacity), which makes this a **related diversification**, but it lacks certification, reputation and access to contracts. Entering head-on would be slow and risky; a more realistic path is to start in a niche (crew seats, business-jet seats), partner with an incumbent, or acquire a small certified firm.
